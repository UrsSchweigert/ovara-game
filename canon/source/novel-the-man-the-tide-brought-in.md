# The Man the Tide Brought In

*A novel of Ovara*

---

# Part One: Washed Up

## Chapter 1. Wrack

He woke on the seabed with a kelp frond across his face and no idea who he was.

That was the second thing he noticed. The first was that he was breathing. Water went in and out of him, cold and easy, and some part of him that had no words was certain this should be killing him.

He lay still and looked up. He was at the bottom of a forest. The trunks around him were as thick as a man's waist and the colour of old brass, and they rose straight up, without a branch, further than he could follow. High overhead they opened into long golden blades that leaned all one way, like grass in a wind. The light that came down through them was green at the edges and gold in the middle, and it moved slowly across the sand. And the forest was making a sound. Every stem was humming in the current, each on its own low note, and the notes ran together into a chord that had no beginning and did not seem to need an end. He felt it in his teeth.

Something bumped his ribs.

He turned his head and found a whiskered grey muzzle a hand's width from his nose. It belonged to an animal like a seal that had been built by someone fond of dogs. It had a seal's sleek body and broad flippers, but the head was blunt and square and the brown eyes were set forward. The ears were small and folded, and they swivelled toward him with an interest that was entirely canine. It wore a collar of plaited cord. It snuffled his neck, sneezed a stream of bubbles into his face, and looked up, very pleased with itself, for someone to tell about it.

A girl of about sixteen hung in the water above him. She was thin and long in the arm and leg, and her dark hair was tied back with a strip of something that shone. Her eyes were large and nearly black. She was holding a harvesting hook the way you hold something you are prepared to use.

"Bother, leave it," she said. Then, to him: "You're wearing shoes."

He looked. He was. Heavy surface boots, a torn grey jacket with a corporate crest half ripped away, and around both wrists the raw red marks of a cable tie.

"What's your name?"

He opened his mouth and found nothing behind it. Not a name, not a mother, not a street. Only a blank like a wiped slate, and under the blank, very far down, the feeling of having done something terrible.

"I don't know," he said. His voice came out strange in the water, slow and round, and he understood, distantly, that he had never spoken in water before.

The girl studied him. The animal she had called Bother swam a tight circle round his boots and came back to report to her knee.

"Then you're Wrack," she said. "It's what we call what the tide brings in. I'm Sefa. Can you swim, or do I tow you?"

He could swim, it turned out, though not as she did. She went through the water without seeming to do anything, a flick of the feet now and then, like a person strolling. He laboured after her in his boots. The hound went ahead and came back, ahead and back, to make sure that nobody was lost.

"Don't touch anything," Sefa said over her shoulder. "And don't say you're from the islands until an elder asks you. Some of them will mind."

---

The forest had been tended. He began to see it as they went. The kelp stood in rows. The sand between was raked clean, and here and there a stem had been cut and bound with cord, as a branch is pruned. Then the ground began to step upward in terraces, each one walled with fitted stone, and there were people on them.

They were long-limbed, like the girl, and dark-eyed, and they moved in the easy way she did. They wore close tunics of a fine grey-green cloth that gleamed like the inside of a shell. They were cutting fronds with hooks and laying them in floating nets. A shoal of fat silver fish, each as long as his arm, grazed along the foot of a wall under the eye of two more hounds. Somebody was singing, and the others took it up without lifting their heads. It was a slow song with a long line, and it fitted itself to the chord of the forest.

They stopped singing when they saw his boots.

"Silverbacks," Sefa said, as though nothing had happened, nodding at the fish. "We herd them. That's the nursery terrace, and those are the drying racks, and that's home."

At the top of the terraces stood a dome.

It was the size of a hill. He could see at once that it had not been built by the people on the terraces, or by anyone like them. It was made of something pale and smooth, neither glass nor stone, in great curved panes. The ribs that held them were a white metal with no rivet or seam anywhere in them. Kelp had been trained up one side of it. Shellfish had been scraped from it so often and for so long that the lower panes were worn to a dull bloom. It was very old, and very beautiful. Light shone through it from inside, but only on one side. The other half of the dome was dark, and against the dark panes somebody had hung long strings of a weed that glowed a faint yellow-green, like a candle seen through a leaf.

"What is it?" he said.

"Low Anthem. It's a hold." She saw that the word meant nothing to him, and her look sharpened. "A hold. Where people live. The Makers built them, before the Sinking. They built all of them, there's three hundred. We just keep them." She shrugged, with the pride of someone speaking of a poor relation. "We're one of the small ones. Four hundred of us. Half our lamps are out. They've been out since before my grandmother was born, so nobody minds."

"Who were the Makers?"

Sefa looked at him for some time.

"You really don't know anything," she said. "They were the people before. They could make things like that," and she pointed at the white ribs, "and like the lamps, and the air-hearts that make the dry rooms dry. Then the sea came up, three thousand years ago, and they went, and nobody knows how to make anything now. We know how to clean it and how to sing to it. If a lamp goes out it stays out." She said it as she might have said that people grow old. "Come on. In at the sea-door, and mind your head."

The sea-door was a round pool in the floor of the dome, twenty paces across, with the sea on one side of its surface and air on the other. He came up through it into warmth and lamplight and the smell of smoke and frying fish, and stood dripping on a stone floor. Then, because his body had apparently always known how, he breathed out the water and breathed in the air. It hurt a little, like a first breath on a cold morning.

A great many people were looking at him.

---

They put him in a dry room and argued about him for three days.

He was not in the room when they argued, but Sefa was, when she could get in. She brought him the news with his meals. She had appointed herself his guard. It seemed nobody had asked her to, and nobody had told her to stop. The hound came with her and lay across the doorway with his chin on Wrack's boot.

"He's a tide hound," she said, when he asked. "Every hold has them. They herd the silverbacks and they watch the terraces at night, and if a child swims off they bring it back by the collar. He's not a very good one. He's nosy. He goes through people's baskets. That's why he's called Bother." Bother thumped his tail-flipper on the floor at the sound of his name. "He found you. He's been insufferable about it."

The meals were flat cakes of pressed kelp, smoked silverback, and a bowl of small green globes that burst salt on the tongue. He ate everything. He did not know when he had last eaten, and that was the least of what he did not know.

The argument, as Sefa reported it, went like this. He breathed water, so he was a mariner; that was what the people of the holds called themselves. He wore the clothes of the islands and did not know a single song, so he was not. He was an agravar, a land-dweller, one of the forty million who lived on the rocks up above and threw their filth down. But agravars drowned. Then he was a spy. Or he was bait. The corporations that ruled the islands had thrown a man in to see who came to collect him.

"What do you think I am?" Wrack asked her.

Sefa considered it honestly. "I think you're somebody who's had something awful happen to them," she said. "Eat your sea-grapes."

It was Elder Oro who ended the argument. Wrack met him on the second evening. He was a very old man with a carved stick and a bald brown head and a gentle, slightly amused way of looking at things. He came in and sat on the bench and looked at Wrack's wrists for a while.

"I am the eldest here, so I speak last," he said. "It's a great advantage. By the time it's my turn everyone else has said all the foolish things, and I need only choose the least foolish." He nodded at the marks. "I told them the sea does not usually deliver spies with their hands tied. Does that seem fair to you?"

"I don't know what I am," Wrack said.

"No. Well. It'll come or it won't. In the meantime Ione will look at you."

Ione was Sefa's mother, and the hold's healer. She was a broad calm woman with her sleeves rolled, and her forearms were ridged from wrist to elbow with old shiny scars, as though she had once put both arms into a fire. She did not explain them. She filled a shallow bowl from the sea-door and set it on the floor between them. Then she held her hands over it without touching it, and went very still.

Wrack felt the room change. It was the way a room changes when someone starts to sing quietly in the corner.

"She's intending it," Sefa told him, low. "Watch. This is the gift."

"What gift?"

"*The* gift." She seemed at a loss as to where to begin. "The sea answers some of us. Not many, one in ten maybe. You ask it for something, and if you've asked properly, it does it. There are two ways of asking. Mother's way is to ask it to care about someone. That's intending, and the water you've asked is intended water. It mends things. Cuts, fevers, bones if you're good." She hesitated. "I can do cuts. The other way is calling, when you ask it to move. I can't do that at all. Hold still. It doesn't hurt."

Ione lifted the water out of the bowl in her two hands. It held its shape, a little, as water should not. She laid it on his head.

It was warm, and it went on being warm inside his skull. It moved gently from place to place, like someone walking through a house and trying the doors.

After a long time she took her hands away.

"There's nothing broken," she told Elder Oro. "No blow, no fever, no poison. The memory isn't damaged." She looked at Wrack with something he did not like to see in a healer's face, which was caution. "It's gone. Taken, clean, all in one piece. I've seen it once before, in a caller from Deep Chorus who reached too far in a storm. She came back not knowing her own children."

"The undertow," Elder Oro said.

"The undertow." Ione dried her hands on her tunic. She spoke to Wrack now, plainly, as healers speak to the people they have bad news for. "The sea gives when it's asked. But if you ask for more than you can carry, or if you let go of yourself altogether, it takes something back. A sense, a year of your life, a piece of your mind. It takes it whole and it keeps it. That's been done to you. I can't undo it. I don't know anyone who can." She paused. "But I'll say this to the elders. The undertow only takes from people who have the gift. So whatever else he is, he isn't an agravar. No agravar has ever been answered. And whatever he did out there, he did it with everything he had."

That decided it, more or less. A mariner the sea had marked was the sea's business. They gave him a tunic of the grey-green cloth, which was called sea-silk and weighed nothing. They gave him a hammock in the long dry hall where the unmarried men slept, a hook, and a row of terrace to work.

---

He was bad at it.

He cut the fronds too low and bled the stems. He let a whole net of silverbacks out through a gate because he could not tell which way it latched, and Bother had to round them up, with a great deal of noise and an air of personal grievance. A net-mender named Davin, a quiet, big-handed man with a slow smile, came and showed him the latch three times, and did not seem to mind showing him a fourth. "Up and across," Davin said. "Everything in this hold is up and across. The Makers were all left-handed, my wife says." His wife was called Hesper. Wrack had seen her only at a distance, a lean hard woman whom people made room for. She was the hold's Tidecaller, Davin said, with a pride he did not try to hide. Others in the hold could nudge a current. She could ask the sea to move and have it do so.

He learned the shape of the days. They were short, and the hold kept them by song. A singer stood at the sea-door and sang the turn of each watch, and the terraces picked it up and passed it out into the forest, so that the hour travelled outward through the kelp like a ripple. Work was done in the water. Eating, sleeping and argument were done in the dry halls, where a fire would burn and a voice did not carry to every ear in the hold. In the evenings the lamps on the lit side of the dome dimmed of their own accord, slowly, as they had every evening for three thousand years. The dark side had only its strings of glowing weed.

"Lampweed," Sefa said. "It grows up north. You hang it where the lamps have failed. The old people say you can tell how a hold is doing by counting its lampweed, and then they say we shouldn't count ours." She was showing him how to strip a frond. "Mother says in her mother's time there were six more lamps. One goes every ten years or so. They just don't come on one evening. Then everyone goes and stands under it for a bit, and somebody brings the weed."

"And nobody can mend them."

"Jef could. He'd have to come, though, and he's only one, and there are three hundred holds." She saw his face. "You don't know who Jef is either."

He heard about Jef from the night crew, lying in his hammock in the dark hall with the others talking round him. He gathered it in pieces. Jef lived at the top of the world. Jef was older than the oldest song. He had been alive at the Hold Wars, nine hundred years ago. When an air-heart failed in some great hold and the dry halls began to go stale, they sent to Jef, and if they were lucky he came, a small old man with a bag of tools. He laid his hands on the thing and it worked again. He was the only one in the world who could do it. Some of the night crew spoke of him as of a clever uncle. More of them spoke of him as a god, and one old woman made a small sign with her fingers whenever his name was said.

"He rides the Dragon," a boy said, from somewhere down the hall.

"Everybody rides the Dragon who can get on it."

"He tells it who to let in, though."

The Dragon, Wrack learned, went round the world from top to bottom and never stopped. It was a kilometre long, and it sang. It came by the nearest calling stone about once a season, if the almanac was right. Davin had seen it once, as a boy, from a long way off. He said that it had looked like a river that had got loose from its bed, and that he had dreamed about it for a year.

"And the Threaded," said the boy. "Tell him about the Threaded."

"Jef's monks," someone said. "He puts a thread in their heads and they can ride the old machines. They keep the islanders out of our water. There's one keeps these waters, a woman. She came through when I was small. Went past the terrace like a thrown knife."

"There's not enough of them," the old woman said. "There never was, and there's fewer now."

"And one went up," said the boy. "My uncle says so. One of the Threaded went up to the islands, before anybody here was born, and never came down again."

"Your uncle says a great deal," said the old woman. But she made the sign with her fingers a second time, and it was not for Jef.

They taught him the tending song, the slow one for work that is never finished. He got the words wrong and they laughed at him, not unkindly. He liked them. He liked the chord of the forest and the green-gold light, and the salt burst of the sea-grapes, and the weight of Bother's chin on his foot. He lay at night with his hands folded on his chest and tried not to feel the thing under the blank.

On the ninth day he walked through the meeting hall on his way to the terraces.

It was the heart of the dome, a round high room where the whole hold could sit. At its centre stood a Maker lamp. It was a smooth pale column from floor to roof, as thick as a kelp stem and without a mark on it. It had been dark since Sefa's grandmother was a child. People touched it as they passed, for luck, so that at shoulder height it was polished to a shine. It was dusted every morning and sung to at every festival. A wreath of fresh weed hung on it. Nobody had the least idea how it worked.

His hands reached up on their own.

He did not decide to. He was looking at the wreath, and thinking of nothing, and his fingers found a seam that no one had ever seen, and opened it. Inside was a shape he could not have named. He knew that it was wrong, as you know a word is misspelled before you can say how. It was seated badly. It was clogged, and tired. He moved three things. He closed the seam.

The lamp came on.

Light poured down the hall, white and steady. People stopped where they stood. A child shrieked. Someone dropped a basket, and the shellfish rolled across the floor in the first clear light that room had seen in three generations.

Elder Oro had been crossing the hall with his stick. He stood looking up at the lamp. Then he sat down on the floor, quite slowly, and began to cry.

"How did you do that?" Sefa whispered.

"I don't know," Wrack said. It was the truth, and it frightened him more than the breathing had.

Nobody in Low Anthem had ever seen a Maker thing repaired. Nobody in any hold had, for six hundred years, except by one pair of hands. Those hands belonged to an old man at the top of the world, and half the people in the hall believed he was a god.

Elder Oro wiped his face with the heel of his hand.

"Somebody," he said, "had better send word north."

---

## Chapter 2. The Barge

Word went north by song.

Wrack had not known it could. He stood on the crown of the dome that evening with most of the hold. A woman with a chest like a barrel and a voice to match faced the open forest and sang four long phrases, very loud and very simple. Far off through the kelp, small with distance, another voice took them up.

"That's Fallow, the next hold," Sefa said. "They'll pass it to Deep Chorus, and Deep Chorus has choirs. It'll be at Greywatch Stone by tomorrow night. Any Threaded who goes by will hear it." She listened to the phrases going away. "It says: *a lamp is lit at Low Anthem, a stranger's hands, tell Jef.* That's all you can fit in a long-call. You can't argue in one. Elder Oro says that's why they've lasted."

After that, things were different.

Nobody was unkind to him. But the night crew no longer laughed when he got the words wrong. Old people stopped him in the passages to touch his sleeve, as they had touched the lamp. A woman brought her baby to be looked at by him, he did not know what for. He found a dish of sea-grapes left beside his hammock, and then, the next night, a carved shell.

"They did the same to Jef," Elder Oro said, when Wrack took the shell to him, at a loss. The old man was sitting under the lamp. He sat there most of the day now, with his face turned up. "He complained of it for nine centuries, I'm told. I shouldn't worry. You'll let a net of fish out tomorrow and they'll remember what you are." He turned the shell over in his fingers. "Though I'd like to know what that is, before I die. Go and see the dark side with me. Humour an old man."

They went round the dark half of the dome together, the elder leaning on his arm. There were eleven dead lamps. Wrack laid his hands on each of them. Nine he could not open at all; his fingers slid over them and found nothing, and he could not have said why. Two had seams. One of those was beyond him. Something in it had not merely slipped but broken, and he stood looking at the break with a baffled sense that there was a tool for this and that it was not in his hand. The last came on.

"Two," said Elder Oro. He was not disappointed. He looked like a man who has been given a second helping. "Two in a fortnight, after sixty years of none. I shall be unbearable at the next Moot."

On most of those evenings Wrack sat with Davin at the net-racks, because Davin did not touch his sleeve. The big man mended nets with a bone needle and talked, when he talked, about the hold.

"You've come at the turn of the year," he said. "Barge season. They come more when the storms are bad up on the Girdle; I suppose they can't get rid of it down there." He pulled a knot tight with his teeth. "You'll see. It's not much to see. You hold your corner and you sing."

"What barges?"

Davin was silent a moment. "You know the islanders are up there. Forty million of them, on rocks. There's no room for anything. So what they don't want, they bring out here in the night, over deep water, and drop it on us." He said it without heat. "It's been going on two hundred years. It's worse than it was. There's stuff in it now that burns. We net it, and bag it, and the carrying crews take it out to the dead ground and leave it. Then we plant bitterwrack to drink what we missed. That's being a Tender. Most of us here are Tenders."

"What else is there to be?"

"A Breaker." He glanced toward the dome, where his wife was. "There's folk in the wild kelp south of here who say we should stop netting and start sinking. Put a few of those barges on the bottom, and see if the islanders learn. Some of them have done it." He threaded the needle again. "Hesper could sink a barge. She's never said she wants to. But I know what she thinks about when she's netting. I've been married to her nineteen years."

"And you?"

"I mend nets," Davin said, and smiled his slow smile. "Somebody's got to. It isn't the islanders' children I'm angry with, and it's their children on those boats, mostly. Boys. I've seen them at the rail."

---

The barge came eleven nights after the lamp, as barges did.

Wrack was out with the night crew when the shadow slid across the moon. It was a long hull, seventy metres up, running dark. The singing stopped. All along the terraces people looked up, and he saw their faces. What was on them was not fear. It was tiredness.

"Halcyon," Sefa said beside him. "You can tell by the keel. Nets. Come on."

The belly of the barge opened and the sludge came down.

It fell slowly, black and thick, spreading as it sank, like ink dropped in a glass. Where it touched the kelp the golden blades curled and went brown. Where it touched skin it burned. The Tenders of Low Anthem did what they had always done. They spread the long nets under it, fine-meshed, weighted at the corners, and caught what could be caught. They folded it into bladders of cured hide and sealed them with wax for the carrying crews. Behind them the old women went along the terrace edges with baskets, planting bitterwrack. It was an ugly brown weed with a leaf like a tongue. It drinks poison, and turns black as it drinks, and when it is black all through it dies. They pushed the roots in with their thumbs and sang the tending song while they worked.

Hesper was there. He saw now what Davin was proud of. She stood on the crown of the dome with her arms out and her head back. Above her the falling blackness bent. A current had come up from nowhere, steady and strong, and it was carrying the worst of the fall aside from the nursery beds, where the young fish were. She was holding a piece of the sea in her two hands and leaning it like a board. It was all one person could do. Against the size of the thing coming down, it was not much.

Wrack held his corner of the net. A gout of the stuff came past him and he saw Ione, at the next corner, take a splash of it across her forearm. She hissed between her teeth, and shook it off, and kept working. He understood then where the scars had come from. She had not put her arms in a fire once. She had put them into this, year after year, since she was Sefa's age.

Something rose in him that was not patience.

He did not know he could do it. He only looked up at the long shadow of the hull and wanted it *gone*, with his whole body, the way you want air.

And the sea heard him.

Hesper said afterwards that it was like standing next to a bell when it is struck. The water under the barge stood up. It rose in a column the full width of the ship and lifted two thousand tonnes of steel clean out of the ocean. The barge hung in the moonlight on a pillar of sea as steady as stone, streaming, with its crew screaming on the deck. And the sludge rose too. Every drop of it peeled back up out of the kelp in long black threads and poured itself into the hold it had come from.

Then the column walked. It carried the barge a full kilometre south and set it down pointing home.

Wrack did not see that part. The moment the sea answered him he lost his hold on it, and the whole terrace went with it. Water slammed sideways through the forest. Nets, hooks, harvesters and tide hounds went tumbling end over end into the kelp. Sefa struck a terrace wall and hung limp. Two of the old women were dragged a hundred metres before Hesper caught them, and she caught them only because she let everything else go.

Then it was over, and he was on his knees on the seabed, shaking so hard his teeth cracked together. He was colder than he had ever been. He was thirstier than he had ever been, here, at the bottom of the sea.

Hesper came down through the wreckage and hauled him up by his tunic.

"You nearly killed nine people," she said. Her voice shook. "You *shouted*. Do you understand? You stood in the sea and screamed an order at it, and it obeyed you, and it very nearly went through us to do it."

"I didn't mean—"

"No. That's the trouble." She let go of him. She was looking at him the way Ione had, and worse. "That was a ship. A full ship, lifted, in fouled water. No caller alive can do that. No caller in the songs."

---

He was ebbed for three days.

That was the word for it. He lay in his hammock under every blanket the hall could spare and shook. He drank until his stomach hurt and was still dry. Worst was the silence. He had not known, until it stopped, that he had been hearing the sea since the moment he woke in the kelp. It had been a presence at the edge of things, like someone else in the house. Now a door had shut. The water at the sea-door was only water.

Ione mended Sefa's cracked ribs and would not let him apologise. "She'll be swimming in two days, and she'll have learned to get behind a wall," she said. "Lie down. You look like a drowned agravar." Davin brought him broth and sat with him without saying anything, which was what he wanted.

Hesper came on the second day. She shut the door of the hall and sat down on the floor by his hammock with her back against the wall. Up close she was younger than he had thought, forty perhaps. She had a strong tired face and a caller's hands, which are chapped white to the wrist.

"I've come to teach you one thing," she said. "It'll take as long as it takes to say. Then I'm going to go on being angry with you. Understood?"

"Yes."

"You think it came out of you. It didn't. There's no power in you, or in me. It's in her." She tipped her head toward the wall, and the forest beyond it. "The sea's alive. I don't mean the fish. I mean her. She hears some of us. Nobody knows why those and not others. When I turn a current I'm not pushing water. I couldn't push a bucket of it. I'm asking. She does the pushing."

"What do you ask with?"

"Three things, the old callers say. You have to be touching her; water that's joined to the sea, not a cupful on a table. You have to want one thing and only that thing. And you have to have standing, which means you've lived in her and looked after her, and she knows you." Hesper turned her chapped hands over. "It took me eleven years of asking every day to stop a fishing boat. One boat. I'm the Mighty of this hold. There's one like me in most holds, and there's been nobody much stronger in all the songs we have." She looked at him. "And then you. Three weeks in the water, never asked for anything in your life as far as you know, and she picks up a ship for you. I don't understand it. I don't like it."

"Nor do I."

"Good. Now the one thing. Every child with the gift learns it before they learn anything else." Her voice changed, and became a teacher's. "*Ask. Do not order.* You're a small thing in a very large sea, and you say please. What you did was give an order. You were so strong she carried it out, and she carried it out the way a flood carries out an order, through whatever was standing in front of it. If you ask, she'll do it with care. If you shout, she'll just do it." She paused. "And when you shout with all of you, when you let go of yourself to do it, that's when the undertow comes. I'd say that's how you lost your name. Say it back."

"Ask. Do not order."

"Again."

He said it until she was satisfied. At the door she stopped.

"Davin likes you," she said, not turning round. "He's a better judge than I am. Don't make him wrong."

---

On the fourth day the gunboats came.

There were three of them, grey, with the Halcyon crest on their bows, and they did not come at night. An amplified voice rolled down through the water, flat and enormous. It announced that Halcyon Dredge & Mineral was responding to an unprovoked attack on civilian shipping. Then the charges started to fall.

He remembered it afterwards only in pieces. The sound, which was not a sound but a blow. The terrace he had worked folding in on itself. A crack running up the pale dome like a branch of lightning, and the sea going in. People pouring out of the sea-door, carrying children, carrying the old. Davin at the nursery gate with the silverbacks streaming past him, waving the last of the herders through. Hesper above the dome with her arms spread, turning charges aside, one, two, three of them, and missing the fourth.

He tried to ask. He stood on the broken terrace with his hands open and said *please* to the sea, over and over, like a child. He was still ebbed. It did not answer him at all.

When the gunboats left, a third of Low Anthem was flooded and dark. Twenty-one people were dead. Elder Oro was one of them. They found him in the meeting hall, under the lamp, with his stick across his knees. The lamp was still burning.

Davin was another.

They buried the dead that evening in the kelp, in the old way. Each was wrapped in sea-silk and weighted with a stone from the wall of the hold. Each was laid at the foot of a kelp stem, and the stem was given the name, cut into it at head height, so that it would rise as the kelp grew. Wrack had passed stems with names on them every day and had not known what they were. There were hundreds. The oldest names had climbed so high he would have had to swim up to read them.

The hold sang for each one. Hesper stood by Davin's stem and did not sing.

Afterwards she came and found Wrack where he sat on the broken terrace. She had a bundle on her back. Round her wrist she had tied a length of knotted cord.

"I know it wasn't you who dropped them," she said. "I want you to know that I know that." Her face was like a shut door. "I've been a Tender my whole life. I've netted their filth for twenty-five years and sung while I did it. He'd want me to go on. He'd say it's boys on those boats." Her mouth moved. "I'm finished singing."

"Where will you go?"

"The Tangle. There are people there who've been saying for years what I should have been saying." She looked at him a moment longer. "Learn to ask, Wrack. If you ever do that again without learning, I'll stop you myself, if I can."

She swam away south, and did not look back.

---

He sat until the light went. The forest still hummed. He could hear it again, faintly; the door was opening a crack. He found that this was worse.

He did not hear the vehicle arrive. Nobody did. It was simply there, hanging over the terrace, where a moment before there had been dark water. It was a sliver of pale metal, three times the length of a man and no thicker than a kelp stem, pointed at both ends. It had no rudder, no screw, and no window. It had no visible means of doing anything. A soft light ran along its flank like the light on a fish. He knew, with the part of him that had opened the lamp, that it was Maker work, and that it was alive in the way the lamp had not been until he touched it.

A woman stepped off it. She had been riding astride, lying along it. She was dark and grey-haired and spare, perhaps sixty, in a close suit the colour of slate. Thin pale scars ran from her temples down the sides of her neck and under her collar, as fine as if they had been drawn with a pen.

One of the Threaded. He remembered the night crew. *Went past the terrace like a thrown knife.*

"My name is Okonta," she said. "I've kept these waters thirty years. Four nights ago I was eighty kilometres west of here, and I felt a ship leave the sea. I've not felt that before, and I've felt most things. Then I came by Fallow and they were still singing a long-call about a lamp." She looked over the broken dome, the dark terraces, the new names cut pale in the kelp. She looked at them for some time. Then she looked at him. "I should have been closer. That's mine to carry. Are you the one?"

"Yes."

"Then you're coming with me. There's an old man who has been waiting a very long time to meet you. He doesn't know it yet."

"I can't leave. I did this. I have to—"

"You can't mend that dome," Okonta said. "Not yet. I watched you look at it. You know there's a way and you don't know what it is. He can teach you." She held out a hand. "Sit behind me. Hold on. Don't touch anything."

A small figure was standing at the edge of the terrace with a hound beside her. Sefa had a strapping of sea-silk round her ribs. She did not come closer.

"Come back," she said. It was not a request. "You owe us a dome."

"I will."

Bother swam after the needle for a little way, barking a stream of bubbles. Then the forest took them, and there was nothing behind but the humming dark.

# Part Two: The Old Man

## Chapter 3. The Dragon

The needle did not move through the water so much as decline to be where it had been.

Wrack clung to Okonta's back with his eyes shut for the first hour. There was no sense of an engine. There was no noise at all but a thin high singing from the hull, and the pressure of the sea against his shoulders. When he opened his eyes the forest was going past in a gold blur, and she was steering with nothing. There was no wheel and no lever. There was only a slight leaning of her shoulders, as a swimmer leans.

"You feel it," he said. "Like a limb."

"Like a limb. And I hear through it. The hull listens to the water for me, a long way off." She tapped the scar at her temple. "That's the Thread. It's a thing he puts in, finer than a hair, from here down the back of the neck. After that the old machines know you. There are a hundred or so of us left, and no two of us agree about anything, which is how he likes it."

"Where are we going?"

"Greywatch Stone. The Dragon's due there in six days, if the almanac's right. It's been wrong lately."

They stopped when the light failed. She moored the needle to a kelp stem with a loop of cord, as one might tie up a horse, and it hung there with its faint light pulsing slowly, like something asleep. She gave him dried fish from a pouch, and a strip of something chewy and sweet that she said was cured sea-pear. Then she lay back in the water with her hands behind her head.

The forest was different at night. The chord was still there, but above it now, far off and very high, something else was singing. It was a long falling phrase, and then a pause, and then an answer from another quarter, fainter.

"Pale singers," Okonta said. "Whales. White ones, as big as a hold's dome. They come down from the ice at this season to calve. That one's two hundred kilometres north of us, and the one answering is further." She listened with her eyes closed. "Every one of them sings its own water. The old pilots learned the songs, and so did I. If I hear that cow, I know where the Greywatch rise is from here without looking. Agravars have instruments for it. I've seen them. Dials."

"You've been on the islands?"

"I've been on their ships. Mostly while removing them." She opened one eye. "You want to know what I am. Ask. You've a week of my back to look at."

So he asked, and she told him, in a voice with no decoration in it.

She had been born in Deep Chorus, the great canyon hold, three days south. She had been a herder's daughter and then a herder. When she was twenty-six an agravar trawler had come over the canyon mouth with its gear down. It was fishing where the charts said nobody lived. It took four hundred silverbacks, and her brother, who had been among them. The elders of Deep Chorus had sung for him and held a council and done nothing, because there was nothing they could do.

"So I went to Greywatch and sat on a bench for two seasons," Okonta said. "The Dragon came by twice. The first time there was no one to carry me aboard. The second time there was a Threaded with a spare seat. I rode to the top of the world and told a little old man in an apron that I wanted to be made into a weapon." She smiled slightly at the dark. "He said he didn't make those any more. He said he could make me into a pair of ears and a fast horse, and that if I still wanted it after a year of gutting fish for the Lantern, I could have it. I gutted fish for fourteen months."

"And then?"

"And then there's a rule. It isn't long. *Keep the water. Own nothing you can't carry. Come when you're called.* You say it to him once and he never mentions it again." She shifted. "I keep the water between Rime and the Slick. I turn their survey boats round. I cut their trawl lines, and I pull people out of wrecks, theirs as well as ours. I've sunk eleven hulls in thirty years and not one with a crew aboard. He minds about that. It used to make me angry." She was quiet for a time. "I wasn't near enough to Low Anthem. One of me, and a stretch of sea you couldn't swim across in a month. There were three of us on this water when I started."

"What is he like?"

"He'll disappoint you," Okonta said. "Everybody's disappointed, the first day. Go to sleep."

---

On the second day she stiffened and brought the needle to a dead halt.

"Engines," she said. "Four. Behind us, coming on a line." She listened a moment longer, with her head tilted, to something he could not hear. "Sleds. Halcyon make. That barge crew talked, and somebody ashore has decided they'd like whatever picked up their ship. That's a recovery team. They'll have wreckers with them, to find the way."

"Wreckers?"

"Divers. Islanders, the kind with no corporation. They go down on drowned Maker ruins and tear out anything that shines, and they sell it to the shipyards. They know the bottom better than the companies do." She spat a little stream of water sideways. "Vermin, mostly. Good at their work."

"Can we outrun them?"

"I can outrun anything they've got. I can't outrun a bounty. Every wrecker between here and the Girdle will have your description by tomorrow." She turned the needle south-east. "We'll go where they won't want to follow."

The Tangle was the forest gone wild. Nobody farmed it and nobody thinned it. The kelp grew so thick that the stems rubbed together and groaned, and the light failed a few metres in. Okonta slowed to a crawl and the needle's glow went ahead of them like a held lamp. Old wrecks loomed out of the murk. They were agravar hulls from wars two hundred years gone, lying on their sides, furred with weed, with fish going in and out of the gun-ports. Once the needle's light fell on a skull in a rusted helmet, and something with many legs looked out of the eye.

Twice Wrack saw lights moving far off between the stems, yellow-green, and felt himself watched.

"Breakers," Okonta said. "They'll leave us alone. They don't quarrel with the Thread."

He wondered if Hesper was among them. He thought of her face at the kelp stem. He did not think she would be carrying a light.

On the third day the ground under them began to rise, and the kelp thinned, and they came out over something he took at first for a hill.

It was a dome. It was three times the size of Low Anthem's, and it had been broken open like an egg. Half of it stood. The pale ribs curved up into the dark with nothing between them. The floor inside was a drift of silt, and out of it stuck the stumps of walls, and a stair that went up six steps to nothing. No weed grew on any of it. Even the wild kelp had left a ring of bare sand around the place, as around a grave.

The needle had slowed without his noticing. Okonta was looking down.

"That was a hold called Sounding," she said. "Six thousand people. It's been like that nine hundred years." She put her hand flat on the hull and the needle moved on. "The last time mariners went to war with each other, that's what it looked like. There were eleven of those when it was over. It's why we've no army, and no king, and no hold that tells another what to do. We frightened ourselves." She glanced back at him over her shoulder. "He was born there. Jef. Don't bring it up unless he does."

The engines followed them in. They were clumsy in the kelp, but there were four of them and they had lamps and patience. By that evening Okonta's mouth had set in a line.

"They're herding us," she said. "Someone on that team knows this ground. There's only one way out that they haven't covered."

"Which way?"

She did not answer. An hour later he knew.

The kelp ended. It did not thin; it stopped, at a line as sharp as a shoreline, and beyond it there was nothing. There were no fish. There was no hum. He had lived inside that chord for three weeks and had stopped hearing it, and its absence now was like a hand over his ears. The stems at the edge were black and slimed. In front of them, in neat rows, stood the bitterwrack that some hold's old women had planted years ago, a wall of it a hundred metres deep. Every plant was black to the root, and dead. The water beyond was the colour of strong tea. Things hung in it, in shreds, that did not sink or rise.

"The Slick," said Okonta. "Twenty years of barges, settled in one place by the current. The carrying crews bring the bladders here too; there's nowhere else to take them. Keep your mouth shut as much as you can. We go straight through."

They were a kilometre in when the needle shuddered. Something had hold of the tail.

Wrack looked back and saw it. It was an eel as thick as his body and as long as the needle. It had no eyes. It was coated in the same black slime as the dead kelp, and where the slime had rubbed away the skin beneath was white and ulcered. A second came up out of the silt beneath them. A third.

"Slick eels," Okonta said. "They hunt by taste. Hold on."

She threw the needle sideways. The first eel lost its grip, and the second struck the hull so hard that Wrack's hands tore free. He was in the water, spinning. The taste of it was in his mouth, oily and sweet. An eel turned towards him with its jaws opening like a split hose.

He reached for the sea. *Please*, he thought, as Hesper had taught him. *Please.*

Nothing.

It was not like being ebbed. When he was ebbed, a door had been shut, and he had known there was a house behind it. Here there was no door and no house. He was asking a dead thing to speak. The water around him was only water, heavy and filthy, and he was a man in a borrowed tunic with nothing in his hands.

The eel came on. Then the needle went through it. Okonta had turned in her own length, impossibly. She took the thing behind the head with the needle's prow and carried it thirty metres. She came round again and hooked an arm through Wrack's as she passed.

"Deaf water!" she shouted. "The gift doesn't answer here. Did nobody tell you?"

"Nobody told me anything!"

"Then you've learned it cheap. Remember it. There's water that's fouled, where she hears you badly. And there's water like this, where she isn't. Where they've killed her." The needle was running flat out and the dead brown fog streamed past them. "It's the one thing the Breakers are right about. She can die. A bit at a time."

They came out the far side an hour later into clean sea. He hung onto her back and coughed black threads into the current. He found that he was weeping, though not for himself. He had not known the sea could be killed. And a thought had come to him in the middle of that silence, and would not go away. He had not been surprised. Somewhere under the blank, he had known that place was there.

---

Greywatch Stone stood on a bare rise above the forest, where the kelp would not grow and the current ran strong and cold.

It was a pillar of the pale Maker stuff, twice the height of a man, six-sided, with nothing on it at all. Round it someone had long ago set a ring of stone benches. Every surface of the benches had been scratched with names. So had the paving, and the pillar's foot as high as a hand could reach. The names were in the mariners' looping script, thousands of them, some sharp and new, most worn nearly smooth. Many had a tally of strokes beside them, to show how many days the writer had waited.

"It's a calling stone," Okonta said. "There are forty or so, strung round the world along the lines the Dragon runs. It passes each of them once a season, more or less. It steps its line a little to one side on every circuit, you see, so that it passes over every water in turn; it never runs the same line twice running." She took a little book of sea-silk leaves from her suit and turned it, frowning. "That's what the almanacs are for. Every hold keeps one, and it's handed down. This was my teacher's. It says the sixth day from when we started, at the turn of the morning watch."

"And the stone?"

"When the Dragon's within a day, it sings. You'll know."

They were not alone on the rise. In a hollow on the lee side, out of the current, a small camp had been made of nets and a cooking-stone. A woman of about thirty sat there with an old man who lay propped against a bundle, breathing as though each breath had to be lifted. They were from Fallow, the hold next to Low Anthem. She had towed her father here on a sled of kelp-floats, eleven days, because the healer at Fallow had said his lungs needed the Wells, and nothing less. They had been at the stone since before the last pass.

"It came, and there was no Threaded to carry us on," she said. She had a tired, flat, capable way of speaking. "So we stayed. He won't last another season. I thought, if one of you came by this time." She looked at the needle, and then, hopefully, at Okonta.

"I've a passenger," Okonta said. "I'll come back for you when he's aboard. It's in the rule."

There was a third traveller. He was a huge young man with a red beard, who wore a coat of sealskin with the fur inside in spite of the warmth of the water. He was sitting on a bale as big as himself, and he had not stopped watching Wrack since they arrived. His name was Brask. He was an ice-diver of Rime, the one hold at the top of the world, going home with a season's trade of kelp-cake and rope.

"You're the lamp man," he said. He had a voice like a slab of something being dropped. "It's all down the long-call. A stranger's hands." He looked Wrack over, slowly, from the feet up. "You're not much."

"No," Wrack agreed.

"Jef's not much either." Brask seemed to think this settled something. He reached into the bale and produced a slab of smoked fish the size of a roof slate, broke it in four, and handed it round.

They waited. The fourth day went by, and the fifth. Wrack read the names on the benches. He sat with the old man from Fallow while the daughter slept. The old man had no breath for talk, but he liked to have the names read to him, and now and then he would lift a finger at one he had known. The stone stayed silent. The almanac said the sixth day.

On the sixth day it was the engines that came.

Four grey sleds came up over the rise in a line. They were squat ugly machines with caged screws, and armoured divers rode them with harpoon guns levelled. The woman from Fallow threw herself across her father. Brask stood up off his bale with a seal-spear in his fist. Okonta moved in front of Wrack.

"There's nowhere to go," she said quietly. "I could carry you off. But the Dragon will pass, and those two will be left here with that lot. And there won't be another pass for a season."

The lead diver dismounted. She was an officer, by the stripe on her helmet. She raised her faceplate lamp and the beam found Wrack's face.

She stopped.

Even through the helmet glass he could see her go white. Her gun dropped a hand's width. Her mouth moved, and the suit speaker carried it clearly across the water.

"*Sir?*"

Nobody moved.

Then her face closed. She brought the gun up again. "Take him," she said. "Alive. He wants him breathing. That's the order. Leave the others."

It was then that the stone began to sing.

It was one note, so low that the sand on the paving danced to it. A moment later the sea answered from the south, on the same note. It was felt in the chest before it was heard. The divers turned. The benches trembled. Far off in the dark something pale appeared. It was a thread, then a rope, then a river, and Wrack forgot the guns.

He had imagined an animal. What came was a river with a spine. A kilometre of pale metal ribs, each one a hoop taller than the dome of Low Anthem, ran one behind another in a long sinuous line, and between them and through them and around them raced water. It was not the water of the sea. It was bound to the ribs, a brighter, denser stuff, shining faintly from inside, and it poured forward along the frame without ever leaving it, as a flame streams back from a torch. That was what gave the thing its body. That was what made it move. It came on faster than anything that size had a right to, and it sang as it came, and the whole forest below it leaned over in its wake.

It never stopped. Okonta had told him so. You matched its speed, and showed your token, and it opened to you or it did not.

It was past the first sled before the divers could scatter.

And then it slowed.

It slowed, and the note fell and fell. For the first time in the memory of anyone living, the Dragon stopped. It hung in the water above Greywatch Stone with its whole length humming. The great blunt head swung round, over the heads of the Halcyon divers, until it was facing Wrack. It had no eyes and no mouth. It was a smooth pale prow the size of a house. A panel in it, which he had somehow known was there, slid open like an eyelid.

"It has never done that," Okonta said, very quietly. "Not for me. Not for anyone. Not for Jef."

The divers had backed off to the edge of the rise. Not one of them had a gun up.

Wrack swam forward and laid his palm on the metal. It was warm. And under his hand he could feel what was wrong with it, the way you feel a splinter. There were a hundred small failures. Bearings were worn and channels silted, and at the centre a great heart was labouring.

"It's tired," he said. "It's so tired."

He did not think about what he did next. He asked the sea, gently this time, and the bound water in the Dragon's ribs moved for him. It flushed the silted channels one by one. His hands went into the open panel and found the worst of it. Then both things were happening at once. The water worked where his fingers could not reach, and his fingers worked where water was no use, and for a little while he was not afraid of anything.

The note rose again. It was clearer than before.

"Get in," Okonta said. She was staring at him. "All of you. You, with your father. You, with the beard, bring your wretched bale. Get in before they remember what they're paid for."

He looked back once from the panel. The officer was still standing where she had stopped, with her lamp on him and her gun hanging at her side.

Then the Dragon closed its eye and ran north.

---

He had expected the inside of a machine. It was more like the inside of a song.

The panel had let them into a single hall that ran the whole length of the Dragon. The mariners called it the gallery. It was flooded, and warm, and the water in it was perfectly still, though the thing was moving faster than the needle ever had. The ribs arched overhead one after another, away and away, until they met in a point of light a kilometre off. And between the ribs there were no walls. There was only the bound water, pouring past, bright and clear as glass. He was looking out through it as through the back of a waterfall, and the whole sea was going by.

For three thousand years people had made themselves at home there. Hammock-nets hung between the ribs, some new, some so old they were grey. There were cooking-stones, and a rack of somebody's dried fish. There were names scratched here too, in a band along the floor, a kilometre of names. Low in the belly was a row of cradles shaped to the needles, and Okonta laid hers into one, where it settled with a click. Each cradle had a name cut beside it. One near the end stood empty, with silt in it. The name beside that one was short and square, and it was not a mariner's name. Somebody had once tried to scratch it out, and had given up. And the walls hummed. The note was everywhere. After an hour he ceased to hear it, and after a day he could not imagine silence.

"Nobody knows what it was for," Okonta said. She had hung a hammock with the ease of long habit. "A road, I think. The Makers had cities all round the world and this went between them. Now it's all the road there is. There's no other way from one end of Ovara to the other that doesn't take half a year and likely kill you." She looked up the long shining hall. "I've ridden it forty times. I've never once seen it empty of somebody going to the Wells to be mended. That's what it's really become. An ambulance."

The woman from Fallow had settled her father in a net near the wall, where he could see out. His breathing had eased a little in the warmth. She came to Wrack that first evening, twisting her hands, and tried to thank him. He did not know what to do with it.

"It stopped for him," she said to Okonta afterwards, not quite out of his hearing. "It *stopped*. What is he?"

"A man who can't work a gate latch," Okonta said. "Leave him be."

They ran north for three days, and he watched the world go by. It was a run the Dragon had once made in two, Okonta said. Even mended, it was going at the pace of a tired horse.

On the first day it was the forest, seen from above, an endless gold canopy rippling in their wake. The lit domes of holds stood up through it like lamps in a field. He counted nine. Twice there was a hold that showed only the faint green-yellow of lampweed, and once a dome that showed nothing at all. Nobody in the gallery spoke as they passed over it.

That night the pale singers found them. He woke to the song, close, and went to the wall. There were six of them, running alongside. They were white, vast, and blunt-headed, each as long as a ship, with small wise eyes. They kept pace for an hour without visible effort, rolling to look in through the bound water at the people inside. Their voices came through the wall and through his ribs. A calf no bigger than a fishing boat swam so near that he could see the scars on its mother's flank.

"They like it," Brask said, beside him. The big man had come to watch with his arms folded. "My grandmother says they think it's one of them. A slow old cow that's lost her voice and only knows the one note." He grunted. "They'll leave it at the ice. They don't care for the ice."

On the second day the forest ended and the water darkened to a deep cold blue. The bottom fell away. Now there was nothing to see below them but distance, and above them the surface, like a ceiling of beaten silver. The cold came through even the bound water, and Brask at last looked comfortable. Wrack spent that day with his hands inside the Dragon's workings. He had found that the floor of the gallery opened, here and there, on seams like the lamp's. He lay on his stomach for hours with his arms in to the shoulder, clearing channels, seating things that had slipped. He did not understand what he was doing. He understood it as you understand how to catch something thrown at you.

Brask watched this too, and at length sat down beside him with a companionable thud.

"You'll want to know about the Pale," he said. "Since you're going to the old man's, and southerners know nothing. It's dark half the year. Not black dark. Twilight, with the sun going round the edge of the sky like a hound round a table. The other half, it's the same sun, a bit higher. The water's still. No current up there, no kelp. So everything that lives there makes its own light, or it'd never find anything to eat." He scratched his beard. "Rime's the only hold. We're frozen into the bottom of the ice. Six hundred of us. We hunt seal on top and dive under for the fat cod, and we send the best of it down to the Lantern, because they've nine hundred healers there and not one of them can catch a fish. That's the bargain. They mend our bones for nothing, and we feed them for nothing, and nobody's written it down."

"What's the Lantern like?"

"You'll see, if he lets you down the Well. My mother's brother had his back broken under a floe. They sent him down there carried by four men, and he walked home." Brask said it flatly, as a thing known. "I've seen it from above. Everybody has. You look down the Well, and it's black, blacker than anything, and right at the bottom there's a star." He was silent. "There's fewer points on the star than when I was a boy. Nobody says so."

On the third day the light changed.

The silver ceiling went white and came down to meet them. It was ice. The Dragon ran beneath it for an hour. The underside of the shelf was a country of its own, turned upside down, with blue valleys and hanging ridges, and domes of trapped air that shone like mercury. Shoals of something small and luminous browsed along it. And then, clinging to the ice like a swallow's nest to a roof, there was a Maker dome hung upside down in the cold, glowing amber, with little lights going to and fro beneath it.

"Rime," said Brask, and shouldered his bale. "I'll swim from here. It's quicker than going round by the landing." He looked at the old man from Fallow, asleep in his net, and at the daughter. Then he looked at Wrack. "They say nobody starves on Jef's doorstep. That's us that sees to it. I'll be over."

Okonta carried him out on the needle, bale and all, and was back in a quarter of an hour.

The note of the Dragon had begun to fall. Wrack went to the wall. The ice had drawn back. They were coming up, slowly now, into a great stillness.

"It doesn't stop," Okonta said. "Except for you, apparently. But at each end of the world it goes round the Well at a walk, as if it were looking in. You step off onto the landing as it passes. It's the only time in a circuit you can reach out and touch something that isn't moving." She put a hand on his shoulder, briefly, which she had not done before. "Mind your manners. He'll have felt what you did to it."

## Chapter 4. The Mechanic

At the top of the world the water was black and perfectly still, and everything in it glowed.

He stepped off the Dragon onto a shelf of smooth dark rock, and it went on past him, rib after rib, at the pace of a man walking. It seemed to take a long time to go. Then its light was dwindling round the far curve of the Well, and he could see where he was.

He stood on the lip of a hole in the world. The shelf ran away to either side of him in a great curve, and before him the rock simply ended, and there was nothing. It was not darkness as he had known it in the Tangle. It was depth. The water was so clear and so still that he could feel the whole height of it under him, and his stomach turned over. Far, far down, at the bottom of that black shaft, there was a single white star.

"The Lantern," said Okonta. "The hospital. You'll see it close, if he lets you."

Lantern jellies drifted over the gulf. Brask had called them lights, and that was what they were, great soft bells of pale blue fire the size of houses, trailing veils that glimmered and went out and glimmered again. They were in no hurry. There must have been a hundred. Weed on the rocks shone a pale green, like the lampweed at Low Anthem, but living, and so thick that it lit the shelf underfoot. Small fish went by, each with its own lantern on a stalk. Nothing moved fast. Nothing made a sound. He had come from a forest that never stopped singing to a place that seemed to be holding its breath.

Okonta's passengers were met at the landing. Two healers in grey had come up from the Well for the old man from Fallow, as though they had known he was coming. They had a litter with them, slung between two lantern jellies on cords. The daughter went with it. She looked back at Wrack once from the edge. Then they sank away into the black, the two blue lights growing small, toward the star.

The house stood a little way along the shelf, where the rock broke the surface.

It was half in the water and half out of it, under a sky the colour of a pigeon's breast, with a low red sun rolling along the rim of the world. It was built of the dark stone of the shelf, low and thick-walled, with a roof of slabs. A garden of weed had been planted in the shallows in front of it, in beds edged with shells. There was a line of washing, quite stiff with frost. At one end was a workshop with its door standing open on a clutter of half-mended things. A kettle sat on the step. There were no guards and no gates.

It was empty.

"Sometimes they're south," Okonta said, and shrugged. "They've a house at the other end as well, just like it. They go to and fro as they please, and nobody knows which they're in. The rays know. The rays don't tell me." She sat down on the step beside the kettle. "We wait. It's his rule: anyone may wait, nobody goes in the workshop."

They waited eleven days.

Brask came on the second, as he had said, with two cousins as big as himself and a sledge of smoked cod. He stacked it on the step without being asked and stayed to drink something hot out of the kettle. After that somebody from Rime came every other day. They were blunt, loud, generous people, who laughed at Wrack's thin southern tunic and brought him a sealskin coat. They would not hear of payment. A girl of ten showed him how to walk on the ice above the house. He stood up there in the long red twilight with the cold burning his face. The white went on to the edge of the sky. He understood that he was at the end of the world.

Mostly he sat on the lip of the Well and practised asking.

Okonta could not teach him; she had no gift. But she had kept company with callers for thirty years, and she knew how they began. "Don't ask her to do anything," she said. "Ask her what she's touching. That's the first thing a child learns. Callers name it Listening."

So he sat with his hand in the water and asked, as politely as he knew how, and for three days nothing happened. On the fourth he felt the shelf. He felt it not under his hand but all of it, the whole curve, as though his hand had grown. He felt the weed on it, and a crab walking. The next day he felt the jellies, each a slow cold pulse. The day after that he reached down into the Well as far as he dared. Something very large was coiled against the wall a kilometre below him, and it had felt him too. He took his hand out of the water quickly.

His eyes went often to the workshop door. His hands wanted to go in. He did not let them.

On the twelfth day the Dragon came round again. An old man came up the path from the landing with a basket of crabs. He was arguing mildly with the woman beside him about whether they had left a window open at the other house.

He was small. He was older than anything Wrack had ever seen, older than the lamp in Low Anthem, with skin like paper that has been folded many times. He had a few white hairs and a pair of very bright, very dark eyes, and he wore a leather apron over a jersey with a hole in the elbow. He walked like a man whose feet hurt. He glanced at Okonta, nodded, glanced at Wrack, and put the basket down.

"Somebody's been in my Dragon," Jef said.

"I'm sorry. It was—"

"Did I say I minded? The third ventral coupling. You reseated it backwards."

"It runs cooler that way."

There was a pause.

"Yes," Jef said slowly. "It does. I've been meaning to try that for two hundred years." He turned to the woman. "Maelis. He reseated it backwards."

The Queen of the Stingrays was tall and straight and grey-haired, and she wore a patched coat with the pockets full of something. She had a long, quiet, attentive face. Behind her, out in the dark water of the Well, shapes the size of ships were turning slowly. They were pale underneath and dark above, winged, and they flew through the water without haste. Wrack felt himself looked at by minds a great deal older than the house.

"The rays have been talking about you for a month," she said. "They say the sea has been shouting. Come in. You'll want soup."

---

Okonta had said he would be disappointed, and he was, for about an hour.

The inside of Kettle House was one long room with a stove at one end and a table that had been mended many times. There were shelves of jars and a box bed behind a curtain. A lantern jelly no bigger than a fist pulsed in a glass bowl on the windowsill; Maelis fed it crumbs. Jef took off his boots with a groan and put his feet on the stove rail. He complained about the crabs, the damp, and the state of his knees. He asked Okonta whether the Rime people had been leaving fish again and whether anybody had thought to bring up the washing. He was a tired old man in his own kitchen. There was no god in the room anywhere.

Maelis cooked. She split the crabs with a short knife and put them in a pot with weed from the garden. When she set a bowl in front of Wrack she looked at him properly for the first time. Her gaze went over his face as Ione's intended water had gone through his skull.

"Somebody tied your hands," she said.

"Yes."

"And you don't know who."

"I don't know anything before the kelp."

"Mm." She gave him a spoon. "Eat. He'll want to look at your hands and he'll be rude about it. He's rude when he's frightened."

"I am not frightened," Jef said, from the stove. "I'm nine hundred years old. I've been frightened of everything already." But when the bowls were empty he pushed his own aside and said, "Well. Let's see them."

He took Wrack's hands in his and turned them over under the lamp. His own were knotted and spotted and astonishingly sure. He looked at the fingertips, and pressed the pads. He bent the thumbs back.

"No calluses to speak of. Never held a hook before last month. A pen, though. You've a pen-bump, look, there." He let go. "Okonta says you opened a hall lamp that I'd have said was dead for good. And then my Dragon. Who taught you?"

"Nobody. My hands knew."

"Hands don't know anything. Heads know, and tell the hands, and forget they did it." He leaned back. He was studying Wrack now with a fixed bright attention that was not rude at all. "I learned from walls. I'll tell you about that some time. It took me eleven years to open my first seam, and I had the pictures in front of me. And you just reach up." He shook his head. "Go to bed. There's a loft. In the morning you'll come into the shop and I'll put something in front of you and we'll see what your head's forgotten."

In the loft, under a sealskin, Wrack lay and listened to them talking below, the old man's grumble and her low replies. Once Jef laughed, a short surprised bark. Outside the window the jellies went by. He slept better than he had since the kelp.

---

By day Jef taught him the craft.

The workshop was the largest room in the house and the only untidy one. Every bench and shelf was heaped. There were lamps in pieces, and things like the lamp's heart laid open, and coils of a pale wire that was warm to the touch. Along one wall hung tools. They were small, and of no shape Wrack could have described, and each had been worn by a hand until it shone.

"The mariners say tend, mend, make," Jef said, on the first morning. He was hunting for something under a bench. "Tend is what the holds do. Wipe it, sing to it, don't poke it. I've nothing against that. It's kept the machines alive three thousand years, which is more than poking would have. Mend is what I do. Find out why it's stopped, and stop it stopping. Make." He came up with a dented grey ovoid and blew dust off it. "Make is building a new one from nothing. I can do that too. Slowly. One air-heart takes me four years. There are two thousand air-hearts in the world, and they're all the same age." He set the thing on the bench. "This is a warm-stone. It heats a room. It's the simplest thing they made. Tell me what's wrong with it."

It was not like learning. It was like being reminded.

Jef would open a machine on the bench and begin to explain it, and Wrack's hands would already be moving. The old man watched this, day after day, with an expression that was hard to read. He was a good teacher, and it was plain that he had expected to be needed more than he was. He was impatient with words, and endlessly patient with the work itself. He would show a thing once and then go and sit on a stool with a mug while Wrack did it wrong six times. On the seventh he would say, "There," as if he had known all along which try it would be.

"You've done this before," he said once. "Not *this*. But something. You've stood over drawings. You've told other people where to put things. You think in sections, look, you always start by working out where you'd cut it." He sniffed. "Agravar training. All those straight lines."

"Is that bad?"

"It's different. The Makers didn't think in straight lines. They thought like water. Everything they built wants to flow from somewhere to somewhere, and when it stops, it's silted up, or it's seated wrong, or it's worn through. That's all there is. Clear it, seat it, or replace it." He drank. "You'll be better than me at seeing where to cut. And worse at knowing when not to. Hand me that."

In the afternoons, when Jef's hands tired, they sat on the step, and it was then that the old man talked. He did not tell his story. He let pieces of it fall, like a man emptying his pockets.

He had been a sick child. That was how he had come to the Lantern. He had had a wasting fever at six years old, and had been carried north on the Dragon by an aunt, and left in a pool in the fourth hall. The healers cured him in a season. Nobody came to fetch him. "There was a war on, down in the Anthem Waters," he said. "People had other things to think of." So he had stayed. He swept the halls and carried for the healers. And at night, when the patients slept, a small boy with nothing to do wandered through nine halls of Maker glass, and in those days the walls still spoke.

"Archive walls," Jef said. "You've not seen one working. Hardly anyone alive has. You'd put your hand on the glass and it would light, and there'd be pictures. Moving. How a thing was built. Every part, one after another, as slow as you liked." He held his palm up flat, remembering it. "The healers thought it was pretty. I thought it was the only thing in the world. I sat in front of those walls for eleven years. I learned their writing off them. I'd get a tool out of a store nobody'd opened in two thousand years and hold it up to the picture to see which way round it went."

"Where are the walls now?"

"Dark. They went one at a time. The last one in the Lantern went out when I was about three hundred. I've never found out how to mend them; they never showed me themselves." He turned the mug in his hands. "So it's all in here now." He tapped his forehead. "One old head. I've told the elders for six centuries that it's a stupid place to keep it."

Of the war, and of Sounding, he said nothing at all. Wrack did not ask.

---

In the second week Jef took him down the Well.

They went on a deep-runner, which was a needle's stout cousin, blunt and thick-hulled and slow. Jef rode it as Okonta rode hers, without touching anything. The descent took nine hours. The water grew cold, and then colder, and so heavy that Wrack's bones ached and each breath was like lifting a weight. An islander would have been dead before the jellies were out of sight, Jef said. The Makers had built their people for the deep, and the air of their halls with them. It was one more thing Wrack's body knew that he did not. The jellies were left behind. The green weed gave out. There was nothing but the runner's small light on the black wall of the trench, going by, and the star below them, which did not seem to grow.

Halfway down something uncoiled from the wall.

It was the thing he had felt. An eye the size of a cartwheel regarded them, gold, with a slotted pupil. An arm as thick as the Dragon's neck drifted across their path, set with suckers like dinner plates. Behind the first there were others, a whole wall of them, stirring.

"Well-wardens," said Jef. "Squid. Don't stare, it's rude." He held up a small worn disc of the pale metal, and the arm withdrew. "Nobody put them here. They came on their own, about the time the hospital did, and they've let nobody down since who meant harm. I've no idea how they tell. I asked Maelis to ask the rays, and the rays thought it was funny."

Then at last the star grew, and came apart into nine.

The Lantern stood on the floor of the Well. It was nine great halls of Maker glass, set in a ring and joined by covered ways, each hall as large as the whole dome of Low Anthem. The glass was the colour of milk and lit from within, so that the hospital stood in the black water like a crown of lamps. Healers in grey went to and fro in the covered ways. And all around it, even here, even in that cold, things grew: white fans, and pale flowers the size of his hand, feeding on the warmth that leaked from the walls.

Inside it was warm and bright and very quiet. Each hall was a single high room, and the floor of each was set with pools. In the pools the sick of the world floated, in water that glowed faintly gold. Healers sat at the edge of every pool with their hands in the water. They worked in shifts, Jef said, three to a pool, day and night, so that the intention never lapsed. Wrack saw a child whose hand was growing back; it was translucent still, like a hand made of wax, and she was holding it up to the light and turning it. He saw a man lying with his chest open like a book, and under the water things were being put right in it. He saw an old woman, brought from the far side of the world, whose lungs had been remade. She was breathing in her sleep without effort. Two pools along, in the same hall, the old man from Fallow lay with his daughter's hand on his forehead.

The gift was strong here. He could feel the sea leaning close, as a person leans over a cradle.

"Why here?" he asked. "Why is it stronger?"

"Don't know. I'm a mechanic." Jef was walking slowly, nodding to healers, who bowed to him in a way he clearly disliked. "Maelis says it's because it's the oldest water there is. It went down at the Tips before there were people and it's never been anywhere else. Nothing's ever been put in it but care." He stopped at a tall archway. "Here's what I wanted you to see."

The hall beyond was dark. The pools were dry. So was the hall after it, and the two after that. Four of the nine.

"Five hundred years I've been patching this place," Jef said. He did not grumble it. He said it quietly, with his hand on the cold glass of the arch. "There's one great heart under the middle of the ring that feeds all nine halls. It's going. I can't get at it without shutting down the five that still work, and if I shut them down, the water in those pools goes dead in an hour, and so do some of the people in them. So I've mended round the edges. And every hundred years or so, another hall goes." He looked at Wrack sidelong. "Unless somebody could hold the water in five halls just as it is. Warm, and still, and cared for. While somebody else had his hands in the works."

"I can't intend. I don't know how."

"You don't need to. The healers will go on intending. You only need to keep the water from knowing the heart's stopped." He scratched his chin. "It's a fool's job. I'd not ask if I had another two hundred years."

It took Wrack six days.

He sat in the centre of the ring with his hands in a channel of seawater, and asked the sea, pool by pool, to stay as it was. That was Holding. He had done it at Kettle House with a cupful. Here there were three hundred pools, and a person in each. He did not shout. He was too frightened to shout. He asked, and went on asking, hour after hour, and felt the Well lean in around him and take the weight. Under the floor Jef worked, with a lamp between his teeth, calling up for tools. On the fourth day Wrack went down to help him, still holding, and discovered that he could do both. One part of him kept three hundred pools still, and another lay on its back in a crawlway beside a nine-hundred-year-old man, handing him things.

On the seventh day all nine halls came alight together.

The healers stood in the covered ways and wept. A very old one, a woman who had been at the Lantern sixty years, went into the fourth hall and stood in the middle of it, turning round and round. Jef said "Hm," and went to see about lunch.

---

By night the sea taught him the rest. Jef could not help with that.

"I've no gift," the old man said. He was sitting on the step with a cup of something hot, watching. It had become his habit. "Never had. I'm a mechanic. The sea doesn't talk to me; I just fix her plumbing. The callers have the gift, and they can't tell a coupling from a clam. That's how it's always been. Makers' craft in one skin and sea's gift in another, and never the two together." He drank. "Then you."

It was Maelis who taught him the names of what he was doing. She was a healer, not a caller, but she had sat with callers at the end of their strength for fifty years and knew the road. Listening came first, she said, and he had found that for himself. After it came Moving, the plain push and pull, a current where there had been none. Then Shaping, giving water an edge and a form, a wall, a rope, a bubble of air. Then Holding, which he had learned at the Lantern in the hardest way. Raising came last of all, the lifting of water against its own weight. Most callers lived and died without it.

"You began at the wrong end," she said. "You raised a ship before you could listen to a crab. It's as if a child ran before it had seen its own feet. No wonder she knocked you down." She was sitting beside Jef with a rug over her knees. "Go back to the beginning. Do each one small. Ask for less than you think you can have. You'll be astonished how much she gives a person who doesn't grab."

So he went back. Night after night he sat at the lip of the Well, under the turning sun, and asked for small things. He asked for a ripple, and then for a current to carry one jelly a little to the left. He made a rope of water and tied it in a knot. He held a bowl of sea upside down in the air until it was easy, and then until it was dull. Every night the sea gave him a little more, as though it had only been waiting for manners. He was ebbed twice, mildly, a day of cold and thirst each time. After the second he began to know the feeling of the edge before he reached it.

At the end of a month a sphere of water a hundred metres across hung in the air over the Well, turning slowly, with a living shoal inside it. The low sun shone through it and laid a moving red light across the ice.

"Why me?" he said. He set it down as gently as an egg.

"Don't know," said Jef. "Maelis thinks you do, somewhere under all that."

"I think you had a foot in each world before you had a name," Maelis said. "I think that's what the sea likes about you. But I'm guessing. The rays know, and they won't say. They say it's yours to find."

She went in. Jef stayed, turning the cup in his hands.

"I'll tell you what I do know," he said. "I'm dying. Don't make that face. I've had nine hundred years and I'm sick of my own cooking. I found a thing in the walls once, when I was young, that keeps a body going. Maker medicine. It's a machine, like everything of theirs. I've mended it as often as it'll stand." He rubbed his chest absently. "When I go, there's nobody. The lamps go, one at a time. The air-hearts. The holds, and the Dragon. No more Threaded, because I'm the only one who can lay the Thread. A couple of million people in the dark, slowly. It might take two hundred years. That's the worst of it. It'd be so slow that nobody would ever decide to do anything."

"You could teach someone."

"I've tried. Sixty apprentices in nine centuries. Clever ones. They could learn to tend. A handful could mend a little, with me standing over them. Not one could carry it away. One carried off everything but the hands, and I'd as soon not talk about him. I used to think it was the mariner in them. Too patient. Too respectful of the thing." He nodded at the place in the sky where the sphere had hung. "I was looking for a mechanic. I'd have settled for a middling one. I did not expect to get *that*."

He went in after a while. Through the window Wrack saw him stop behind Maelis's chair and rest his hand on her shoulder. She put her own up to cover it without looking round.

---

He came to know her slowly. She did not let fall pieces of herself as Jef did. She answered what she was asked, exactly, and then stopped.

She had been born at the other end of the world, in the Quiet, and trained as a healer at the Hush, the southern hospital. At twenty she had been sent to gather venom. The great rays lived in those waters, and their sting killed in seconds, and a drop of it rightly used could wake a stopped heart. The healers took it from the spines the rays shed. She had gone to the place where they gathered, and one of them had come and looked at her.

"That's all," she said. "It looked at me. For about a day. When it had finished I knew things I hadn't known, and I could tell what it wanted. They don't speak. They remember, and they can put a memory into you as you'd put a cup in someone's hand. They wanted somebody who could say it in words to the rest of us, now and then. I was standing there." She was stripping weed for the pot. "The mariners called me their queen. It's a silly word. I'm their mouth."

"What are they?"

"Older than the Makers. Older than the holds. They've watched everything that ever happened in the sea, and they've kept it." She glanced at the window, where the long shapes turned. "They keep what the undertow takes, too. Did Ione tell you that? No, she wouldn't know. Nothing the sea is given is ever lost. It goes to them."

He set down the knife he was holding.

"Then they have it. What I lost."

"Yes."

"Can they give it back?"

"Through me, they can. There's a thing I make." She went on with the weed. "I'm not going to make it yet. You're not ready to want it, and he hasn't finished with you. And there's a question I ask first. I ask everyone. You've not got an answer to it."

"How did you come to marry him?"

She smiled then, for the first time. It changed her whole face.

"He came south to mend the warm-stones at the Hush, fifty-one years ago. He was eight hundred and something. I was twenty-four and I'd been the rays' mouth for four years, and everyone I met either knelt to me or ran away. He asked me to hold a lamp for him. Then he told me I was holding it wrong." She shook her head. "There were wives before me. Seven. He remembers every one of them, and he's never once called me by the wrong name. I asked the rays if it would be a foolish thing to do. They showed me him at twenty. So I did it."

"What was he like at twenty?"

Maelis did not answer for some time.

"Sorry," she said. "He was very, very sorry. Pass me the pot."

---

The visitor came in the seventh week, on a day of thin fog.

Okonta knew first. She had been mending a strap on the step, and she stopped with the awl in the air and turned her head toward the south.

"Needle," she said. "One. Coming up under the ice." She frowned. "I know every hull in the north by its voice. I don't know that one. It's an old pattern. I've seen its like in a cradle on the Dragon, with nobody to ride it."

Out in the Well, the rays had stopped turning. Wrack had never seen them still. They hung in the black water all facing one way, like weathervanes.

Maelis came to the door with a cloth in her hands. She looked at the rays, and her face shut. It was like a house with the shutters put up.

"Put the kettle on," Jef said, to nobody. He had come out of the workshop. He stood wiping his fingers, slowly, one at a time. "No. I'll do it. Wrack, fetch another bowl."

It came up at the landing without a sound. It was a needle, shorter than Okonta's and grey with age, and its flank was scored as if it had been dragged over stone. The rider got off it awkwardly. He wore a hard suit. It was surface work, the finest Wrack had seen, dark and close-fitting, with a helmet of clear glass. He waded ashore, and undid the helmet, and breathed the cold air with evident relief.

He was an old man. He was not old as Jef was; he was perhaps seventy. He was broad in the hand and heavy in the shoulder, and his white hair was cut close. He had a calm, square, weathered face. It was the face of a man who has been listened to for a long time. Above the collar of the suit, thin pale scars ran down from his temples and along his neck.

Okonta saw them. Wrack heard the breath go out of her.

"Hello, old man," the visitor said.

"Anselm," said Jef. "You've got fat."

"I've got rich. It comes to the same thing at my age." He looked past Jef to the door. "Maelis."

"You'll not come into my kitchen," Maelis said. Her voice was quite steady. "You can have the step. It's his rule that anyone may wait. It was never mine." She went in and shut the door, and they heard the bar go across.

So the three of them sat on the step in the fog, with soup. Okonta stood a little way off with her arms folded, and did not sit at all. The visitor ate neatly. He praised the crab. He asked after Jef's knees. He might have been a nephew on a duty call. Only his eyes kept coming back to Wrack. They had gone to him in the first moment, and rested there, as a man's eyes rest on something of his own that he has come upon in a neighbour's yard. He had not seemed surprised.

"And this is the one," he said at last. "The long-call is full of him. A stranger's hands."

"He can open a seam," Jef said.

"So I had always understood." The visitor set down his bowl. "I'll say what I came to say, and then I'll go. I've found your city. The one under the Girdle. You told me about it when I was twenty-three. You'd looked for it for a hundred years, you said, and given it up. It's there. It's whole. It lit from end to end, once, for one man, and went dark again when he left it." He did not look at Wrack as he said it. "There's an engine in the lowest vault. My people have had the half of it out, like a tooth, and bolted it to a frame. It runs about as well as a tooth would. I want it seated as it was meant to be, and I've nobody now who can. Come south and do it. Or tell me how."

"What does it do?"

"You know what it does."

"I know what sort of thing they'd have put in a lowest vault," Jef said. "I'd not seat it for you if you carried me there on your back."

"No. I thought not." The visitor nodded, as at a column of figures that has come out as he expected. "You'll have told him about the holds, I suppose. That they're going. You told me, on this step. I was twenty-two, and I didn't sleep for a month. You've had your hand cupped round a candle for nine hundred years, old man, and you call it a life's work. Up there are forty million people on a few rocks. I can give them ground. Real ground, to stand on, that nobody is standing on already."

"At whose cost?"

"At the cost of people who are dead already and haven't been told."

"Go home, Anselm."

"Yes." He stood, and picked up the helmet. Then he turned to Wrack, directly, for the first time. His voice was gentle. "You don't know your name, do you?"

Wrack's mouth had gone dry. "No."

"I do. I could tell it to you now, in a breath. I could tell you what you were, and what you were paid, and what you're worth. It's a great deal." He held Wrack's eyes. "Come south with me, and I'll tell you all of it on the way. You'd be home in a week. You'd know your own front door."

It went into him like a hook. He had told himself for weeks that he no longer thought of the blank every hour. He found that his hands were shaking. He put them between his knees.

Jef said nothing. He was looking at the water.

"What am I to you?" Wrack said.

"An investment." The visitor smiled a little. It was not unkind. "Twenty-two years of one. The best I ever made."

"Then you'll know who tied my hands."

There was a pause. It was the only time the visitor seemed to choose his words.

"Yes," he said. "I do know that."

Wrack looked at the old man beside him, in the apron, with the hole in his elbow. He looked at the barred door. He thought of the warm-stone lying open on the bench, half done.

"I'll stay," he said.

"Of course you will. He's put you in a jersey and given you a stool, and you think it's a home." The visitor said it to Jef, without heat. "You did that to me."

"You were happy."

"I was twenty." He settled the helmet on its ring, and his voice came out of the suit flat and small. "I shan't come again."

"No," said Jef. "I don't expect I'd be in."

The grey needle went down from the landing and was gone. Out in the Well, one by one, the rays began to turn.

Nobody spoke for some time. Then Okonta said, "That Thread is older than mine."

"It's forty-nine years old. I laid it myself." Jef had not moved. "He was the fifty-ninth. The best head of the lot. He could draw anything he'd seen once. He's an islander. The rays pulled him out of a wreck when he was a boy and left him at the Hush, and I found him sweeping it, as I swept the Lantern. I thought—" He stopped. "Well. He had hands like a pair of feet. Eight years, and he never opened a seam. I gave him the Thread in the end. I thought that if he could hear the machines, his hands might learn. He heard them very well."

"What does he do now?" Wrack asked.

"Owns things."

"He knows who I am."

"Yes."

"You could have let him say it."

"I could." Jef turned his head then. His bright dark eyes were very tired. "And it would have been true, every word, and it would have been his. You'd have had your whole life handed to you by that man, in his voice, with his price on it. I'll not have that. You'll get it another way or you'll not get it." He pushed himself up off the step, with a hand on Wrack's shoulder. "There's a thing I'd meant to leave till the spring," he said, to nobody. "I don't think I'll leave it."

---

The cold deepened. The sun dropped a little lower each day on its round, and the ice above the house groaned at night. Wrack had been at Kettle House two months. He could strip an air-heart and put it back. He could hear a crab walking at the bottom of the Well. Somewhere in those weeks he had stopped thinking of the blank in his head every hour, and thought of it only every day.

Jef was slower. Wrack saw it, because he was watching for it. The old man sat down more often. He let Wrack carry things. One morning he could not close his hand on a tool, and he stood looking at his fingers with a kind of detached annoyance, as at a machine that had been well maintained and had no excuse.

That night he cooked.

It was an event. He did not cook, Maelis said, more than twice a year, and the results were generally a matter for healers. He sent Wrack down to the weed garden and Okonta out for crab. He stood at the stove for two hours in his apron, swearing at it. What came to the table was a stew, red with some southern spice, and it was very good. Brask and one of the cousins had come over the ice with a cask of something made from fermented seal's milk. They stayed, and filled the room. Jef told a long scandalous story about an elder of Deep Chorus four hundred years dead. Brask laughed until he had to be thumped on the back. Even Okonta smiled into her cup. Maelis watched her husband down the length of the table. She ate almost nothing.

When the Rime men had gone out into the dark, singing, Jef pushed his bowl away and laid something on the table.

It was a tool. Wrack had seen it in his hand every day, and never out of it. It was small and worn and of no shape that he could have named, and its grip had been polished by nine hundred years of one man's fingers.

"That's for you," Jef said. "I took it out of a store at the Lantern when I was nine years old. Don't thank me. It's a thing for getting into seams, and you get into them without it, so you need it less than I did. But it's the one that'll do the job you couldn't do at Low Anthem. The lamp that was broken, not slipped. You felt there was a tool for it. That's the tool."

Wrack picked it up. It fitted his hand as though it had been waiting.

"I haven't taught you to make," Jef went on. "Tend, mend, make: that's the order, and making's the long one. We'll start tomorrow, or the day after. But there's something I want done first, while I've the hands for it." He glanced at Okonta. "Tell him what it's like. He'll not believe me."

Okonta turned her cup round on the table.

"You know what I can do with it," she said. "You've ridden behind me. What I've not told you is what it's like to have it." She touched the scar at her temple. "He lays a thread along the nerves, from here to the base of the skull. It's Maker stuff, he draws it himself. It takes a night. You sleep through it. When you wake, the old machines aren't things any more. They're more of you. I know where my needle is now, down at the landing, the way I know where my left hand is. When I'm riding, I hear through it for eighty kilometres." She paused. "And you're never by yourself again. Some of us can't bear that. There's always the hum of something, far off. It's company. I've come to like it."

"Does it hurt?"

"For a week. Like a tooth."

"You steered my Dragon's water from the outside," Jef said to Wrack, "like a man pushing a cart. It let you, because it's a patient old thing. You ought to be able to drive it. Feel it from the inside. Know what's wrong with it from a thousand kilometres off, before it stops." He rubbed his eyes. "I haven't laid a Thread in six years. There was a boy from Fallow; he was the last. It takes more out of me each time. I think I've one more."

"Do I have to swear? The rule?"

"No. I'm not making a monk of you. You've enough to carry." He almost smiled. "I'm making a mechanic who can hear his own machines. It's what I'd have given anything for at your age. I had to go round and knock on them."

Maelis had not spoken. She got up now and went into the still-room, where she kept her jars, and shut the door. After a moment Jef got up, slowly, with a hand on the table, and went in after her.

Wrack and Okonta sat and looked at the lamp. They could not hear the words. They heard her voice, low and steady and then not steady. They heard his going on, gently, for a long time. Once her voice cracked. Then there was silence. It went on so long that Okonta rose and began, very quietly, to clear the bowls.

When the door opened it was Maelis who came out. Her face was perfectly composed.

"Tomorrow night," she said to Wrack. "Eat nothing after noon. I'll give you something to make you sleep." She took her coat from its peg. "I'm going down to talk to the rays."

---

He spent the next day doing nothing, by order. In the afternoon Jef found him sitting on the lip of the Well.

The old man lowered himself down beside him with a grunt. They sat with their feet in the water, like two boys on a dock. The jellies went by. Far below, the star had nine points.

"She thinks it'll finish me," Jef said. "She's probably right. She generally is. I told her I'd rather be finished doing the one thing nobody else can do than last another year complaining about my knees." He threw a pebble and watched it sink, down and down, until it was gone. "She said that was a selfish thing to say to a woman. I said it was. Fifty-one years. You'd think I'd have learned to argue with her."

"Then don't do it. Teach me to make instead. I'd rather have that."

"You'd rather. Yes. So would I." He was silent a little. "Listen. I'll tell you something I've not said to an apprentice before. I never wanted to be the only one. People think I did. They think I kept it close. I'd have given it to anyone with the hands. I spent three hundred years begging holds to send me their cleverest children. They sent me their most devout ones." He shook his head. "I got to be a god because I was the last man who could mend a lamp. That's all a god is. Somebody people can't do without. It's a terrible thing to be. Don't let them do it to you. Teach everybody. Teach people you don't like."

"I will."

"You say that. You don't know yet what you'll want to keep." He looked at Wrack. It was a long, shrewd, not unkind look. "You've something under there, where your name was. I've seen you not thinking about it. I had one of those. I'll not tell you mine, and I'll not ask yours. But I'll tell you this for nothing. It doesn't get lighter for being left. I gave mine nine hundred years to get lighter."

He put his hand on Wrack's shoulder, and used it to push himself to his feet.

"Come up when the sun's at the notch," he said. "Don't be late. I'm not so sure of my hands after dark."

Wrack remembered little of the night. There was a bitter drink in a clay cup, and Maelis's cool hand on his forehead. There was the long table in the workshop, cleared for the first time, with a clean cloth on it. A cold white light hung overhead. Jef's face was above him, upside down, intent and steady. The old hands were quite sure of themselves. He heard the old man say, to nobody, "There you are. Now then." After that he heard nothing.

He woke in the box bed by the window, and the world had a second edge to it.

His head ached from the temples to the nape, as Okonta had promised. But it was the other thing he noticed. He could feel the needle moored at the landing as if it were his own foot. He could feel the deep-runner beside it, and the warm-stone on the workshop bench. Every lamp in the house was a small bright pressure, like sun on closed eyelids. Further off, there was a great slow pulse below him that was the heart of the Lantern. And a thousand kilometres south, moving, was a long bright hum that he would have known anywhere.

Jef was asleep in the chair beside the bed, with his chin on his chest and his apron still on.

"He wanted to be here when you woke," Maelis said from the door.

He did not wake that day. Wrack carried him to the box bed, and was shocked at how little he weighed. Maelis sat with him. She did not use her gift. Wrack asked her once, and she said that there was nothing wrong with him to mend. He was only very tired, she said, like a road near its end.

On the second night Wrack woke in the loft with his heart going hard.

Something was coming. He felt it with the new sense, the one that still ached. It was far to the south, under the ice, and it was fast. It was narrow and bright, like a needle. But a needle with a rider had a warmth in it; he had learned that from Okonta's. This had none. It was cold all through, and there was an edge on it.

He was down the ladder before he knew he had moved. Okonta was at the door with her hand to her temple.

"There's nobody on it," she said. "Something is driving it from a long way back. There's a hull sitting at the ice edge, eighty kilometres south. It isn't moving. It's listening." She looked at him. "Old pattern."

It went under the house. He felt it pass, like a draught under a door. Then it tipped, and went down the Well.

He ran to the lip and put his hand in the water. He felt the wardens rise. They came off the walls all together, a kilometre down, a whole forest of arms closing across the shaft. The thing went through them. He felt that too, and wished he had not.

Far below, the star flared white. Then it shrank. Two of its points went out, and then a third.

In his head the great slow pulse of the Lantern's heart staggered, and caught, and staggered again.

"That's a dart."

Jef was standing in the doorway in his nightshirt, holding on to the frame. Maelis was behind him. He was looking at nothing. He was listening, as Okonta listened.

"That's one of mine," he said. "I made forty of them, once. I broke thirty-eight with a hammer. I looked eight hundred years for the other two." He let out a breath. "Trust Anselm. He always could find a thing. He only couldn't mend it."

"There are three hundred people in those pools," Maelis said.

"I know how many there are." He had straightened. Wrack saw, with a kind of dread, that the tiredness had gone out of him. He looked as he looked at the bench when a thing was laid open on it. "Get the runner, Okonta. Wrack, you'll hold the water, as you did before. All of it. You'll start now, from here. You'll hold it the whole way down, and you'll not let go, whatever you see. Can you?"

"Yes."

"I'll want my apron."

Maelis had it in her hand already. She put it over his head and tied it behind him. Her fingers were quite steady. For a moment she stood with her forehead against the back of his neck. Neither of them said anything. Then she took down her coat.

It was nine hours to the bottom, and afterwards Wrack remembered almost none of it. He lay along the flank of the deep-runner with one arm in the water and held three hundred pools still. They were further off than they had ever been, and he was less afraid. He asked, and went on asking, and the old water of the Well leaned in and took the weight with him. Beside him, on the runner, Jef said something to Maelis, quietly, at some length. She answered him once.

They passed the wardens. Two of them were drifting loose, with their great arms trailing. The rest had drawn back against the wall. A gold eye followed the runner down.

One hall of the nine was dark. It was cracked from roof to floor, and the sea stood in it. The centre of the ring had been stove in. The dart was still there. It was sunk to half its length in the floor above the heart, a pale sliver no longer than a man, and it was humming faintly. The healers had not left their pools. They sat at every one with their hands in the water, three to a pool, in what light there was. Some of them were weeping. None of them had moved.

Jef looked at the dart for a moment. Then he got down on his knees, with a grunt, at the mouth of the crawlway.

"Lend me that," he said.

Wrack did not understand. Then he took the tool out of his pocket and put it into the old man's hand.

"I'll want it back," Jef said. "Don't come down. You've enough to do." He put the lamp between his teeth, and went in.

He was eleven hours under the floor.

Wrack could not go to him this time. The cracked hall was letting the cold in, and the pools wanted to know it. It took everything he had to keep them from knowing. He heard the old man's voice now and then, calling up for something, and a young healer ran with it. Later the voice came less often. Then it did not come.

Then the pulse steadied. He felt it in his head before the lamps showed it. The great heart under the ring drew one long clean beat, and another. The milk-white glass brightened round him, hall after hall. There were eight. The ninth stayed dark.

"Jef," he called down the crawlway.

He let the pools go, one at a time, each as gently as he had ever done anything. Then he went in on his elbows.

The old man was lying on his back under the heart, with the lamp beside him, still burning. One hand was up inside the works. It was resting on the thing it had seated. The other lay on his chest. It held the tool a little way out, handle first, as he had always handed a thing.

Wrack took it.

---

"He left something for me to tell you," Maelis said.

They had brought him up. It had taken a day. She sat on the step of Kettle House with the old man's hand in both of hers. The rays had risen out of the Well, hundreds of them. They hung motionless in the black water, wing to wing, as far as he could see.

"He said it on the runner, while you were busy. He said: *he has to ask for his memory himself.* Not be told it. Not by Anselm, and not by me. He said you'd understand it when it was time."

"He hadn't finished teaching me."

"No."

"I can't make. He said tomorrow. Or the day after."

"I know what he said." She laid Jef's hand down and smoothed it. "I'm taking him south. He liked the hot springs. He always said he was never once warm enough in this house."

"Who is he? The man who came."

"A boy I grew up with." Her voice was perfectly level. "Don't ask me now. When you're ready for my question, come to the Hush. I'll tell you about him then. It's the same story." She stood. She seemed taller than before, and much further away. "Not before."

The people of Rime came over the ice that day, all six hundred of them, and stood along the shelf without a word. The healers had come up with him out of the Well, in a long grey line, and they stood beside the ice-divers. Brask carried the old man down to the landing himself. He would not let anyone help.

The Dragon came round at dusk, at a walk. Maelis stepped aboard with Jef in her arms. The rays went too, all of them, flying beside it in the red twilight like an escort of sails.

Wrack stood on the landing with the tool in his fist. Okonta stood beside him. She had been south to the ice edge on her needle that morning, and had found open water with nothing in it. She said nothing now, and he was grateful for it.

Far out over the Well, the Dragon's long pale light flickered, steadied, and flickered again.

# Part Three: The Dark

## Chapter 5. The Lamps Go Out

For nine days after Jef died, nothing happened at all.

That was what Wrack found hardest. The kettle sat on the step. The washing hung on the line. In the workshop the warm-stone they had been working on lay open on the bench, with the old man's mug beside it and a ring of dried tea in the bottom. Wrack could not bring himself to move the mug. He worked round it.

He was trying to teach himself to make. He had the tool, and he had two months of watching. He took a dead air-heart out of the store behind the workshop. It was a small one, the size of a barrel, of the kind that served a single dry room. He laid it out in pieces and tried to see how a man might begin to build such a thing from nothing. He could see how each part fitted the next. He could not see how any of them had come to be. The pale metal did not look cast, or forged, or cut. It looked grown. Somewhere there was a first step, and Jef had been going to show it to him tomorrow, or the day after.

Okonta stayed. She did not say why, and he did not ask. She mended her suit, and cleaned her needle, and stood for long stretches at the edge of the Well with her head tilted, listening to things a long way off. Messages were coming up from the south, in the slow way they did. A long-call was passed from hold to hold. A Threaded had turned off his patrol to carry word. She gave him the sense of them at supper, shortly.

"They know," she said. "It's in every hold by now. Some of them don't believe it. Deep Chorus has declared forty days of singing. Nine Spires has asked who's to decide about the Dragon now, which is like them." She pushed her fish about. "The Threaded want to know who they answer to."

"Who do they?"

"Nobody. That was the point of us. We answered to him because he'd made us, and he asked for nothing but the rule." She looked up. "Some of them are asking whether the rule still holds with nobody to say it to. I've had that from three different waters."

"What did you tell them?"

"That I'd said it once, and didn't remember there being a clause about him staying alive." But she was frowning. "They're frightened. We were never many. And every one of us knows there won't be another."

"And the dart?"

"Nobody saw it but us and the healers. I've put it about that the Lantern was struck. I've not said by whom. I can't swear to a rider I never saw." She set down her knife. "I asked the old ones about a Threaded called Anselm. Two of them remembered a name beside a cradle. One had heard that the islanders' great bank is run by a man with scars on his neck. He'd taken it for a wreckers' tale."

On the ninth evening he was at the bench when the hum went wrong.

He had carried it at the back of his mind since he woke with the Thread: the Dragon, far to the south, a long bright steady note, like a lamp left burning in another room. Now it went ragged. It slowed. He put down the tool and stood with his hands flat on the bench and felt it labour, more than four thousand kilometres away, like a heart missing beats.

It ceased.

He put the tool in his pocket and went down to the landing. Okonta was already there. There was a second needle in the cradles, an old one of Jef's, shorter than hers and scarred along the flank. It woke under his hand as if it had been expecting him.

"Above Chorus Canyon," Okonta said, listening through her own hull. "Dead in the water." She looked at him. "Without it there's no reaching the south. No Maelis. No Wells for anyone who isn't already there. No anything."

"Then we go and start it."

"You've had the Thread for twelve days. You've never ridden."

"Then I'll learn on the way," he said.

He did, badly at first. There was nothing to hold and nothing to steer with. He lay along the needle, and it was as though he had been given a new limb in the dark and told to run on it. He thought *forward* and went into the ice. He thought *down* and nearly went into the Well. Okonta shouted at him for the first two hundred kilometres. Somewhere in the third hundred he stopped thinking at it and began simply to lean, as she did. After that the needle and he were one thing going south, and the sea opened in front of them like a door. He could hear through the hull. He could hear the pale singers, and the grind of the ice shelf, and the engines of a ship somewhere far to the east. He heard a hold go by beneath them with its people singing the forty days.

He did not sleep much. When he did, the needle carried him on, and he dreamed in its hearing.

---

They came down over the Anthem Waters on the sixth day.

He saw the Dragon first. It was a pale line lying across the top of the forest, motionless, like a rope dropped on a carpet. A faint mist was drifting from its ribs. It was the bound water coming loose, a little at a time, and going back into the sea it had been drawn from.

"How long?" Okonta asked.

He laid the question against what Jef had shown him. "Three days, perhaps, before it's lost too much to run. After that it's a ruin."

Then he looked down, and saw the canyon.

It was a crack in the floor of the world. The golden forest ran to its edge on both sides and stopped. Between the two rims there was a gulf a kilometre wide and so deep that the bottom was lost in blue. And down both walls of it, from the rim to as far as he could see, ran galleries. The Makers had cut them from the living rock and faced them with their pale glass. There was tier below tier below tier, hundreds of them, each a long lit window. Slender bridges crossed the gulf from wall to wall, one above another, and people were crossing them. Hanging gardens of weed trailed from every ledge. It was a city built like the inside of a throat.

"Deep Chorus," Okonta said. Her voice had changed. He remembered that she had been born there. "Twelve thousand people. The oldest hold in the north. There's a saying that the Makers built it to sing in. You'll hear why."

He heard it as they went down between the walls. Somewhere a choir was singing the mourning for Jef, forty voices or so, slow and grave. The canyon took it, and gave it back from the far wall a moment later. Then it came back again from below, deeper, and from above, and the echoes sang with the singers. The forty were four hundred, and the water rang like the inside of a bell. He had never heard anything so beautiful. People had lived inside that sound, he thought, all their lives, for a hundred generations. No wonder they were proud.

Then they passed the twentieth tier, and the white light of the windows gave out.

Below that the Maker lamps were dark, gallery after gallery, all the way down into the blue. In their place hung long swags of something that glowed a weak greenish yellow. He knew it at once. He had seen it on the dark side of the dome at Low Anthem.

"Lampweed," said Okonta. Her face had gone grim. "I was here two years ago. There was none. Not a strand."

The elders received them in a high hall on the third tier, under good lamps. It was the grandest room he had seen under the sea. The floor was inlaid with shell in the pattern of a wave. The roof was a vault of clear Maker glass, with the canyon's upper waters moving beyond it like a green sky. There were seven elders on seven stone seats. Their speaker was a tall, exact woman of seventy named Vey. She had silver hair bound close to her head, and she sat like a person who has never in her life been seen to slump.

She said that Deep Chorus was honoured. She said the hold was in excellent order. The lampweed was traditional at this season, as an observance of mourning. The Dragon would no doubt resume its course, as it always had. When Wrack asked to see the air-hearts, she said that would not be necessary.

"He lit the Lantern," Okonta said. "Nine halls, at the old man's side. I was there."

"We have heard a great many things," said Elder Vey, "since the god died." Her eyes rested on Okonta without warmth. "And you, daughter of this hold, have been thirty years away from it. You'll forgive us if we don't take instruction on its condition from a herder who left."

They were shown out. In the corridor a small figure detached itself from the wall, and something heavy and whiskered hit Wrack in the knees.

"Bother, *leave* it. Hello, Wrack."

Sefa was thinner. She had grown, or something in her face had. She wore a healer's grey band on her upper arm now, over a tunic that had been mended at the shoulder. Bother was beside himself. He went round Wrack three times and then tried to climb him.

"You're a healer," Wrack said, stupidly.

"Somebody had to be. Mother stayed at Low Anthem with the ones who couldn't travel. A hundred and ten of us came here, after the dome. I was the only one who could close a cut, so they gave me the band on the road." She had Ione's flat way of delivering bad news, he saw; she had not had it two months ago. "They put us at the bottom. Come and see. Come quietly. We're not supposed to be up here."

She took them down. She knew the back stairs and the service ways. Those were narrow tunnels behind the galleries, where the pipes ran, and where the people who cleaned the lamps had gone to and fro for three thousand years. They went down past tier after tier of failing light. The singing followed them. It grew fainter as they went, and stranger. At the bottom it was no longer music but a kind of slow breathing in the rock.

The lowest galleries were dry halls, long and low. Three thousand people were in them, in the green dimness. Many were from dark holds further north. He saw faces from Low Anthem, and they saw him. An old woman from the night crew lifted her hand a little from her blanket. They lay close together and very still, as people lie who have learned not to waste anything. The air was thick and stale and warm. He found himself breathing fast without getting anything from it.

"Why don't they go into the water? They can breathe water."

"For how long? Days? You can't sleep properly in it, not for weeks, not the old ones and the babies. You can't cook in it or get warm in it. There's a reason holds have dry halls." Sefa rubbed her eyes with the back of her wrist. "And the flooded galleries down here are going bad as well. The air-hearts clean the water too, did you know that? I didn't. It's all one thing. Three of the seven have stopped. The fourth is going."

"Does Vey know?"

"She's known for six years." Sefa's voice had gone hard and quiet. "They've been hiding it. They moved everyone who complained down here, where it's worst, because the lower tiers don't send speakers to the council. They hung the weed and called it tradition. I've had eleven people die this week of nothing but breathing." She was sixteen years old. "They think if they say it out loud there'll be panic, and the Breakers will take the hold. So they sing. They've got the whole hold singing forty days for Jef. It's very beautiful, and nobody can hear anything else."

He put his hand on the wall. Through it, through the Thread, he could feel them: seven great weights below him in the rock. Three were silent. Four were labouring, and in one of the four the labour had a catch in it that he knew from the Lantern.

"Show me."

The heart-chambers were at the very bottom of the canyon, under the last gallery, behind a door that had been sealed with wax and an elder's mark. He broke it. Inside, in a vault as big as the meeting hall at Low Anthem, stood an air-heart.

He had only seen the barrel-sized ones. This was the size of a house. It was a pale ribbed thing like a great closed shell, standing on its hinge. All round it ran channels as thick as his body, bringing the sea in on one side and carrying it away on the other. When such a thing was alive, it opened and closed, very slowly, a few times in an hour. With each closing it drew the foulness out of the water that passed through it. With each opening it breathed clean air up through the rock, into every dry hall above. It was the lungs of the hold. It was cold under his palm, and furred with a fine grey dust.

"How long for the people up there?" he said.

"In those rooms? Two days. Then they start to go quickly."

Two days here. Three for the Dragon. And the hearts would take him more than two days. He knew it the moment he touched the thing.

---

He chose the hold. He never afterwards thought it was the wrong choice. He only thought he had made it like a fool.

There was no time to do the work and no air to do it in. So he did what had served at the Lantern. He asked the sea to help him cheat.

He sat on the floor of the heart-chamber with one hand in a channel and reached out into every flooded gallery of the lower hold at once. In each one he asked the water to give up what air it carried, and to gather it against the roof in a great still bubble, and to keep it there. That was Shaping, and Holding. He had done each a thousand times at the lip of the Well. He pushed the bubbles through into the dry halls, room after room. It was air for three thousand people. He held it there with one part of his mind, and with the rest of himself he opened the first dead heart and went in.

It worked for a day and a night.

He did not sleep. Sefa brought him water, and he drank it without knowing. The heart was silted to its core. He cleared channels with his hands and with the sea, as he had in the Dragon. Then he found the real fault, deep in. It was a thing not slipped but broken. He took out Jef's tool and found that the old man had been right. It fitted. He worked.

Some time on the second day he noticed that he could not remember the tending song.

It was not that he had forgotten a line. The song was not there. He reached for it, idly, as you reach for a tune while your hands are busy, and there was a smooth place where it had been. He reached for the name of the tide hound that was leaning against his leg, and that was gone too.

A cold, pleasant feeling was spreading up from the soles of his feet. It was a looseness, like the moment before sleep. The three thousand rooms were very heavy. He could give the sea a little more of himself to hold them with. He could give it everything; it would be so much easier. He would not have to carry anything at all—

Something struck him across the face, hard.

"*Stop* it!" Sefa had him by the collar with both hands. Her face was a hand's width from his and she was shaking him. "You're going grey. You're going *under*. Mother told me what it looks like. Let go of it, Wrack. Let go of all of it, now!"

"They'll suffocate—"

"You'll be no use to anyone as a man who can't remember how to breathe! *Ask for less!*"

He let go. Far above, in three thousand rooms, the bubbles shivered and began to dissolve. He sat on the floor of the chamber with his head between his knees. The cold drew back slowly, like a tide. It did not return what it had taken.

"What's his name?" he said. "The hound."

Sefa stared at him. Her eyes filled.

"Bother," she said.

"Bother," he repeated. The hound looked up at the sound and thumped his flipper on the stone. He would have to learn it again, like a stranger's.

---

It was Sefa who went up to the high hall. He heard about it afterwards, from several people. They told it differently, and all of them told it as the thing they would remember about that year.

She went by the main stair, not the back ways. She took with her a net-maker from the lower tier whose son had died four days before. She walked into the council of the seven elders with the grey band on her arm, and nobody stopped her, because nobody could believe it was happening.

Deep Chorus had been built to sing in. Every word spoken in the high hall went out through the glass vault into the canyon, and the canyon gave it to every gallery. That was how the elders had made their announcements for three thousand years. It had not occurred to any of them that the hall would do the same for a girl.

She said that the air-hearts of Deep Chorus were failing, and had been for six years. She said that three were dead and a fourth was dying. She said that people were suffocating on the lowest tier, eleven that week, and she said their names. She said the man who had lit the Lantern was on his knees in the heart-chamber, trying to mend them, and that he had nearly gone into the undertow doing the elders' work for them. Then she asked what the choirs of Deep Chorus intended to do about it.

Twelve thousand people heard her, twice, and then a third time from below.

There was no panic. Elder Vey had been wrong about that.

The choirs did what choirs do. They organised. Deep Chorus had forty choirs, and each had a master, and each master knew every voice in it and where that voice lived. In six hours the whole lower hold had been moved up. They went tier by tier, the sick carried on litters across the bridges, into the galleries where the living hearts could still serve them. People were packed into the high halls. They lay in the elders' own chambers and along the stairs, and across the inlaid floor of the council hall itself. The singing of the forty days stopped. In its place the choirmasters called the tiers one to another across the gulf, in short sung phrases, and the canyon carried their orders more quickly than any runner.

The Mighty of Deep Chorus came down to the infirmary. He was a quiet grey man named Aurel, and he could have stopped a ship. He held one small bubble of clean air over the forty worst cases, and that was all. When he tired, another caller took it. They worked in shifts, and none of them asked for anything they could not carry.

It gave Wrack the time. He mended the first heart on the third day, and after that he knew the way. The one that had been dying he caught before it stopped.

The other two he could not mend. He lay under each of them for a night, with Jef's tool in his hand. They had not slipped, and they had not broken. The great shells were cracked through at the hinge, and the pale metal along the crack had gone grey and soft, like old bone. There was nothing left there to seat. They wanted making, and he could not make.

The dying heart came back at dawn on the fifth day. He was lying under it with a strand of lampweed tied round his wrist for light. He felt the great shell above him stir. It opened, very slowly, with a sound like a long indrawn breath. It closed. Then he felt air begin to move through the lower tiers, cold and clean. In the gallery overhead a lamp came on.

They came on down both walls of the canyon, one after another, tier by tier. He did not see it. People told him that the whole hold stood on the bridges and watched the light go down into the blue.

It reached the sixtieth tier, and there it stopped. Below that were forty tiers more, and they stayed dark. Five hearts could not feed them. The choirmasters sealed the lower stairs that week. Nobody has lived in the deep galleries of Deep Chorus since.

He was too tired to know whether he had won or lost. He went up through the hold with the tool in his hand, and people drew back against the walls to let him pass. Some of them reached out to touch his sleeve. Somewhere above him the choirs had started to sing. It was not the mourning. It was something older and quicker, and all twelve thousand voices echoed.

Elder Vey was waiting at the sea-door on the rim. She was alone. She looked a good deal older than she had five days earlier, and for the first time she was not standing quite straight.

"We were afraid," she said.

"I know."

"My grandmother was speaker when the first heart began to labour. She told my mother, and my mother told me, as you hand down a debt. Each of us thought it would last our time." She was silent a moment. "A child shamed us in our own hall. I've been trying to be angry about it, and I find I can't."

"She does that," Wrack said.

"Two of the hearts you could not mend," Vey said.

"No. They want making. I can't make."

"Then we are a smaller hold than we were, and we shall be smaller yet." She said it without complaint. "There is a thing you should know. A man came to this door in the spring. He came in a bell of glass, let down on a chain from a ship. He wore grey. He said that his house knew of our difficulty. He did not say how it knew. He said it had prepared a refuge. There were to be tanks, on an island shore, with the sea piped into them, and lamps that do not fail. There was passage for every soul in the hold. He had a paper for me to mark."

"What did the paper ask?"

"Service. Twenty years of it, from each of us, to pay for the passage."

"And you sent him away."

"I did. I have wondered every day since whether I had the right." Her mouth tightened. "Three holds to the north of us did not send him away."

She looked out over the forest, to where the long pale shape lay across the kelp.

"The Dragon," she said. "You chose us, and it has had five days. I am told it will not run again."

"We'll see," said Wrack.

He looked where she was looking. There were lights along the Dragon that had not been there five days before. They were small and pale, and they moved. He counted thirty of them before he stopped.

## Chapter 6. The Eldest Brother

The lights were needles.

There were forty-one of them. They lay in the cradles along the Dragon's belly, row on row, each with its faint glow pulsing, like a stable full of sleeping horses. Wrack had seen that row with one needle in it, and thought it a long one. He had never asked himself why the Makers had built so many places.

The gallery was dim. The bright racing walls between the ribs had thinned to a film. In places they had gone altogether, and the open sea stood in the gaps. The hammock-nets hung slack. And all down that kilometre of failing light, in knots of three and four, the Threaded were waiting.

He had thought of them as an order. Jef's monks, the night crew had called them. They were not much like monks. There was a woman of eighty with a face like a walnut, who sat cross-legged in a net and smoked something in a clay tube that made the water round her taste of cloves. There were two brothers from Pearl Stair who finished each other's sentences. There was a man with one arm, and a girl who could not have been twenty, with a harpoon across her knees. They wore suits of every colour, patched and faded. They had nothing in common but the fine pale scars at the temple and the look that Okonta had. It was the look of people who spend a great deal of time alone, listening.

"Every one of us felt it stop," Okonta said quietly. "Where else would we go? It's the only house we've got."

They knew her. They made room. An old man gripped her forearm and held it. Then they looked past her at Wrack, and the talk died.

"That's him," said the woman with the clay tube. "The last one. The one he spent himself on." She blew a thread of clove-water at him. "Well? Can you start it?"

"I don't know yet."

"That's honest, anyway. He'd have said yes and then sworn at it for a week."

They had been arguing for four days, and they were tired of it. He heard the news of the world from them, piece by piece, as he had once heard about Jef from the night crew. Four more holds were dark. Refugees were arriving at holds that could not feed them. Breakers out of the Tangle had sunk three agravar ships in a week, with their crews aboard, and it was said that a new caller led them, a woman from a bombed hold. Halcyon Dredge and Meridian Assurance had jointly announced protective patrols throughout mariner waters. There were gunboats now over holds that had never seen a keel. And over the dark holds there were other ships, white ones, which let down glass bells on chains.

"They took Kelder," a big man said. "My hold. All of it, eleven hundred people. They went up in nets, singing, with a paper in the speaker's hand." He had a grey beard and heavy shoulders, and eyes that looked as though they had been made for laughing and had got out of the way of it. "I was two days late. I saw the wake."

"Corin," Okonta said. Something in her voice made Wrack look at her. "I'm sorry. I'd not heard."

"No. Well. You were at the top of the world, burying him."

"Corin and I kept the Anthem Waters together for twenty years," she told Wrack. "With old Sabel, till the Slick took her. He taught me to fly in kelp. Then the survey boats got thick on the Shoulder, and he went east to see to them." She was still looking at the big man. "Where did they take them?"

"A shore. Nobody will tell me which." Corin rubbed his face with a broad hand. "My sister's there. And her girls."

Nobody spoke.

"And we're to do what about it?" said the girl with the harpoon. "That's what I've been asking for four days. There's forty-one of us in this hall. There were two hundred when my teacher took the Thread. He's gone. There'll be no more made. What's the rule for, with nobody to say it to?"

"You said it," said Okonta. "I was there. I don't remember there being a clause about him staying alive."

"That's easy to say when you've got the new one riding behind you."

It went on like that. Wrack left them to it, and went down through the floor to look at the heart.

---

The visitor came that evening.

Wrack felt him before anyone spoke. He was lying in the workings with his hand on a wheel, and the hull above him rang, faintly, as a glass rings when another is set down beside it. A needle had come into a cradle. It was an old pattern.

When he came up through the floor, the whole gallery had turned to face the stern.

The grey needle lay in the last cradle of the row, the one that had held nothing but silt. The man who had ridden it stood beside it, in his dark suit and his helmet of clear glass. He had one gloved hand on the rib where the square name was cut. He stood easily. He might have been waiting for a lift.

"I'm told that somebody tried to scratch this out," he said. The voice came from a grille at his throat, flat and clear in the still water. "I'm glad they gave it up. It's the only place on Ovara where my name has ever been written down for nothing." He looked along the row of them. "Some of you have been told a story about a Threaded who went up to the islands. I am he. My name is Anselm Rook. I have carried the Thread for forty-nine years. That is longer than any of you, and I think longer than any two of you together. I said the rule to him on a step, as you did. I've come because I was called."

"Nobody called you," Okonta said.

"The Dragon stopped. If that isn't a call, I don't know what would be." He inclined his head to her, quite courteously. "You're Okonta. You keep the water between Rime and the Slick, and you've turned back nineteen of my survey boats. I've read your name more often than you'd care for."

"You broke the rule."

"I broke a third of it. I own a great deal that I can't carry. I don't deny it." He let his hand fall from the rib. "I'd say I've kept the first part better than anyone in this hall. There are five houses up there with guns. I have held them off each other's throats for thirty years, with paper. Any one of them would have had engines in your forests long ago, if the other four had let it. I'm the reason they didn't. I don't ask to be thanked."

"What do you ask?" said the woman with the clay tube.

"I ask you to look at where you are." He spread his hands, a little, to take in the dim hall and the thinning walls. "He's dead. I'm sorry for it. I am sorrier than you'll believe. The Lantern was struck, I hear, by a dart of his own making, from his own war. He left a great many sharp things lying about the world. And now there are no more of you to come. You know it. In forty years the last Threaded will be an old woman on a slow needle, and there'll be nobody left to hear her go by."

No one answered him.

"The holds are going dark. You've seen it. You carry the people out. I have somewhere to carry them to. I've air, and lamps, and clean water in tanks. I've eleven thousand in already, and I shall have a hundred thousand by the spring. I need people who know the bottom. Pilots. Guides. People a frightened hold will trust, to bring it in gently, without anyone being hurt." He paused. "I pay. I don't mean scrip. I pay in the thing nobody up there can buy. Ground. A piece of it, deeded to you, and to your kin after you. And your own people come first into the refuges, and are housed together. I know where every one of them is."

Wrack saw Corin's head come up.

"*Own nothing you can't carry*," Rook said gently. "It was a good rule. It was a rule for a world with him in it."

"You sent that dart," Wrack said.

He had not meant to say it. It came out of him too loud, and the gallery gave it back.

Rook turned the glass helmet toward him. For a moment he looked at Wrack as he had on the step of Kettle House, steadily, like a man checking a figure.

"I have said what I know about the dart," he told the hall.

"I was there. So was she. It was driven from an old-pattern hull at the ice edge."

"Did you see the hull?" Rook asked Okonta.

She was silent a moment. "I heard it."

"She heard it. Eighty kilometres off, through ice, on the worst night of her life." He did not raise his voice. "I don't ask anyone to take my word against hers. I ask you to think about who has a place for your people and who has not. I'll be at Greywatch Stone until the turn of the watch tomorrow. After that I've work to do."

He went back to the cradle. Then, as though it were an afterthought, he turned to Wrack again. The grille clicked, and the voice that came from it was lower. It was meant for one man.

"I aimed at a machine," Anselm Rook said. "He chose to go down to it. He always did choose the machine." He looked at the open floor, and at the tool in Wrack's hand. "Mend this one, if you can. I should like to see it run. And my offer stands. It will always stand. You've only to come to any Meridian door and say that the chairman is expecting you."

The grey needle slid out of its cradle and was gone.

Nobody moved for some time. Then, far down the row, there was a small sharp click. It was a needle leaving its cradle. A moment later there was another. They went one by one, without a word, and nobody tried to stop them. It sounded like a man cracking his knuckles. Wrack counted fourteen.

Corin's was not among them. He was still sitting in his net with his big hands hanging between his knees. He was looking at nothing.

---

They came for him in the dead of the night watch.

He was under the floor, on his back, with a strand of lampweed tied to his wrist. He had found the patches by then. They lay on the heart in layers, nine hundred years of them, neat and stubborn and ingenious. He was so tired that the wheels swam. He had not properly slept since the heart-chamber, and the cold of that place was still in his feet.

Bother saved him the first time. The hound had gone to sleep across the open hatch, and he woke the whole gallery. He did not bark. He screamed. Wrack came up through the floor into a tangle of flippers and bubbles. Something hit him in the ribs, harder than he had ever been hit.

It was the prow of a needle. He doubled over it. A second struck him behind the knees.

There were three of them. They did not ride as Okonta had ridden when she had him behind her. They rode as she had ridden at the eel. The needles came in from three sides, and jinked, and were somewhere else. He reached for the sea. *Please—*

He had not finished the word before he was hit again.

That was what he remembered afterwards, with the most shame. He had lifted a ship. He had held three hundred pools still for nine hours. And he could not get to the end of a thought. Each asking took him a breath, and in a breath a needle crossed the hall. He got one out, a great blind shove of water. It flung the smallest of them against a rib. It also tore six hammock-nets from their moorings, with people sleeping in them, and he heard Sefa cry out. He flinched from it. He let go.

A net came down over him in that moment. It was steel, the fine heavy mesh the wreckers used. A line snapped taut. Then the gallery was gone, and the sea was going past him in a roar.

He was being towed. He was on his face in the wake of a needle at full speed, wrapped to the neck in wire, and the water came at him so hard that he could not see or think. He could not have said *please* to save his life. Twice he managed to gather himself and ask the sea for a wall in front of them. Twice it rose, and the needle simply was not where the wall was. He had seen Okonta do that. He had thought it wonderful.

"Lie still!" a voice shouted back at him. It was Corin's. It was not an angry voice. "Lie still, you'll only cut yourself! Nobody's going to hurt you! He wants you whole!"

The kelp went by in a gold smear. Then it went by black.

Then it was not there at all.

He knew the silence before he saw the water. It closed over his ears like a hand. The sea was the colour of strong tea. Shreds hung in it that neither rose nor sank. He reached, and there was no door and no house, and he remembered that he had already been taught this once.

Corin had brought him into the Slick, where he could not ask for anything.

*Somebody clever*, he thought. *He's been thirty years among callers. He knows just where we stop.* He lay in the net like a fish, and was towed.

He did not see Okonta come. He saw only that the needle ahead of him lurched, and that something was drifting down across their path, small and pale, in a cloud. It was dried fish. It was a whole pouch of it, torn open and flung. The scent of it went out through the dead water like blood.

The silt below them moved.

The eels came up all together. There were five of them, six, blind and slimed and as long as the needles. The boy who had been thrown against the rib was flying rearguard. They took him first. One had the tail of his needle, and another struck him across the body and carried him clean off it. He went down in a knot of them with his mouth open.

"*Tam!*"

The tow-line went slack. Corin had cut it. He did not hesitate, and Wrack remembered that afterwards too. He let go of what he had been sent for, and stood his needle on its nose, and went down into the eels after the boy.

An arm hooked through the mesh. Okonta dragged the net up onto her hull with one hand, and steered with nothing, as she always did. The steel had opened her forearm from wrist to elbow, and the blood was going back along the needle in a ribbon. The eels would have that too, in a moment.

"Okonta!" Corin had the boy across his cradle. The third rider, a scarred woman on a dart, was circling over him with a lance out. He was shouting across forty metres of dead brown water, and his voice cracked. "It's wages! That's all it is! Thirty years! What have you got? A needle and a bad back!"

"I've got the rule!"

"It was said to a dead man!"

"I said it," Okonta shouted. "I wasn't dead!"

He looked at her a moment longer. Then he turned east, with the boy held against him and the dart flying cover. The brown fog took them.

She ran for clean water with an eel on her wake the whole way.

---

Sefa stitched the arm. She did it with intended water, in the gallery, with her lip between her teeth, while twenty-seven Threaded pretended not to watch. It was the first time she had healed anyone who was not from Low Anthem. The wound closed crooked. Okonta looked at it and said that it would do, and that she had never much cared for that arm.

Wrack sat against a rib with a blanket of somebody's round him. His own ribs were cracked. Sefa had seen to those first, and had told him that he was not to be let out by himself.

"I couldn't do anything," he said.

"No," said Okonta.

"I lifted a ship."

"You lifted a ship that wasn't trying to stop you." She flexed her fingers, and winced. "I've been wondering when you'd find that out. I'd hoped it would be cheaper. You're the strongest thing in the sea, and three tired people carried you off like a sack of cake. Strength isn't fighting. Any herder's child could have told you so."

"Then what is?"

"Being ready." She considered him. "I've no gift. But I've stood beside the Mighty of six holds when it came to blows, and I've watched how the good ones do it. They don't call in a fight. There isn't time. A fight is over before you've finished saying please." She touched the scar at her temple. "They do their asking beforehand, when it's quiet. They ask her to stand ready about them. A skin of water, an arm's length out, that's already theirs. Already listening. They hold it there all day, as you'd carry a shield. And then, when something comes, they've only to lean."

"As you do on the needle."

"As I do on the needle." She got up, stiffly. "You can Hold. I've seen you hold a lake. Now you'll hold one arm's length of sea, and you'll hold it while you're eating and while you're asleep, and I shall come at you with this." She laid her good hand on the needle's prow. "Every time I find it thin, you'll get a rib like the ones you've got. We'll start when the girl says you can stand."

"He can stand now," Sefa said. "He just doesn't want to."

He stood.

She was a hard teacher. She was harder than Jef, and she did not sit on a stool. In the days that followed she came at him out of the dark at every hour, and from every side. For the first two days he was struck so often that Sefa stopped apologising for the bruises and began simply to count them. On the third day the needle's prow met something an arm's length from his shoulder. It slid off, as off wet glass. Okonta went past him and round and came back, and looked at him without expression.

"Again," she said.

---

In between, he mended the Dragon.

It had lost two thirds of its water by then. He swam the length of it, a kilometre, with his hand trailing along the ribs. He could feel the great heart through his palm. It was not labouring now. It was still.

The heart of the Dragon lay amidships, under the gallery floor. It was as large as the air-hearts of Deep Chorus and of no kind he had seen. It was not a shell that opened and closed. It was a wheel within a wheel within a wheel, each one ringed with channels finer than hairs. When it turned it took in the plain sea and gave out the bright bound water that was the Dragon's body. He lay down in its workings and looked at it for a long time.

There was nobody to tell him how. Jef had patched this thing for nine hundred years. Under the patches the wheels were simply worn out. They could not turn as they had been made to turn. There was not enough of them left.

He lay there for two days and a night. Sefa came, and tied a fresh strand of lampweed round his wrist, and went away. The Threaded who had stayed came down by ones and twos, and held lamps for him, and passed him what he pointed at. The old woman with the clay tube sat by the hatch for a whole watch.

"He had us carry for him as well," she said. "He never said thank you either. Don't start. I shouldn't know where to look."

He thought about a coupling seated backwards. He thought of an old man saying *I've been meaning to try that for two hundred years.* And he thought, clearly, in Jef's voice: *You'll be better than me at seeing where to cut.*

He stopped trying to mend the heart as it had been made. He took apart a thing that had run without fault for three thousand years. He laid the pieces out on the gallery floor, in order, and twenty-seven Threaded stood round them in a silence like the silence at a graveside. Then he put it together again differently. He left out one wheel of the three altogether. He turned another end for end. It would be a smaller heart, and a slower one, and it would run on less.

Then he asked the sea, as politely as he knew how, to come back into the ribs.

For a long time nothing happened. Then he heard a note, so low that he thought he had imagined it. The film between the nearest ribs thickened, and brightened, and began to pour.

"It's slower," Okonta said, when the Dragon was moving.

"Four days to the Tip, not three. It'll last."

The third wheel lay on the floor of the gallery where he had left it. It was as wide across as a table, and worn as thin as a shell. Nobody liked to touch it. In the end the girl with the harpoon lashed it to a rib with cord, as one might hang up a dead man's coat.

By morning there were nineteen needles in the cradles. The rest had gone in the night. Some had gone home to their own holds. One or two, Okonta thought, had gone east. She did not say after whom.

The young man from Fallow was among those who went. He was the last Jef had made before Wrack, six years ago, and the scars at his temples had hardly faded. He had held a lamp for most of a night without being asked.

"I'm going to my mother," he said. He would not meet Okonta's eye. "Fallow's lamps are going. Somebody ought to be there if the white ships come. I'm sorry. I'd have followed him anywhere."

"Then go and keep that water," Okonta said. "It's in the rule."

When he had gone, Wrack sat for a while with his hand on the rib beside him, feeling the new, slower pulse of the thing he had rebuilt.

"He wanted it mended," he said.

"Who?"

"Rook. He said so. *I should like to see it run.*" He frowned. "He can't mend anything. He told Jef so himself. So he sent three people to carry me off, and then he asked me, quite politely, to mend the Dragon first."

"He wanted you both, I should think. One or the other, as it fell out." But she was frowning too. "What would a man who owns the islands want with a road that only goes round the sea?"

"I don't know."

Sefa sat between them with her arms round Bother, staring out through the bound water at the forest sliding away beneath. She had never been on the Dragon. She had told them that she was coming. She said that they would need a healer. She said that Wrack in particular needed somebody to tell him when he was shouting. Elder Vey, of all people, had settled it. A hold that had been saved by a girl of sixteen, she said, could hardly tell that girl where she might not go, and she would send word to the mother herself.

"He held all this up," Wrack said. "One old man with a basket of crabs."

"Yes," said Okonta. She was looking down the gallery, at nineteen needles in a row built for two hundred.

The Dragon ran south.

## Chapter 7. The Door

It was four days to the bottom of the world, and Sefa watched all of it.

She had never been further from Low Anthem than Deep Chorus. She hung at the bright wall of the gallery, with Bother beside her, and would not come away to eat. Wrack sat with her when he was not asleep. He had been ordered to sleep; she had taken the grey band off her own arm and shaken it at him. He found that he could look at things properly, for the first time since the Pale, with someone beside him who had never seen them either.

Okonta did not let him off. She came at him in the gallery morning and evening, with the needle or with the flat of her hand. By the third day he was carrying his arm's length of sea without thinking about it, as a man carries a coat. Sefa said that it made him look pleased with himself, and that she had preferred him bruised.

For half a day it was the forest. Then, toward evening, the kelp failed and the water turned warm and cloudy, and the Dragon tilted its long head and went down.

"The Girdle," Okonta said. She had come to stand behind them. "The middle of the world. All the islands are up there, and all the islanders, and the storms. The Dragon goes under it. Deep, a kilometre down, along trenches the Makers must have known. It's never once come up in these waters. There's not a calling stone between the two Shoulders." She folded her arms. "He never came here either. Nor she. They'd ride through underneath, asleep, as you'd ride through a bad part of a town. I told him so more than once."

They went down into the brown dark. It was the only stretch of the circuit, Okonta said, where there was nothing to see. She was wrong. There was the snow.

It fell past the walls without end. It drifted down out of the murk overhead, grey and fine. There were flakes, and clots, and now and then things with a shape. A bottle. A shoe. A sheet of something that turned over slowly, flashing a printed face. The whole sea was full of it, sinking. It lay on the floor of the trench below them in drifts that the Dragon's wake stirred up like ash.

"What is it?" Sefa whispered.

"It's what forty million people throw away," Okonta said. "That's a day's worth. It falls like that all the time."

Sefa did not say anything. After a while she put her hand flat on the bound water, as if she could stop it.

Wrack watched it too. He had a strange cold feeling. He knew the rate of that fall. If someone had asked him, he could have given a figure.

Once they passed under a storm. He felt it through the Thread before he saw it, as a great bruised trampling overhead. The surface a kilometre above them was churned to a dull pewter, and lightning went across it like cracks in a plate. Once a pattern of dark squares slid over, each as long as the Dragon's head. There were the keels of forty ships in line.

"Kessler hulls," Okonta said. "That's a fleet moving."

Then the trench floor rose, and the water cleared, and on the third morning they came up into light.

Sefa cried out. Wrack shut his eyes against it and opened them again.

They were running a bare ten metres under the surface, over white sand, in water so clear it was hard to believe there was any. The sun came straight down through it. The sand was ribbed like the roof of a mouth and it went on to the edge of sight in every direction. Shadows of small clouds moved across it. There were meadows of dark green grass, combed flat by the current, and herds of silverbacks grazed over them in thousands. And there were reefs. They stood up out of the sand in thickets, as clear as ice, branching like bare trees, and the sunlight broke in them into every colour there is. As the Dragon passed, its wake moved through them, and they rang.

"Glass coral," Okonta said. "Don't ever touch it. It'll take your hand off." She was nearly smiling. "The Bright Shelves. Everybody does that, the first time."

Far off to the east something stood up out of the sea. It was a cluster of slender white towers, nine of them. They rose from the sand through the surface and on into the sky, and the water round their feet was dotted with boats.

"Nine Spires. Fifty thousand people. The biggest hold there is." She sniffed. "They trade with Driftfolk. They wear agravar cloth. They've got opinions about everything."

Sefa watched it out of sight. "It's so *open*," she said. "Where do they go when a barge comes?"

"The barges don't come. Too shallow, too many eyes. They've never netted a day in their lives." Okonta shrugged. "It's why the north thinks they're soft. And why they think the north's sour."

On the fourth day the sand fell away, and the light with it. The water went dark again, and began to grow warm.

---

The sea at the bottom of the world steamed.

They came up through it on the last morning into a fog so thick that when Wrack stepped off onto the landing he could not see the Dragon's head from its neck. It went by him at a walk, rib after rib, each one looming out of the white and gone. The air was hot and wet, and smelled of struck matches.

The water was black. It was not the tea-coloured dead black of the Slick. It was the black of deep clear water over dark stone. Far down, red light pulsed slowly, like embers breathed on.

"The Smokers," Okonta said. Her voice was low. Everything here seemed to want a low voice. "Chimneys. The sea floor's on fire here, and has been since before the Makers."

He put his face under and looked. There were fields of stone towers down there, hundreds of them, crooked and tall. Each poured out a plume of black water that glowed dull red at its root. Round their feet grew gardens. They were tubes as thick as a man's leg and as tall as trees, blood red, swaying together though there was no current. Between them the rock was furred with a moss the colour of rust. It was like a forest in a fever. Pale crabs walked in it, and blind white fish.

Nothing sang. He realised that he had not heard a sound he had not made himself since the Dragon's note died away. It was not the silence of the Slick. That had been an absence. This was the silence of a room where someone is listening.

Ember House stood on an islet of black sand, with hot springs smoking among the rocks. It was the twin of Kettle House, down to the washing line. The same low stone walls, the same slab roof. There was a garden in the shallows, and a step with a kettle on it. Where the other house had its workshop, this one had a long still-room. He could see through its window the shelves of jars and bottles and bunches of dried growths hung from the rafters.

The door was open. On the table inside were two cups. One of them was clean, and turned upside down.

Behind the house, where the largest spring ran down into the sea, somebody had built a cairn of black stones. A leather apron was folded on top of it, held down with a rock.

Okonta stood in front of it for a long time with her hand on her temple. Sefa, who had never met him, knelt and laid a strand of lampweed from her own wrist among the stones.

"She's not here," Sefa said at last.

"She's at the Hush." Okonta sat down heavily on the step. "This is as far as I go. You too, girl. The rays don't care for visitors. If they want him, they'll come for him."

They came at dusk.

He was standing at the water's edge. The fog had thinned, and the low sun made a red road across the sea. The water in front of him rose slightly, as the ground rises over a root, and a shape the size of a ship slid beneath it. He saw a wing, pale underneath, and a long tail with a spine on it as long as his body. Then it was beside the rocks and quite still. An eye like a dark lamp looked up at him through a hand's depth of water, and he understood that he was meant to go with it.

"Wrack," Sefa said behind him. Her voice was small.

"It's all right," he said. He did not know whether it was.

He went down holding on to nothing. The ray moved, and the water it moved carried him.

---

They brought him into a place of open dark, far from the Smokers, where the water was warm and there was no bottom. Hundreds of them were there. They were turning, slowly, wing behind wing, in a ring so wide that he could not see across it. It was the Wheel. Maelis had named it to him once. It had been turning before the Makers built their first city, and it had never stopped.

He was set at the centre of it, like a stone in the middle of a pond. They turned around him. And then, without any words at all, they began to show him.

It was not like seeing. It was like remembering something he had not been there for.

He saw the sea as it had been.

There were forests that ran from Tip to Tip, unbroken. The water was so full of life that it was never dark. He saw the Makers' cities, lit, under clean water, with children swimming between the towers. He saw the Dragon new, and a hundred like it, a web of bright roads round the world. He saw the Sinking. It was not the wave he had imagined. It was slow, a long patient drowning that took generations. Some people walked down into the water as it rose, and some climbed away from it. He saw two sisters part on a shore, one going each way.

He saw the first mariner who was ever answered. She was a woman kneeling in a kelp bed with a dying child in her arms, asking and asking and asking. He saw the sea turn toward her, as a sleeper turns toward a voice.

He saw a war. Domes cracked like eggs, and the water was full of the dead. In the middle of it he saw a boy of about twenty, small and dark-eyed, standing in the ruins of a hold with a tool in his hand and his mouth open.

He saw a black sea under a storm, with cases and cork floats and coats drifting on it. One boy was among them, alone, in a ferryman's jacket far too big for him. A wing rose under the boy and lifted him.

Then he saw the sea as it was.

They showed him everything. The Slick was there, and eleven other places like it that he had not known existed. He saw the black bitterwrack and the eels. He saw the snow falling under the Girdle. He saw a hold with its lamps out and its people sitting in the dark. And he saw, with a clarity that was almost gentle, a sheet of paper. It was a chart. A hand was drawing a line across it in red, and the line passed over a small dome in a golden forest. The hand signed its name at the bottom.

He could not read the name. He knew the hand. He had been looking at it for months.

He did not know how long it went on. When it stopped, the largest of them was hanging directly before him. It was older than the others, and scarred, and one wing was notched. One eye, dark and ancient and entirely without anger, was level with his face. It was waiting. He understood that the showing had been a question. They wanted to know what he was going to do with the answer.

He had nothing to give it except the truth.

*I want to know,* he thought. *Even that. I want to know all of it.*

The eye regarded him a moment longer. Then the ring opened. He saw a faint warm glow far below.

---

The Hush was built of black stone, and it had no lamps.

It stood at the bottom of the southern Well. He had expected the cold and weight of the northern one. But the water grew warmer as he sank, and softer, until it was like sinking into sleep. The hospital was a single great building, low and wide. It was made of the rock of the Well floor, and it was not Maker work at all. Human hands had built it, long ago, block on block. The warmth of the earth came up through its floors.

Nobody in it spoke. He understood that at the threshold, without being told. Healers in grey moved through the dark halls by touch, with a hand on the wall. Patients floated in pools that he could feel but not see. Some of them, he came to understand, were not sick in the body. They were people who had seen too much, or lost too much, or who could not stop. The Lantern mended what was broken. The Hush was where you came when nothing was broken and you still could not go on. The dark and the silence were the medicine, and the warm water, and somebody's hand.

One of the healers took him by the sleeve. She led him through the quiet to a small room where a single ember of light burned in a dish.

Maelis sat beside it. She had cut off her hair.

She let him sit. For a long time neither of them said anything. He thought that she looked like a house with one room shut up.

"He's in the hot spring behind the house," she said at last, very quietly. Even she kept her voice down here. "You'll have seen. He liked that spring. He'd sit in it for hours and complain that it was too hot." She folded her hands. "They weighed you."

"Yes."

"They don't often trouble to." On the floor between them was a small clay bottle, stopped with wax. "You know what this is. It's venom, and heat-moss from the Smokers, and water I've sat with for nine days. And one other thing, that isn't mine. They've been keeping it for you. You gave it to the sea yourself, all at once, the night you went into the water. The sea doesn't lose what it's given."

He looked at the bottle.

"*He has to ask for his memory himself,*" he said.

"Yes. He was very firm about it. He said a man who is handed his past will spend his life wishing he could hand it back." Her eyes rested on him. "I ask everyone the same question. What will you do with the life you get back?"

"I think I did something," he said. "I've felt it since I woke up in that kelp. I knew about the Slick before I saw it. I knew how fast the snow falls under the Girdle. The rays showed me a hand, drawing a line." He made himself go on. "I think when I drink that, I'm going to find out I'm the reason for the scars on Ione's arms."

"Possibly. That isn't what I asked."

He thought of Elder Oro under the lamp. He thought of Davin showing him a gate latch for the fourth time, and of Hesper's shut face. He thought of the Dragon's tired heart. He thought of three thousand people lying still in the green dark, and a girl of sixteen in a healer's band saying *eleven this week*.

"Whatever I did," he said, "I'll go back and face it. In front of everyone. And then I'll spend whatever I've got left fixing it."

She gave him the bottle.

It tasted of nothing. Then it came back all at once.

---

His name was Ilan Voss.

He had been born in Saltgate, the free port, in a tenement on a canal called the Sluice. His father, Tomas, was a dock engineer, a big gentle man with oil in the creases of his hands. He could mend any crane on the waterfront, and he read his son the manuals at bedtime because they were the only books in the house.

His mother's name was Nerys, and she was a mariner.

She had come up through the locks one evening when she was nineteen, to see the lights. She had seen a young man mending a winch. She had stayed for him. She stayed twenty years, in four rooms on the fourth floor, drying out slowly. She wore high collars and long sleeves. She kept a basin of salt water under the kitchen table and sat with her feet in it in the evenings, humming songs with no words. She never told anyone what she was. In Saltgate a mariner was a curiosity. Anywhere else on the islands she would have been a specimen. She taught her son the names of kelp from memory. She taught him to hide.

*High collars. Never swim where people can see. And never, ever let the water move when you're angry, Ilan. Promise me.*

He had been seven. He had made the bathwater stand up in a fist because he did not want to get out of it. He remembered her face. He had promised.

She died when he was fifteen, of nothing a doctor could name. Her skin had gone like paper. His father followed within the year. Ilan buried the collars and the basin and the songs, and buried the promise so deep that he forgot what it had been about. And he climbed.

He was very good. He had his father's hands, and something else that he never looked at, which told him where things were under the water.

The week after his father's funeral a man came to the school on the Sluice. He was from the Meridian Scholarship, which every dock child had heard of and none had ever won. He set out a tray of objects on the master's desk, and had the top form come up one at a time to handle them. There was a brass sextant, and a slide rule, and a lump of ore. There was a short grey rod of something smooth that was neither glass nor stone. Ilan picked it up because it was the only thing on the tray he did not know. It was warm. For a moment, far down inside it, there was a light like a struck match.

He put it down quickly. The man from the Scholarship had not been looking at the rod. He had been looking at Ilan's hands.

He met the chairman a month later, in Concord, in a room with no windows. He had expected a banker. He found a broad, square, quiet man of fifty in a high collar, who gave him fish and asked what he thought of the tide tables. He listened to the answer for an hour. *I am going to pay for you,* Anselm Rook said at the end of it. *School, and Keelhaven, and whatever comes after. I shall want nothing back but your attention. Do you understand what an investment is?* Ilan said that he did. He was sixteen, and nobody else on Ovara wanted him for anything.

They dined together once a year after that, on the day the paper was signed, for twenty-two years. Rook chose the fish. He chose Keelhaven, and the woman who would teach Ilan hulls. He chose Halcyon, because Halcyon dug, and a man who could feel the sea floor ought to be where the digging was. He never raised his voice. He never forgot a thing he was told. He came to the launch of Ilan's first survey ship and stood at the back. Ilan had loved his father. He had not known, until now, how badly he had gone on needing one.

He was a surveyor at twenty-two. At thirty-eight Ilan Voss was Chief Surveyor of Halcyon Dredge & Mineral, the youngest in its history. He had an office on the rim at Port Halcyon and a view over the ash. He knew the sea floor of the Girdle and both Shoulders better than any agravar living. People said that he could look at a blank chart and tell you the depth.

He planned the dumping routes. It was a small part of the work, a matter of an afternoon each quarter. Tonnage came in from the refineries, and had to go somewhere. He chose deep water in the north, over ground that Halcyon's own old surveys marked as *native settlement, unconfirmed*. He chose it because the people down there, if they existed, had no way to complain. He drew the lines in red. He signed them. He never went to see. And he never once let himself wonder how he knew, so exactly, where the holds were.

Then he found the city.

It lay under a sill in the open Girdle, beneath forty metres of silt, entire. He had felt it there for years, without knowing what he felt. Rook had asked for the dig. He had asked gently, over the fish, six years running: *that sill in the open Girdle, the one you say feels wrong. Humour me.* Ilan led it himself. He was the first to walk into its central hall, in a hard suit, with a lamp, and it woke. Lamps came on down every street. The walls turned to light. They showed him the people who had built it, and they did not have mariner faces or agravar faces. They had both. Long limbs and broad hands, and large dark eyes in weathered skin. One people. He stood in the light, and the water in the flooded hall rose around his gloves and moved, for the first time since he was seven. And he understood what his mother had been. He understood what he had been drawing lines across for fifteen years.

The city showed the men who came in behind him something else. There was an engine in its lowest vault. The Makers had sealed it there themselves, behind three doors. It could boil a sea.

He tried to shut the dig down. He went first to the one man who would understand. He told Rook everything, on a closed line from the survey ship. He told him about the faces on the walls, and his mother, and the lines on the charts. He said that all of it must stop, and that he meant to tell the islands why. There was a long silence on the line. *I see,* Rook said. *Stay where you are, Ilan. I'll send someone.*

He sent Adler Crane. Crane put a cable tie round Ilan's wrists in the control hall. He was Ilan's own chief of security. He had eaten at Ilan's table a hundred times and had taught him to play cards. He walked him to the rail. It was night, and the water was deep, and no one was looking. Crane said he was sorry, and seemed to mean it. *It's the chairman's order,* he said. *I asked him twice.*

And as he sank, the boy who had promised never to let the water move had at last, in terror, let go of everything. He let go of his name, and his guilt, and his whole life, in a single breath. The sea took what it was given. It carried him north, nearly two thousand kilometres. It laid him down in a kelp bed, under a route he had drawn himself, for a girl and a tide hound to find.

---

He was on the floor of the little room. He did not know when he had got there. He was making a noise. A healer had come to the door, and Maelis had sent her away with a look.

"I did it," he said. "Low Anthem. All of them. The burns. Davin. It was a line on a chart. I never even went to *see*."

"Yes."

"He should have had a better successor."

"Probably."

He looked up. She had not moved. There was no softness in her face, but there was no disgust either.

"And it was him," Ilan said. "On the step. Anselm Rook. He ate soup and looked at me, and I didn't know him. He'd had me put over the side three months before."

"Yes."

"You knew whose I was. Both of you."

"He told us that much himself, by coming." She folded her hands. "I said I would tell you about him. I was seventeen when the rays brought him to the Hush. He was fourteen. A ferry had gone down in the Belt with nine hundred people in it, and he was the only one they carried out. I don't know why him. He didn't speak for a year. He sat in the dark with his hand on the wall. Then he began to sweep. I taught him our script. He taught me a game from the islands, played with stones." Her voice did not change. "He was the cleverest person I had ever met, and the most afraid. He had seen how little there is to stand on. He never got over it. When Jef came south and found a boy sweeping a hospital, he thought the sea had sent him his own self to do over. He took Anselm north that same season. Six years later he came south again, to mend the warm-stones, and took me."

"And he couldn't learn it."

"He learned everything but the one thing. He learned where the holds lie, and that the machines were failing, and that the elders hid it. He learned there had been a city. He wanted Jef to build. Engines, pumps, anything, for the islands, so that there would be ground enough and nobody need be afraid again. Jef had sworn never to build, and would not tell him why. They fought about it for a year. Then Anselm took his needle and went up." She looked at the ember. "Jef never spoke of him as a failure. He spoke of him as a thing he had made badly."

"Why wouldn't he tell him?"

"Because he was ashamed. I'll tell you, since he's past minding. You'll have passed a ruin, coming north. In the wild kelp. Okonta will have told you its name."

"Sounding."

"He was born there. When he was nineteen he came home from the Lantern with eleven years of the walls in his head. He was the cleverest boy in the world, and his hold was at war with its neighbours over a kelp boundary. He wanted them to be proud of him." She said it evenly. "He built them darts. Maker darts, armed, the first new ones in two thousand years. They were very good. When it was over there were eleven holds fewer than when it started. The last to go was Sounding. The other side had taken two of his darts and learned to ride them."

The ember hissed in its dish.

"He stood in what was left of it and swore he'd never build another thing that couldn't be used to keep someone alive. He kept that for eight hundred and eighty years. It didn't make him good. He said so. He said it made him useful, and that useful would have to do." She rose. "That was the boy the rays showed me, when I asked if I should marry him. Jef built worse than you did, once. Go and fix it."

Outside, in the dark water above the Hush, the rays were turning. Before he left, the old one with the notched wing came close again. It showed him a single picture: a raft-town, somewhere in a storm, and a small fast boat putting out from it.

He understood. They would carry word ahead of him.

He also knew now, to the day, how long Halcyon needed to finish the engine.

---

It was morning when the ray set him down at the black sand. Okonta was asleep against the wall of the house. Sefa was sitting on the step with Bother's head in her lap. She had not slept.

She looked at his face, and stood up.

He had thought, coming up through the warm dark, that he might wait. There would be a better time. She was sixteen, and a long way from home. He had thought it for about as long as it took to rise a hundred metres. Then he had heard Jef say *it doesn't get lighter for being left.*

"I've got my name," he said. "It's Ilan Voss. Sit down, Sefa. I have to tell you what I did."

He told her on the step of a dead man's house, with the springs smoking round them. He told her about the Sluice, and his mother, and the office on the rim. He told her about the chairman and the fish, and who had sat on the step at Kettle House. He told her about the charts. He told her whose hand had drawn the line that ran over Low Anthem, and how long ago, and why. He did not make it short.

She did not interrupt. When he had finished she sat without moving. Then she got up and walked down the black sand into the water, and swam straight out, and was gone. Bother looked from him to the sea, whined, and went after her.

Okonta had woken. He did not know how much she had heard.

"All of it," she said. She had her eyes on the water. "I've been keeping your routes out of my hair for fifteen years. I knew somebody clever drew them. They moved whenever I got in the way." She let out a long breath. "Well. He picked you, and he was no fool. And I said the rule to him, not to you." She stood up, stiffly. "I'll keep it. I'll not pretend I like it this morning."

Sefa was gone for three hours. When she came back her eyes were swollen and her jaw was set. She walked up the sand and stopped in front of him.

"My mother's arms," she said.

"Yes."

"And Davin. And Elder Oro. He *liked* you."

"Yes."

"I should hate you. I've been out there trying to." She wiped her nose on her wrist, furiously. "I hit you in the face a fortnight ago to stop you dying for a lot of strangers. You didn't even know what you'd done then, and you nearly died for them anyway. I can't make it fit." She glared at him. "You said you owed us a dome."

"I do."

"You owe us a lot more than a dome now. I'm coming with you to see you pay it. Every bit. And you tell my mother yourself, to her face. I'm not doing that for you."

"I will."

"Good." She turned her back on him and sat down hard on the step. After a moment Bother, who had no sense of occasion, came and put his wet head on Ilan's foot.

# Part Four: Ashore

## Chapter 8. Saltgate

They rode the Dragon north for a day, as far as the edge of the Bright Shelves, and left it there on the needles. Sefa rode behind Ilan with her arms round his waist. She did not speak to him for the first two hundred kilometres. In the third hundred she said that he leaned too hard into his turns, and he took it for the beginning of a peace.

He was Ilan now. It sat on him like a coat he had not worn for years, familiar and the wrong size. Sefa and Okonta went on calling him Wrack, out of habit or on purpose, and he found that he was glad of it.

The water grew warm and thick. The white sand was left behind. On the fourth day the surface above them began to heave, and a grey twilight came down through it, and Okonta slowed.

"The Belt," she said. "That's the south edge of the storms. From here to the far side it's three hundred kilometres of weather, and every island on Ovara is somewhere in it. I can take a needle under. You can't take a girl who's never seen an agravar into Saltgate on one, and walk her up out of a canal in a Threaded's wake. You'd have every wrecker in the port on you by noon." She looked up. "There should be a boat. The rays said a boat."

There was a boat.

It was painted four different colours, in patches, and it sat on the swell like a duck. When they came up beside it a long weather-beaten face looked over the side. It belonged to a woman of fifty or so, with a pipe in her teeth and a hat tied on with string.

"You'll be the party," she said. "I'm Dace. I'm Driftfolk, out of Lashing. A stingray the size of a customs cutter came up alongside my raft five days ago and put a picture in my head, and I've not been right since." She took the pipe out. "I intend to dine out on it for the rest of my life. Which of you's the one that's worth four years' scrip?"

"I am," said Ilan.

"Thought so. You've the look of a man who's been to an office." She put down a rope ladder. "There's a face like yours on every wall from here to the Crown, with a reward under it. So you'll want a hat. I've brought a hat."

Okonta would not come aboard. She took the second needle in tow behind her own and said that she would be under the keel all the way, and under the city after that, and that if Ilan wanted her he had only to think so loudly. Then she was gone, without a ripple.

The boat was named *Patience* on the bow, *Second Wife* on the stern, and something else again on her papers. She was eleven metres of patched timber with a deckhouse and a mast and an engine that Dace addressed as an equal. Ilan knew her kind. Every harbour on the Girdle had a hundred. They belonged to the people with no corporation, the ones who lived on the water because every scrap of land was owned. They were called the Driftfolk, and they had no island. They had rafts, lashed together in floating towns, which followed the calm places through the storms. They carried what the corporations would not carry, to people the corporations did not serve, and asked no questions of anyone. Halcyon's security manuals classed them as a nuisance. He had signed off on those manuals.

Sefa had never been above the surface for more than a few minutes. She sat in the bows with her feet trailing in the sea, at Dace's order, and stared at the sky. It was low and bruised and moving. Rain came across the water in walls.

"It's so loud," she said. "How does anyone think?"

"Most don't," said Dace cheerfully. "Hold on to something. Here's where it stops being pretty."

---

They crossed the storms by the Eyes.

Ilan had done it a hundred times on company ships, behind glass, with a drink. He had never done it in an open boat. You ran flat out through wind and black rain, with the sea standing up in grey hills on every side, toward a patch of calm that you could not yet see. The calm drifted. There were dozens of them, scattered through the Belt, and they moved on a pattern that took years to learn. The big ships had instruments. The Driftfolk had it by heart, handed down like a song. You reached your Eye, and the wind dropped as though a door had shut. You sat in flat water under a ring of blue sky, with the storm standing all round you like a wall, and you waited for the next Eye to come within reach. If you misjudged it, you were caught between two with nowhere to go. Ships were lost that way every season.

Gale skates went over them in thousands. They were fish, flat and broad and silver, with fins like wings. They threw themselves out of the wave-tops and glided on the wind for a kilometre at a time. Dace read them like a newspaper. When they flew high, she said, the Eye was near. When they turned all together, like a shaken cloth, the wind was about to change. Grey fins followed the boat, six or eight of them. They were belt sharks, Dace said. They followed anything that floated, for the scraps and the dumping. Bother stood with his front flippers on the gunwale and barked at them until he was hoarse.

Sefa lay in the bottom of the boat and was sick. She held Bother's collar, and said that a sea you had to stay on top of was no sea at all.

In the Eyes there was time to talk. It was in the second one, with the boat turning slowly on water like green glass, that she asked him.

"Tell me about them. The islanders. I want to know what I'm going to see." She was sitting up, pale, with a blanket round her. "Not what Mother says. What they're actually like."

He thought about how to begin.

"There are forty million of them, and there's no room," he said. "Start there. Every island on Ovara put together would fit inside the Anthem Waters hundreds of times over. So every bit of ground is owned. You can't be born without being born on somebody's land. Two hundred years ago the trading houses bought out the island governments, one after another, and now there are five that matter. Each one's a company, and it's also a country. If you're born on a Halcyon island, you're Halcyon's. It houses you and schools you, and pays you in its own money. Scrip. You can only spend it in the company's shops. If there's a war, it puts you in a uniform."

"What are they at war about?"

"Land. Each other's. There isn't any left that's spare." He looked at the wall of cloud. "Halcyon digs. Ore, fuel, anything under the sea bed. Kessler-Vane builds ships and guns for all of them. Brightwater sells food and water and medicine, and it's the one everybody likes. Meridian lends the money and insures the ships. Orrin owns the screens and decides what people hear." He rubbed his wrists; it had become a habit. "They're not wicked, Sefa. Most of them. They work twelve hours and go home to two rooms. They like music and fried food, and they love their children. They've been told all their lives that there's nothing down here but fish and a fairy tale. And so what they throw away, they throw in the sea."

"And you told them where."

"I told them where."

She was silent for a while. Dace, at the tiller, was carefully watching the skates.

"Did you like it?" Sefa said. "Being one of them?"

It was not a question he had expected.

"I liked being good at something," he said. "I liked that there was always a next thing to get. I had a flat with a bluewood table. Do you know what bluewood is? It's the last real tree. The table cost more than my father earned in his life." He shook his head. "I don't think I liked anything, particularly. I think I was very busy."

"That's the saddest thing I ever heard," Sefa said, and was sick again.

---

Saltgate came up out of the rain on the fifth evening.

Ilan had forgotten it, and then remembered it. Now he saw it for the first time, all at once.

A hundred sea-stacks stood in the gap between two corporate waters. They were pillars of black rock, some no wider than a house, and they rose sheer out of the swell. On every one of them people had built. They had built up, and then out. They built across from stack to stack on stilts and girders and cables, tier on tier, until the city hung over the water like a mass of nests in a dead tree. And every surface of it flashed and crawled with coloured light. Signs forty metres high sold noodles, loans, cigarettes, and a singer with silver eyelids. Words ran up the sides of buildings and fell down again. Pink and green and hard electric blue lay on the rain and broke on the black water. He smelled smoke and frying oil. He heard ten thousand engines, and music from a hundred doors, and over all of it the horns of ships.

Sefa had her mouth open. Her face was green, then rose, then blue, as the signs changed.

"Where's the dark?" she said. "Where do they put the *dark*?"

"They don't keep any," said Dace.

Under everything the canals ran between the pilings, and at the mouth of each stood a lock. They were great iron gates, streaked with rust, and they stood open to the sea. Men in oilskins and brass badges sat in glass boxes above them, drinking tea.

"Lockkeepers," Dace said. "They're the only law there is here. They own the gates, and they take a toll off everyone who comes through. Halcyon, Driftfolk, your people, all the same rate. They won't take a side for any money. That's why it's a free port. If the Lockkeepers shut the gates, the whole city starves in a week, so nobody's ever cared to annoy them." She raised two fingers to the nearest box. A badge raised two fingers back. "Your folk come in by the locks, love. Right into the middle of town. It's the only place in the world they can."

She looked at Sefa.

"Now you listen. You stay wet. Canals and the Wet Market, no higher. I've a tub in the deckhouse for when we're tied up. A day in the dry up there and you'll be on your back, and two days you'll be in a box."

"I know that."

"She knows that," Dace said to Ilan. "They all know that. I've carried three out on a plank."

His own face looked down at him from a wall at the first landing. It was younger and smoother, in a good collar, over the Halcyon crest. CHIEF SURVEYOR ILAN VOSS. LOST AT SEA. THE COMPANY MOURNS. A REWARD IS OFFERED FOR INFORMATION.

Sefa looked at the wall, and then at him.

"You had a different mouth," she said.

He pulled the hat down.

---

The Wet Market lay in the heart of the city, where six canals met.

It was a basin a hundred metres across, roofed over, high above, by the floors of buildings. The stalls stood round it on three tiers of planking. The top tier was dry. The middle one was awash at high tide. The lowest was a metre under water, and the stallholders there sat in the sea up to their chests, or were mariners and sat in it altogether. Lamps hung on chains. The water was full of light and legs.

Everything was sold here. Agravars in plastic capes sold steel knives and needles and coils of wire, and glass beads, and small cheap radios sealed in wax. Mariners from holds along the Shoulder sold sea-silk by the bolt, and pearls, and smoked silverback. They sold carved shell, and medicines for the skin. Driftfolk sold whatever they had found. There was shouting in three languages. A mariner woman was arguing over the price of a hatchet with a man who had his trousers rolled to the thigh. She kept sinking to her chin between offers, to breathe.

Sefa swam through it with her eyes going from side to side, and Bother swam at her shoulder, appalled.

"They're just *talking* to each other," she said, surfacing beside the boat. "They're cheating each other over fish. That one called that one a thief, and then they both laughed." She pushed her wet hair back. "Nobody told me there was anywhere it was like this."

"There isn't anywhere else," Ilan said. "My mother came up through that lock there, when she was nineteen. To see the lights."

He had not meant to go to the Sluice. His feet took him, that night, as his hands had once gone to a lamp.

It was a back canal in the poor quarter, narrow and dim, with washing strung across it and the tenements leaning together overhead. He knew every door. He knew the noodle stall on the corner, and the smell of it. The tenement was still standing. There was a light in the fourth-floor window, and somebody else's curtain.

He stood on the towpath in the rain and looked up. She had sat at that window with her feet in a basin, humming. She had put her hands on either side of his face when he was seven. He had thought that he was ashamed of where he came from. It had been easier than knowing that he was afraid of what he was.

An old man came out of the noodle stall with a bucket and looked at him twice. Ilan went away again.

---

"The twenty-second floor doesn't have a button," Ilan said.

They were in a noodle shop across the canal from the tower. Dace was eating. Sefa was in the water under the window with her chin on the sill and a bowl balanced in front of her. A thin, nervous young man named Pell sat with them. He was a filing clerk at the port office, and he had not touched his food. Dace had introduced him as a friend with clean hands.

"The Clean Hands," she had explained. "It's a sort of club. Clerks, mostly, and stokers. A few doctors. People who see the tonnage sheets and can't sleep. They jam a valve here and lose a manifest there, and they send word down to the holds when a barge is due." She had patted the young man's shoulder. "They've no leader and no meetings. It's the only reason any of them are alive."

Across the water the Brightwater Wellness Centre rose thirty storeys, white-tiled, softly lit. A fountain played in front of it. Its sign was a blue drop and a smiling sun.

Brightwater was the corporation people liked. It sold bread, and bottled water, and the pills that every agravar child took for the lungs. Its shops were clean and its staff were kind. Its advertisements showed grandmothers. Ilan had dealt with its board for ten years, and they were the only people on Ovara who had ever frightened him. Its chief was a physician named Linnea Marsh. She had begun in a dockside dispensary, and still kept a stethoscope in her desk. She had once explained to him, kindly, over tea, why a medicine that cured in a single dose was a poor product.

"It's listed as plant," he said. "I signed a requisition for that floor once, as a favour between companies. Sixty thousand litres a week of filtered seawater, trucked up in sealed tanks. I thought it was for a pool."

"It's a woman," Pell said. He spoke almost too low to hear. "They've had her eight months. One of ours cleans on twenty-one. She hears the pumps."

"A Wellwalker," Sefa said. She said the word as someone else might say *saint*.

Ilan had heard of them only as a line in a security briefing. Sefa told him the rest, there at the window, in a low fierce voice. The healers of the two great hospitals did not all stay at the bottom of the Wells. Some took a turn at walking the world. They went alone, with nothing. They went from hold to hold, and into the ports, and up onto the beaches of the islands. They healed whoever they met, mariner or agravar, and asked for nothing. They never stayed. To meet one was the luck of a lifetime. Children in Low Anthem played at being one. Ione had met one, once, when she was a girl, and it was why she had become a healer.

"And they caught one," Sefa said. "And put her in a pot."

"They're trying to find out why intended water stops working," Ilan said. "It fades in a few days. If they could make it keep, they could bottle it. It would be worth more than everything else they sell."

"It stops because she stops minding it. Any child knows that."

"They don't believe that. It isn't a thing you can put on an invoice." He turned his cup. "There's no sea in that building. Nothing that's joined to it. It'll be orphan water, all of it, in pipes and tanks. Whatever I can do in there will be weak, and it won't last."

"Then how do we get in?" Dace asked.

He took off the hat.

It was the most frightening thing he had done since he woke up, and the easiest. He walked into the white lobby in a suit that Dace had procured without explanation. There was soft music, and a smell of lemons. He gave his name and his grade and an eleven-digit authority code that his mouth knew better than he did. Then he watched a receptionist's face do what the lieutenant's had done at Greywatch Stone.

Chief Surveyor Voss was not dead. He had been on a confidential assignment. He was here to inspect the plant on twenty-two, on behalf of a partner corporation that was, he let it be understood, extremely displeased. He would not be signing in.

Nobody stops a man of that grade in a building like that. Agravars had built their whole world on it. He heard his own voice, cool and bored and faintly threatening. He knew it well. He had used it for fifteen years.

She was in a tank.

It was a glass cylinder in a white room, with machines round it. The woman in it had been tall once. She floated curled, with tubes in her arms, and her eyes were open. Her grey hair moved in the current of the pumps. The water round her was orphan water. It had been cut off from the sea and filtered until it was dead. He could feel that she had not been answered in eight months. He could feel that she had gone on asking every day.

He put his hand on the glass. Her eyes moved to it, and then to his face. He saw her register what he was. She saw the gift in him, standing in a place like this, in a suit.

"I'm sorry," he said. "For all of it. Can you swim?"

The alarm went when he broke the seals. After that it was not elegant.

There were four guards in the corridor and nothing to call on. He stood with a dying woman in his arms and asked, and there was no one there. Then he thought of the sprinklers. A grid of pipes ran through every ceiling in the tower, full of stale standing water. He reached up and closed his hand round the nearest sprinkler head. He asked again. It answered faintly, like a voice down a bad line. But there was a great deal of it. It came down through the tiles in ropes and sheets. It was enough to fill a corridor to the knee and take the feet from under four men. It held for a few seconds as a slope of water down thirty flights of the emergency stair. It was already fading as they rode it down. He carried the Wellwalker against his chest, and she weighed nothing at all.

They went into the canal from a loading dock as the last of it gave out. Dace's boat was there. Sefa was in the water with her arms up.

---

Her name was Yeva.

She had been walking the world for thirty years when they took her. They had found her on a beach in the Green Terraces, setting a fisherman's leg. She lay for two days in the shadows under the Wet Market, where the sea came in clean on the tide, and let it come back to her. Sefa did not leave her. She fed her broth from a spoon and held her up in the water, and looked at her as Elder Oro had looked at the lamp.

On the third day Yeva opened her eyes properly. She had a long worn face and a dry manner. She looked at Sefa, and at the band on her arm.

"Who gave you that?"

"My hold. On the road. There wasn't anyone else."

"Hm. You've good hands. You grip too hard." She turned her head and looked at Ilan. "And you call like a man shifting furniture."

"I've been told."

"I felt you, in that corridor. You went through the pipes like a bailiff." She sat up, slowly, against a piling. "Give me your hand. No, in the water."

A market porter had dropped a crate on Dace's foot that morning, and the foot was the size of a melon. Dace sat on the edge of the planking with it hanging in the canal, smoking, and said she did not mind being practised on.

"You know how to ask the sea to move," Yeva said. "Now you'll ask it to care. It's the same asking. You're simply not allowed to want anything for yourself while you do it. Not even to be good at it. Go on."

He stood waist deep in the canal with a cupful of water between his palms. For an hour he failed.

He knew why. He wanted very badly to be a man who healed instead of a man who burned. He wanted it so much that the water could feel nothing else in him. Yeva watched him fail without comment. Sefa, behind her, was trying not to tell him what he was doing wrong, and nearly bursting with it.

Then Dace shifted, and swore at her foot, in real pain. He stopped thinking about himself and thought about the foot. He thought about the small crushed bones in it, and how it must throb.

The water went warm in his hands.

"There," Yeva said. "That's all it is. That's all it ever is. You'll spend the rest of your life trying to get out of your own way."

He learned to mend, to purge and to ease. Yeva taught him the three of them in the nine days she could spare. She taught Sefa at the same time, and Sefa learned twice as fast. She said frankly that he would never be delicate. She said also that she had not felt strength like it outside the Wells.

"Purging is your one," she said. They were working on a stoker's lungs, black from twenty years of refinery smoke. Ilan had his hands on the man's back, and the foulness was coming up out of him into the water like ink. "Poison, rot, filth. You've a feeling for where it sits. I'd wonder why, if I didn't already know who you were."

He looked up.

"The sea tells me things too," Yeva said. "It told me in that tank who'd signed for the water." She let that land. "I'm not going to forgive you. It isn't mine to do. But I'll tell you what I tell every healer, and you'll remember it. You can't mend anything you won't look at."

---

The clinic was Sefa's idea.

She said that if agravars thought mariners were a story, somebody had better show them otherwise. She said that she had not come all this way to hide under a market. And she said, when Dace objected, that Brightwater had kept a Wellwalker in a jar for eight months to find out how to sell what she did. The quickest way to spoil that was to give it away under their noses.

Yeva looked at her for some time. "I've been thirty years on the road," she said, "and I never thought of sitting still in one place and letting them come to me. Help me up."

They set it up on the lowest tier of the Wet Market, where the stalls stand half in the water and half out. It was a plank table and two basins and a girl of sixteen. Behind her stood a Wellwalker out of a legend. She had been starved for eight months, and she was upright through pure obstinacy.

For the first morning nobody came. People looked, and walked on.

In the afternoon a dock porter came down the steps with a child in his arms. He was a big slow man with a broken nose. The child was four or five, and limp, and her breath rattled. The company clinic had priced the fever at three months' scrip. He did not have three months' scrip. He stood at the edge of the planking and did not seem to know how to ask.

Sefa held out her arms. He looked at her wet hair and her dark eyes for a long moment, and then he put his daughter into them.

She laid the child in the basin and put her hands in the water. The market went quiet around them. It took about as long as boiling an egg. The child sat up, and looked at the lamps, and asked for noodles.

By nightfall the queue went round the lock and up the stair.

They healed anyone. They asked nothing. They said, to everyone who asked, that it could not be bottled or sold and would not keep, and that anybody claiming otherwise was a thief. Ilan worked at the second basin with his hat on. He purged lungs and blood and old infections. In nine days he saw more of what the refineries did to people than he had seen in fifteen years of sitting above them. The porter came back every day. His name was Garran, and the child's was Pim. He appointed himself to keep the queue in order, and nobody argued with him.

Brightwater's security came on the fourth day. There were twelve of them, in white, with batons. They found three hundred dock families standing between them and the table. Garran was in front with his arms folded. Above them, in a glass box, a Lockkeeper was drinking his tea with great attention. The men in white went away again.

She came on the sixth night, alone, after the market lamps were dimmed.

Ilan knew her walk before he saw her face. She came down the steps to the lowest tier in a plain raincoat, with no guard, and stood at the edge of the planking where Garran had stood. She was sixty, thin and upright, with cropped grey hair and a doctor's hands. She looked at the two basins. She looked at Yeva for a long time.

"I had you in a tank for eight months," Dr Linnea Marsh said. "I signed for it. I told myself it was research."

"I know what you told yourself," Yeva said. "I could hear it through the glass."

"Yes. I expect you could." She did not flinch. Her eyes moved to Ilan, under his hat, and rested there without surprise. "Mr Voss. I thought it must be. Nobody else knew which floor." She turned back. "I've watched this table for three days from a window. I saw that child's lungs. I trained eleven years to do less than that girl did in four minutes. I've spent thirty more working out what people will pay not to watch their children die." She said it flatly, like a diagnosis. "I have forty hospitals. There are beds in them empty tonight, because the people who need them have no scrip. I want to open the wards. To you, and to any healer of yours who'll come. No charge at the door."

Sefa had stopped with a cloth in her hand.

"Why?" Yeva said.

"Because I was a doctor before I was a board. I'd like to be one again before I die. I haven't a better reason."

"I'll not go into one of your buildings again," Yeva said. "The girl might. That's hers to say."

"I'd want to see the tank first," Sefa said. "Empty. With the lid off."

"You shall."

"Can you do it?" Ilan asked. "It isn't only yours to open."

For the first time Marsh hesitated.

"No," she said. "Brightwater's notes are held at the Exchange, like everybody's. I can't give away a bandage without the chairman's leave. I've asked to see him." She turned her collar up against the drip from the planking overhead. "He's always been very civil to me. He remembers my children's names."

Ilan said nothing. He knew whose leave she meant. He knew how that civility felt, from the inside.

She went back up the steps. Sefa watched her go.

"She meant it," she said. "I could feel her meaning it. Is that allowed? For one of them just to stop?"

"I don't know," said Ilan. "I've never seen anyone try."

On the ninth day Yeva went back to her road. She kissed Sefa on the forehead, which made Sefa cry. To Ilan she said only, "Look at it. All of it. Every day." A week later there was a mariner healer at the waterline in Clearwell, under Brightwater's own white terraces. After that came another in the Stubborns, and one at the Crown. Pell came to the boat with the figures, shaking with joy. Within the month, Brightwater's medical division had lost a third of its custom. In Clearwell the workers' towns had stopped buying at the company pharmacy. The board was in emergency session, and the smiling sun had been taken off three buildings by persons unknown.

On the last evening Garran stopped by the canal where Ilan was sitting. He had Pim on his shoulders. He had been watching Ilan for days.

"You're not one of them," he said. "Not quite. You're one of us, and you're not that either. You talk like the Sluice." He scratched his broken nose. "Who are you?"

He could have said *Wrack*. It was a good name. A kind girl had given it to him, and he had been a better man under it.

"Ilan Voss," he said. "I was born four streets from here."

Garran looked at the poster on the wall across the canal. He looked back.

"Thought you were drowned," he said.

"I was. It didn't take."

The big man considered this. Then he nodded, slowly. He seemed to be putting it away to think about later. He went off up the stair with his daughter, who was waving.

## Chapter 9. The Engineers

They left Saltgate by the north lock, in the rain, with Sefa in the tub in the deckhouse and a week's noodles in the hold.

Ilan stood in the stern and watched the lights go down into the weather. He had told a stranger his name, and nothing had happened. That would not last. Pell had said so, wringing his hands on the quay. Halcyon had people in every port. They would know by now that the Chief Surveyor had walked into a Brightwater building and walked out with its most valuable property. They would be putting that together with a barge that had left the sea.

"So we've a fortnight before they come looking in earnest," Dace said. "Where to?"

"Keelhaven."

She took the pipe out of her mouth. "That's Kessler ground. Every second man's a guard. What do you want there?"

"Halcyon has an engine and no ship to put it in. Kessler-Vane is building one." He was looking north, where the hum of the Thread had begun to prickle. "I know how long they need. I want to see how far they've got."

It was three days, by four Eyes. On the third night the sky ahead of them turned orange and stayed that way.

The Kessler Banks had been islands once. They were low and sandy and so thoroughly built over that it was no longer possible to say where one stopped and the next began. They made a single plain of steel a hundred kilometres long. Slipways ran down into the sea like the teeth of a comb, hundreds of them, each with a hull in it. Cranes stood over them in rows, taller than the towers of Saltgate. Sparks fell from them in showers, all night, and the smoke above was lit from below like a ceiling. There was a sound, too, a steady ringing roar. It was fifty thousand hammers.

Three hundred thousand people lived in Keelhaven, and the city kept no clocks. It kept sirens. One sounded as they came in past the breakwater, a long rising howl. On every gantry the small figures put down their tools, and others came up the ladders to take their places.

"Six shifts a day," Ilan said. "They name the hours by them. *I'll see you at third siren.* I lived here two years, when I was learning hulls."

He could feel the ships on the slips through the Thread, faintly. They were not Maker work, and they did not answer. But there were Maker things in them. Kessler-Vane bought every scrap the wreckers could tear out of the drowned ruins, and put it into its hulls without understanding it. He could feel those pieces, scattered through the yards like teeth coming in.

"Slip Nine," he said. "The big one."

Okonta had come ashore with him. She had come up out of the harbour after dark, in a cloak, and was drying out by inches. She would not hear of his going alone. They stood on a coal wharf and looked across the water.

It was the largest hull he had ever seen. It was three hundred metres long and half plated, with the ribs showing forward like a carcass. Floodlights stood all round it. Amidships there was a void. It was a great square well, open to the sky, braced with girders thicker than a man. It was the shape of a thing he had last seen in the lowest vault of a drowned city.

"That's what carries the engine," Okonta said. "Six weeks?"

"Five. They've doubled the shifts."

"Then sink it. Tonight. You could lift it off the slip and break it in half. I've seen what you can do."

"And they'll lay another. I'll have killed a night shift to buy a month." He was watching the people on the gantries. "I know these people. I trained beside half of them. They're not like Halcyon's. Halcyon digs things up and sells them. These people *make* things. It's all they care about. Do you know what the designers at Kessler-Vane do with their own money?"

"I don't."

"They buy Maker scrap from wreckers. A dead lamp. A length of rib. A warm-stone, if they're rich. It's forbidden, the company wants it all, so they keep it in drawers. They take it out after hours and try to understand it." He almost laughed. "There are fifty thousand engineers in this city. They're the best minds on the islands. They've been in love with your machines all their lives. And all anyone has ever let them do with it is build gunboats."

"So?"

"So I'm not going to fight a shipyard," Ilan said. "I'm going to make it a better offer."

---

Teodora Raske had been chief designer of Kessler-Vane Shipworks for twenty-two years.

She was sixty, small and grey, with ink on her cuffs. She had come up from the fitting sheds, the daughter of a riveter. She had designed every capital hull launched on Ovara in two decades. She had taught Ilan Voss to read a framing plan when he was nineteen and insufferable. She had told him then that he had the best eye for a section she had ever seen, and that he would waste it on a company that dug holes.

Her office was at the top of the design loft. The loft was a hall as long as a ship, with drawing tables in ranks under green-shaded lamps. It was the fourth siren, and the tables were empty. She was still at her desk. She did not look up when her door opened.

"If that's the revised framing, leave it. If it's the board, I'm dead."

"It's Ilan Voss, Teo."

She looked up then. She did not go white, as the others had. She looked at him for some time over her spectacles.

"You're drowned," she said. "I went to the service. Crane gave a reading."

"I expect he did it well."

"He did, the swine." She took the spectacles off. "You look terrible. You look like something that's been living in a pond. What do you want?"

He looked at the shelf behind her desk. Among the models and the awards stood a smooth dark ovoid on a stand, the size of a loaf.

"You still have it."

"My warm-stone? Twenty years. I paid a wrecker a year's salary for it. I've had it scanned, sectioned on paper, modelled. I had a man in to hit it." Her mouth twisted. "It's a rock, Ilan. We don't know what they did. We'll never know."

He picked it up. His fingers found the seam. He took the tool from his pocket, and Raske's eyes fastened on that worn little thing and did not leave it. He opened the stone on her desk, among her drawings, and showed her what was inside.

"This is seated wrong. It's been dropped. This is the channel; it wants to be clear. You don't need a laboratory. You need to stop assuming it's a machine like yours. Yours push. These flow." He moved three things, and closed it, and set it back on its stand.

The warm-stone began, very gently, to glow. In a minute the office was as warm as a kitchen.

Teodora Raske put her hand out and held it over the stone. She was sixty years old and had built the navies of five corporations, and her hand was shaking.

"Show me that again," she said.

He showed her until the fifth siren. Then he told her what Slip Nine was for. He told her what the void amidships would carry, and where it had come from. He told her who he was now, and what he had been, and what his mother had been. He left nothing out. She listened without a word, with the warm-stone between her two hands.

"I can't build what the Makers built," he said. "The one man who could is dead. He died before he could teach me. But I can mend. I can teach mending, and I think anyone can learn it. I think that's all it ever was, something nobody was left to teach. Every hold under the sea is going dark, Teo. There are two million people down there, and they need engineers more than they've ever needed anything. I can't pay. I'm offering the work itself. Any of your people who puts down tools on that hull and walks out, I'll teach them."

"They'll hang you for this," Raske said. "They'll hang *me*."

"Probably."

She looked at the warm-stone for a while.

"A man in grey brought you to this loft," she said. "Did you ever know that? He put a paper on my desk. He said, make an engineer of this one, and send the account to the Exchange. I told him I didn't take pupils to order. He said he quite understood, and he asked after my father's lungs. By name." She turned the stone in her hands. "I've been making things to that man's order ever since. I have never once seen him angry. I think that's what frightened me.

"I was told that hull was a deep dredger," she went on. "I didn't believe it. I didn't ask, either. I'm good at not asking. It's how you get to be chief designer." She set the stone down. "Give me two days. And stay off the streets. You've a face like a wanted poster."

---

He spent the two days in a net loft over a chandler's shop, which Dace had found. He did not see how the thing was done. He saw only one part of it.

On the second night Raske came up the ladder with a lantern, and five people came behind her.

They were her section heads. There was a hull man, grey and stooped. There was a woman from engines with burn scars on her hands, and a young draughtsman who looked ready to faint. There were two more. They stood in a row among the hanging nets and looked at Ilan as people look at a ghost. He supposed that he was one.

Raske took the warm-stone out of a canvas bag and set it on a barrel. It was glowing. Nobody spoke. Then the woman from engines stepped forward and held her scarred hands to it, as one does to a fire. Her face crumpled.

"I've got a lamp," she said. "At home. In the wall, behind the stove. I've had it nineteen years. My husband thinks it's a piece of drainpipe."

"Bring it," Ilan said.

She went and fetched it, there and then, through the patrolled streets, wrapped in a shawl. It was a hand lamp, a small one, such as the Makers must have carried about. He did not mend it. He put it into her hands, and laid Jef's tool in her fingers, and showed her where the seam was. He told her what she was looking for. It took her forty minutes. He did not touch it once.

When it came on she made a sound he had heard only once before, from Elder Oro, under a lamp in Low Anthem.

The draughtsman was crying openly. The hull man had sat down on a coil of rope.

"How many can you carry?" Raske asked Dace. Her voice was quite steady.

"Me? Twenty. If you want more you'll need tugs."

"We've tugs," said the hull man. "I sign for them."

---

It took a day and a half, and then it went wrong very quickly.

Three hundred and eleven of them came. There were designers, fitters and a whole hull-survey team. Two apprentices came who were not old enough to sign their own indentures, and there were the families of all of them. They gathered at the old ferry stage on the seaward side an hour before the first siren, with what they could carry. There were children in coats over their nightclothes, and an old man with a birdcage. Many of them carried something small and heavy, wrapped in cloth. Raske came last, with the warm-stone under her arm in its canvas bag.

Somebody had talked. Somebody always did. The company's security came down the stage road in armoured trucks with the lights off. Over the yard loudspeakers, a calm voice began reading out the clauses of an indenture.

There were yard tugs at the stage, eleven of them, and Dace's boat. Okonta took the trucks. Ilan never saw how. He saw a silver needle come up out of the harbour and go the length of the road at head height, a thing no needle should have been able to do. After that the trucks were not on the road, and nobody was dead.

"Go!" she shouted. "I'll find you!"

They went out through the breakwater in a string of overloaded tugs, into the storms. Two company cutters came after them.

He had not called in a storm before. Hesper had warned him in passing. Yeva had told him in so many words: *it's stronger than anything, and it's nobody's friend.* He stood in the bow of the last tug in black rain, with three hundred people behind him, and he asked.

The storm answered at once. It gave him twenty times what he had asked for.

A sea rose under the lead cutter and threw it sideways. He had meant only to slow it. He caught it, barely, and set it upright. In doing that he lost his grip on the wind. A gust laid two of his own tugs on their beam ends, and a child went over the side. He had her back in a fist of water before she had sunk a metre. He was soaked in sweat inside the rain. It was like trying to drink from a waterfall.

"*Less!*" Sefa was screaming from Dace's boat. "Ask for *less!*"

He stopped trying to use the storm. He asked for one small thing only: a road. It was a strip of flatter water forty metres wide, from the bow of the lead tug to the grey gap in the cloud where Dace was pointing. He asked for the storm to go on doing as it liked everywhere else.

That he could hold. The cutters lost them in the second squall. They made the first Eye at dawn, and lay in it, tug beside tug, while people who had never been to sea in their lives hung over the rails. They made the fourth Eye two days later.

On the third evening the gale skates were pouring overhead, all one way. They were going toward something low and dark on the water, with a thousand lanterns coming alight along it.

---

Lashing was a town of six thousand hulls.

They had been roped together, over generations, on a mat of stormgrass a kilometre wide. The grass was a floating weed with a stem like tarred cable. It grew in the Eyes and nowhere else, and it knitted itself so thick that a man could walk on it. The Driftfolk had built on it as other people build on rock. There were barges and old trawlers, and houseboats, and platforms on oil drums. There was the gutted hull of a Meridian frigate with a market in it. There were streets of planking, and bridges of rope. There were gardens in tubs, and goats, and a schoolroom. Smoke rose from a thousand stovepipes. The whole of it moved, very slowly, with the Eye it lived in. It rose and fell on the swell like a sleeper's chest.

It had a law. Nobody who reached it was ever handed back. It had never been in the same place twice, and no corporation had ever taken it. Several had tried.

The Driftfolk hauled the tugs in without asking a single question. Women came across the planking with blankets. A fat old man in a striped jersey stood on the frigate's bow, counting heads and shouting numbers to someone below. Dace's aunt kept a soup barge at the middle of the town, and by the time the last tug was made fast there were three hundred bowls on its deck.

"It's what we're for," Dace said, when Raske tried to thank her. "We've all of us run from somebody."

On the second night Ilan stood on a barge deck with a dead Maker lamp out of somebody's cabin. Lanterns hung in the rigging. Three hundred agravar engineers sat in rows before him on the planks, in borrowed oilskins. Many of them had unwrapped what they carried. Each had a dead lamp, or a cracked warm-stone, or a length of pale rib. They held them on their knees.

He was more frightened than he had been at the Brightwater tower. He thought of an old man on a step with a mug. *Teach everybody. Teach people you don't like.*

"The mariners say tend, mend, make," he said. "I can't teach you to make. I can teach you to mend. It begins with finding the seam."

He taught the seam first. Teodora Raske sat in the front row with a notebook, like a girl of nineteen.

It was slow. For three days nobody opened anything. On the fourth the young draughtsman did. By the tenth there were forty lit lamps along the barge rail. Children from Lashing came to look at them. A handful of mariners came with them. They traded at the raft's edge, and had heard, and did not believe it. They hung in the water alongside and watched islanders do what no mariner had done in six hundred years. Ilan saw one of them, an old pearl-diver, turn and swim away very fast toward the south.

Sefa had made friends with the two apprentices. They were fifteen, a boy and a girl, and they wanted to see a hold more than anything in the world. She was teaching them to hold their breath, with the patience of a person training puppies.

Kessler-Vane was not destroyed. Dace brought word a week later that the yards were still working, after a fashion. But the heart had gone out of the place. The framing on Slip Nine had been found defective, and there was nobody left with the skill to say why. The hull would launch, by direct order of the Halcyon board, unfinished.

She brought something else too, and she did not want to hand it over.

It was a flimsy, printed in the cheap purple ink the Driftfolk used for notices. There had been a bale of them on every raft-trader's deck from the Crown to the Bright Shelves. They had been paid for in advance, to be carried down to every hold that traded at the tide line.

His own face was at the top, the smooth one, in the collar. Under it were a personnel summary and a list of routes, dates and tonnages. The charts were reproduced in miniature. Each had its red line, and each was signed.

THE MARINERS' NEW SAVIOUR, it said across the bottom, IS THE MAN WHO CHOSE WHERE TO DUMP.

"Halcyon," Dace said. "It'll be in every hold in the world inside ten days."

Sefa read it. She had known for weeks. She went white all the same, at seeing it in print, with the name of her hold on the third line.

Okonta had come in that morning on a needle with a bent prow. She read it over his shoulder.

"There was already talk of a Moot," she said. "All the waters, the first there's ever been. Deep Chorus called it, and Nine Spires offered the ground. They wanted to see the man who mends lamps." She folded the flimsy and gave it back to him. "They'll still hold it. But it won't be to see you now. It'll be to try you."

"Where?"

"Tidehall. On the Bright Shelves."

Ilan looked across the lantern-lit deck. Three hundred agravars were bent over their lamps, arguing. A mariner girl was showing an apprentice shipwright how to breathe out slowly. He put the flimsy in his pocket, next to the tool.

"Good," he said. "I was going to have to tell them anyway."

He was still standing there when the fat old man in the striped jersey came puffing along the planking, with his brass trumpet under his arm.

"There's a launch come into the Eye," he said. "Grey. No flag. One old gentleman in it, and a hamper. He's asked for the law, in the proper words, and he's got it. I can't refuse it him. I never refused it you." He looked at Ilan unhappily. "He says he's come to dine with his surveyor. He says you'll know the day."

Ilan counted. He had not thought of it in months, and it was the day.

## Chapter 10. The Table

Lashing had one law, and it had never before been asked to keep it for an enemy.

The trumpet-man's name was Odo. He had been harbourmaster of the raft-town for thirty years, which meant that he counted heads and settled quarrels about moorings. He stood on the planking with his jersey straining over his stomach and explained it to them twice. He looked as though he would have liked someone to explain it to him.

"He came in under his own power and he asked for the law. *I've reached you, and I ask not to be handed back.* Those are the words. It doesn't say who's to ask them. My grandmother gave the law to a Kessler deserter with a price on him, and to the bailiff who came after him, on the same day. They sat at opposite ends of the same barge for a month." He wiped his neck. "Nobody's handed back. That means nobody lays a hand on him while he's on our grass. And he doesn't lay a hand on you. If he tries, he's broken it, and we put him in the sea ourselves."

"He won't try," Ilan said. "He doesn't do things with his hands."

"You're never going," said Dace.

"He's come a long way."

"He had you thrown off a ship with your wrists tied!"

"Yes," Ilan said. "I'd like to ask him about that."

Sefa said nothing. She was in the water beside the barge, holding the planking with both hands, and she had gone very quiet. He had told her on the black sand who Anselm Rook was. She had asked him then whether he had loved the man, and he had said that he supposed so, and she had not asked anything else.

Teodora Raske would not come. She said that she had sat across a desk from the chairman of Meridian twice in her life, and each time had agreed to something. "I'd end by asking his opinion of my framing," she said. "Go on. Don't drink his wine."

Okonta said only that she would be on the roof.

---

He had chosen the frigate.

It lay near the middle of the town, a long grey hull with the guns out of her and a market on her main deck. Her name had been painted over years ago. The stalls had been cleared from the afterdeck, and a table had been carried up from somebody's cabin. There were two chairs, and a lantern. Under the table the deck was an iron grating, and below the grating was the sea. Lashing rose and fell beneath it all, gently, like a sleeper's chest.

Anselm Rook was standing at the rail with his hands behind him. He wore a dark coat with a high collar. He turned when he heard the step, and he looked at Ilan for a moment without speaking. It was a different look from the one on the step at Kettle House. That had been a man checking a figure. This was simply an old man who was glad.

"You look well," he said. "You look a great deal better than you did at his house. You had a face like an empty plate there. It was very hard to see." He gestured at the deck. "I insured this ship. She was lost with all hands in a typhoon, off the Stubborns, nineteen years ago. I paid out on her myself. It's pleasant to see that someone has had the use of her."

"You brought a hamper."

"I brought a bream. From the tanks at Concord. I gave it to a formidable woman on a soup barge. She told me that everyone who reaches Lashing gets soup, and that I should have soup. Then she looked at the fish and said it was a sin to waste one." He drew out a chair. "Sit down, Ilan. It's the day. I have not missed it in twenty-two years, and I didn't care to begin."

Ilan sat.

It was what he had not been ready for. He had been ready for the calm and the courtesy, and for being afraid. He had not been ready for how well he knew it all. He knew the way Rook unfolded a napkin. He knew that he would pour for his guest first, and taste nothing until the other had tasted. He had sat across a table from this man on this day every year since he was sixteen. He had told him things he had told nobody. He had been told, each year, quietly, that he was doing well.

Dace's aunt brought the bream herself, on a tin dish, with her mouth shut like a purse. It had been cooked in stormgrass ash, as the Driftfolk cooked. Rook thanked her. He boned it at the table, as he always had.

"You could have told me my name," Ilan said. "On the step."

"I offered."

"You offered to sell it to me."

"I offered to take you home. I don't see the difference, but I know that he would have." Rook laid a fillet on Ilan's plate. "Ask me what you came to ask. You've never been able to eat until you'd said the thing."

"You gave Crane the order."

"Yes."

Ilan had thought that there would be more. He waited.

"You rang me from the survey ship at one in the morning," Rook said. "You told me that the Makers were one people, and that your mother had been a mariner, and that you meant to say so to forty million islanders by the evening bulletin. You were crying. I had never heard you cry. And I knew what was in the lowest vault, because my own people were standing in front of it." He set down the knife. "Consider what you were about to do. You'd have told five boards that there was a city in the open Girdle, belonging to nobody, with an engine in it. They'd have been at war for it in a week. The whole Girdle, Ilan. With that engine in the middle, and Halcyon nearest to it. I had perhaps ten minutes. I've made worse decisions, and taken longer over them."

"You could have talked to me."

"I had talked to you for twenty-two years. You stopped listening the moment those walls lit up. I heard it happen." He said it without reproach. "I asked Crane to be quick. I understand that he was not. I'm sorry for that."

"And do you regret it?"

"I regretted it every day until the barge went up. I sat in my office and I couldn't add." For the first time something moved in the square calm face. "Since then I have thought it the best mistake I ever made. You went into the water a surveyor. Three weeks later I had a report on my desk. Something in the Anthem Waters had lifted a Halcyon barge out of the sea and set it down a kilometre off. A deckhand had drawn what he saw. I knew whose hands those were before I had turned the page. I'd been waiting for them all my life."

"He died mending what you broke."

"I broke a machine that was nine hundred years past its time. I wanted to see if he could let one go. He never could let anything go. It's why there's nothing left of him but you." Rook lifted his glass, and did not drink. "I didn't mean him to go down to it. I'll say that once. I don't expect it to count."

Under the grating the sea moved, and the lantern light slid on it. Something else moved there too, small and still. Ilan did not look down.

---

"I'll show you what I want," Rook said, when the plates were cleared. "I'd sooner you refused the real thing than some notion of it."

He took a chart from inside his coat and spread it between the glasses. It was Ilan's own work. He knew the hand. It showed the Girdle, the whole belt of it, with every island and every sounding.

"Tell me the depths," Rook said. "You always could."

"Sixty metres. Forty here. A hundred and ten on the sill."

"A shelf. The islands are only its high places. Two hundred thousand square kilometres of ground, and nowhere on it more than a tall building's depth of water. That is all that stands between forty million people and room to breathe." He laid his broad hand flat on it. "Halcyon thinks it has a gun. Halcyon has only ever had the one idea. My people recorded those walls for four hours before they went dark, and I've had the pictures read. It was never a gun. The Makers built it when the sea first began to come up. It keeps water out. It tells the sea where it may not be, and the sea is not there. They meant to stand their city dry behind it for ever."

"Then why is it behind three doors?"

"Because they lost their nerve."

"Or they saw what it left."

Rook looked at him with a kind of pleasure. "Yes. You'd see that. Fetch me some of the sea, would you? In the soup bowl. I can't reach."

Ilan knelt at the grating and dipped the bowl. He felt the water as he lifted it, as he felt all water now. It dwindled as it left the sea. It was like a voice heard from the next room, and then from down a passage.

Rook had taken something from his pocket. It was no bigger than a plum stone, pale and ribbed. Ilan's hands knew it at once for Maker work, and knew that it was a piece of something much larger.

"The governor," Rook said. "It's the smallest part of it, and the only one I carry." He set it in the middle of the bowl. He did not touch it again. He only looked at it, with his head a little on one side, as Okonta looked at her needle.

The water drew back.

It went away from the little pale thing on every side, smoothly, and stood up round the rim of the bowl in a ring as high as a finger. The middle of the bowl was dry. It was not like calling. Ilan had shaped water a thousand times, and it had always been like guiding something alive. This was like watching a nail go into wood.

Below the grating someone caught their breath.

Rook lifted the governor out between finger and thumb. The ring slumped, and the water lay flat in the bowl.

"Now ask it for something," he said.

Ilan did not need to. He had felt it go. There was no voice at all now, near or far. It was a bowlful of the Slick, as clear as glass.

"It will never answer anyone again," Rook said. "That is the cost, and I don't hide it. The engine as Halcyon has it would dry a harbour. Seated where it belongs, in the city, with a heart large enough to drive it, it would hold the sea off the whole shelf. The water it holds back is shared out over every ocean on Ovara. My actuaries say the width of two fingers. Nine Spires will not notice."

"And round it?"

"A ring of that." He nodded at the bowl. "A wide one. The Anthem Waters as far north as Chorus Canyon, and the near half of the Bright Shelves. They'd not die in a day. The kelp would go quiet, and then it would go."

"Deep Chorus is on the edge of that."

"I have tanks for Deep Chorus. I have tanks for all of them, and lamps that work, and wages. They are going dark anyway, Ilan. You've lain under those hearts. You know better than I do how many of them want making, and that nobody alive can make. I am offering two million people an end that isn't the dark."

"On twenty years' service."

"Everyone owes something. It's how a person knows where they belong." He folded the chart. "Here is what I came to say. That hull at Keelhaven is Halcyon's notion, not mine. I hold Halcyon's paper. I can stop that fleet with a letter. I'd do it this week, if I had no need of a gun. Come back. Seat the engine properly, in its own city, as only you can. Then nobody boils anyone. You'd have saved your twelve thousand in the canyon by sitting down at a bench." He paused. "Without you I have only the gun, and men like Crane to point it. I'd much rather have you."

Ilan did not answer at once.

It was the cleverest thing Rook had ever offered him, and it was kindly meant. That was the worst of it. He could stop the fleet tonight, with a word. He would be home. He would know his own front door. And Rook was right about the hearts.

"How long have you known?" he said. "About the routes. That there were holds under them."

"Always. I read the tonnage every quarter."

"And you let me draw them."

"I thought you knew what you were, and had made your peace with it. As I had." Rook's eyes were steady. "I was wrong about that. It's the only thing about you I ever got wrong."

"You taught me to look at a blank chart and tell you the depth," Ilan said slowly. "I've been looking at yours. It's a good chart. There's nobody on it. There's ground, and tonnage, and service owed. I drew eleven of those. I know just how it's done, and how well you sleep after." He pushed the bowl an inch across the table. "I won't seat it."

"And the forty million?"

"I'll find them somewhere."

"Where?"

"I don't know yet."

"No," Rook said. He sounded tired, and not surprised. "You don't. He never did either. It's a great weakness in a mechanic."

He rose, and buttoned his coat, and looked out at the lanterns of the raft-town rocking on the swell. On a barge two decks away, forty white lamps burned steadily along a rail.

"The paper was mine," he said. "The purple one. I had it printed in the week you walked out of that clinic. Your mariners are going to try you at their Moot, and half of them will want you dead. I'd sooner they were done with you quickly. When they are, come to any Meridian door." He put the governor back in his pocket. "One more thing, since you'll hear it. Linnea Marsh came to see me. She wished to open her wards to your little girl with the basins. I told her that I could not release the notes. There was a fire at her clinic in Clearwell on Tuesday night. She was working late. The board has named a successor."

Ilan was on his feet. The chair had gone over behind him.

"You—"

"I hold her company's paper. I did not light anything. I never do." Rook regarded him across the table. "You've done something to the water round you. An arm's length of it, under this grating. Who taught you that? The herder? It's well done. You'd not have needed it." He inclined his head. "If you ever do need to win, Ilan, and not merely to mend, remember the bowl. You gave that barge an order and begged its pardon in the same breath, and the sea did both badly. The engine isn't sorry. There's nothing in it but the one thing. You were like that at a desk for fifteen years. It's the only thing I ever taught you that you were truly good at."

He went down the frigate's side to his launch, unhurried, an old man minding the rungs. Nobody laid a hand on him. Odo stood at the rail with his trumpet and watched the grey boat out of the Eye.

Dace's aunt came for the dish. There was a square of scrip under the wine bottle, a great deal of it. She looked at it for a moment. Then she picked it up with the tongs and dropped it through the grating.

---

Sefa came up the side like something thrown.

She stood on the deck with the water running off her, and Bother at her heels. She went straight to the table and put her hand into the soup bowl. She took it out again at once and held it against her chest.

"I felt it go," she said. "From down there. It was like when a patient goes. You've got your hands on them, and there's somebody in the room, and then there isn't." Her voice shook. "He did that to show you. He killed it to *show* you."

"Yes."

Ilan took the bowl to the rail and tipped it over. The water did not mix. It lay on the swell for a moment in a flat patch, like oil, before the sea closed over it.

Okonta had come down off the deckhouse roof. Her face was grey.

"A heart large enough," she said. "I've good ears. What heart?"

"There's only one that size." He was still looking at the place where the water had gone. "It runs a kilometre down under the Girdle, in the trenches, where nobody from the islands can reach it. It was worn out. He couldn't mend it, and he couldn't have made it run."

"And now it runs."

"On two wheels. Yes." He turned round. "He told me to mend it. On the Dragon, in front of all of you. *I should like to see it run.*"

"It never comes up," Okonta said. "Not in these waters. Not in three thousand years."

"No," said Ilan.

Neither of them said anything more about it.

Raske had been waiting at the foot of the frigate's ladder with a shawl over her head. She looked at the overturned chair.

"Well?" she said. "Did you agree to anything?"

"No."

"That's more than I ever managed." She studied his face. "But you wanted to."

He sat down on the deck, with his back against the rail. Sefa sat beside him. After a moment she leaned against his arm, as she had not done since before the black sand.

"He said he could stop the fleet tonight," Ilan said. "With a letter. I believe him. He's right about the hearts, Sefa. Two of them at Deep Chorus are finished, and I can't make. He's right that there are forty million people up here with nowhere to put their feet. I haven't an answer for him." He rubbed his wrists. "I wanted to say yes. For about as long as it takes to pour a glass."

"I know. I could see your feet through the grating. You'd turned them toward him."

"Had I?"

"He's right about lots of things," Sefa said. "So were you. You knew exactly how deep it was everywhere. Mother's arms didn't come out of somebody being wrong about the depth." She pulled Bother's ears through her fingers. "He never once asked you what you wanted. I listened the whole time. He told you what you were worth, and what you were good at, and where you belonged. Jef asked you things. You said. He asked you why you'd done the coupling backwards."

Ilan thought of an old man on a step with a mug. *You say that. You don't know yet what you'll want to keep.*

"Dr Marsh meant it," Sefa said, very low. "I told you she did."

"Yes."

"I was going to go and look at the tank. With the lid off."

He put his arm round her. Out in the dark, beyond the last lanterns, the wall of the storm stood up all round the Eye, and the gale skates were going over.

## Chapter 11. Deaf Water

The young Threaded from Fallow came into the Eye two nights later, on his needle, and fell off it at the barge rail.

Okonta had him out of the water before anyone else had moved. He was grey to the lips. He had not slept, he said, since the forest. Sefa put a cup into his hands and held them round it.

"Fallow's dark," he said. "The last lamp went nine days ago. The white ship was over us the next morning, with the bell. Our speaker wouldn't mark the paper. She said that Deep Chorus had lit its lamps again, and we'd go there." He drank, and coughed. "So they went. All of them. Nine hundred, with the sleds. It's five days' swim to the canyon with children. I flew guard. On the second day the grey ships came up behind us. Twelve. They're not hurrying. They're only following, and every hour they're a little nearer."

"Meridian?" Ilan said.

"It's their mark. And they've a pilot. Somebody who knows the kelp, and takes them round the shoals, and knows where a column would rest." He looked at Okonta, and then away. "I know the hull. I held a lamp beside him for a night. He told me I had good hands."

"Corin," Okonta said. Her face did not change.

"I couldn't do anything by myself. So I came."

It was most of a day to the Anthem Waters by needle, running flat out. The Moot was in nine. Ilan did the sum, and found that he had already decided.

"Go south with Dace," he told Raske. "Take the forty. I'll meet you at Nine Spires."

"And if you're late?"

"Then tell them I was at Fallow. They'll know where that is. It's on the third line of the paper."

Sefa was already on the needle behind him, with her arms round his waist and the grey band on her arm. He did not argue. He had found that it saved time.

---

He heard the forest before he saw it.

He had not been in the Anthem Waters since Deep Chorus. The chord came up through the hull of the needle and into his chest, as it had on the first morning of his life that he remembered, and for a moment he could not see for it. He had lived in that sound for nineteen days and had not known what it was. It was the sound of a place that was well.

Then they came over the last rise, and saw the column.

It lay along a valley in the kelp for nearly a kilometre. Nine hundred people were moving through the gold light at the pace of the slowest. They had sleds of kelp-floats, lashed in strings and piled with bundles. The old rode on them, and the babies. Tide hounds worked up and down the flanks, turning the children back in. Somebody was singing the tending song, very low, and it went along the line from mouth to mouth. Near the front he saw a woman he knew, with a tired capable face, hauling at a sled rope. An old man was walking beside her. He was walking easily. The Lantern had given him his lungs back.

Over them, like clouds, hung the shadows of twelve hulls.

"They've started," Okonta said.

The two leading ships had drawn ahead of the column. Something was coming down from them. It was brown, and it fell in a curtain. Ilan knew it before it reached the kelp. He had ordered it by the tonne.

"Refinery spoil," he said. "They're laying it across the valley. They're making a wall."

"What for?" said Sefa.

"So that nobody down there can call."

He saw it work. At the head of the column an old man had stopped, with his arms out and his head back, as Hesper had stood on the dome. He was Fallow's Mighty. The brown water reached him. His arms dropped, and he stood looking at his own hands.

Then the nets came down.

They were trawl nets, the great ones, with mouths forty metres wide. They came through the valley from behind, slowly, low to the ground. They did not hurry and they did not swerve. They went into the tail of the column as a scoop goes into grain. A voice was coming down through the water, enormous and calm. It said that the people of Fallow were being taken into protection. It said that nobody should be alarmed, and that their speaker's mark would be accepted on arrival.

He saw a net close. It was full. There were forty people in it, pressed together like fish, and it went up toward the stern ramp of a white-painted transport. A child's arm stuck out through the mesh to the shoulder.

And above the nets, pacing them, a needle moved to and fro. Its rider was pointing. He was showing the trawl-masters where the column bent, and where the ground would foul their gear. He was doing it carefully. He had been asked to see that nobody was hurt.

"I'll take Corin," Okonta said. "You take the nets. *Ask*, Wrack." She was gone.

He went in.

He had his arm's length of sea about him, and it saved his life in the first minute. A gunboat had seen the needle. Charges came down, three together. They burst against the skin of water he had been carrying for a month, and he was thrown end over end, deafened, but whole. He came up under the nearest net with his hands open.

*Please.*

It answered him as the pipes had answered in the Brightwater tower. It was faint, and a long way off. The brown curtain had come down the valley behind him, and he was inside it. He asked for the net to part. A few strands went. Eleven people came out through the hole, and the rest went on up.

He asked again. He asked for the transport's ramp to jam, and it shuddered and went on turning. He asked for a current to push the trawls aside, and got a breath of one. He was asking a sick thing to lift a weight. There were twelve ships, and he was getting in the way of one of them, a little.

Somewhere to his left a charge went off among the sleds. He saw floats burst, and bundles fly, and people. He saw the woman from Fallow thrown down. Her father was on his knees beside her.

*Purge it,* he thought. *Yeva said it's your one. Clear the water and then call.*

He put both hands into the brown and tried to think of the valley as a sick body. He tried to want nothing for himself. He looked up at the grey hulls, and he could not do it. He wanted them dead. He wanted it with his whole heart, and the water could feel nothing else in him. It lay cold in his hands.

A second net closed, and a third. The first transport was already turning away to the south-east with three hundred people in her tanks. High in the kelp, Okonta's needle was a silver fleck among five others. He saw her struck, and spin, and right herself.

He was afraid. He had not known how afraid, until he heard the voice. It was not the great calm voice in the water. It was a quiet one, across a table, saying *if you ever need to win*.

*The engine isn't sorry. There is nothing in it but the one thing.*

He knew how. He had always known. He had done it at a desk for fifteen years.

He stopped asking.

He reached out past the brown wall, into the clean forest beyond it, the whole singing sea from the valley to the ridge. He did not say please. He put everything out of himself that was not the one thing. He put out the fear, and the pity, and Davin's voice saying that it was boys on those boats. When there was nothing left in him but an order, he gave it.

*Off them.*

It was not like the barge. The barge had been a shout, and the sea had reared like a startled horse. This was quiet. It was like watching a nail go into wood.

Twelve hulls went under in the time it takes to shut a door.

The sea did not lift them. It closed on them. Each was taken from above by a weight of water as hard as stone, and pushed down, and broken. The gunboats folded. The trawl cables parted with a sound like bones. The white transports opened along their keels like pods, and the people in the tanks poured out of them into the forest.

Then it was done, and there was no sound at all.

He did not understand at first what he was hearing. He thought that the charges had deafened him. Then a tide hound began to howl, somewhere along the column, and another took it up.

The kelp had stopped humming.

It stood all round him, gold and tall, leaning in the current as it had always leaned. It made no sound. The chord was gone, from the valley floor to the surface, as far as he could reach in every direction. It had been sounding in that forest for three thousand years.

He reached for the sea, to ask it what he had done. There was no door, and no house.

Then the cold came up from his feet.

It came fast. It was not the plain cold of being ebbed. It was the other, the pleasant one, the looseness before sleep. He had put himself out of himself to give that order, and something had noticed the room standing empty. He thought, quite calmly, *this time it will be all of me.*

Two hands closed on the back of his neck.

"No," Sefa said in his ear. "No, you *don't*. I've got you. Stay there. I've got you."

She was holding him as Yeva had taught her to hold a patient who is slipping. She was asking the sea to care that he stayed. Her hands were warm. He felt the cold check, and stand, as a tide stands at the turn.

It could not have him. So it took what was holding him.

He felt it go out through her palms, like a rope paid out through fingers. She made a small surprised sound. Then the cold drew back, and he was on his knees on the floor of a silent forest, and Sefa's hands were still on his neck. They were only hands.

---

There were sailors in the water.

He had not thought of them. He had put them out of himself with the rest. There had been nearly three hundred men and women on those twelve ships, in grey, with Meridian's mark on their sleeves. Most of them had been below. Those who had been on deck were drifting down through the kelp now in a slow rain, with their arms out.

The people of Fallow saw them before he did. They had been in the nets ten minutes before. The woman with the tired face left her father sitting on a sled, and went up through the wreckage, and came down with a boy of nineteen by the collar. She put him across a float with his face in the air. She went up for another.

They did it for an hour. They brought out a hundred and thirty living. They laid the dead on the sleds, in rows, beside Fallow's own. There were nine of Fallow's. There were a hundred and sixty-one of the others.

Sefa had gone to the boy on the float. He was coughing up water, and something in his chest was broken. Ilan watched her put her hands into the sea beside him and go still.

Nothing happened.

She frowned. She shook her hands out, as she did when they were cold, and put them in again. She stood so for a long time. The boy looked up at her large dark eyes, in terror, and she did not seem to see him.

"It's just water," she said.

She said it to nobody. She looked at her palms. Then she looked across the valley at Ilan, and he saw her understand.

She did not cry. He would rather she had. She took the grey band off her arm and held it for a moment. Then she put it on again, pulling the knot tight with her teeth.

"I can still set a bone," she said. "Somebody find me two straight pieces of float. Bother, *leave* it."

---

Okonta came down with her needle's flank scored open and blood on her mouth.

She looked at the sleds. She walked along the rows of grey sleeves, slowly, to the end, and back. He knew what she was counting. She had told him once, in the dark, on the way to Greywatch Stone. Eleven hulls in thirty years, and not one with a crew aboard.

"He's alive," she said. "Corin. And the boy. They were above it." Her voice was quite flat. "He wants to say something to you. I've told him he's the law of Lashing for as long as it takes him to say it."

Corin hung in the water twenty metres off, with his hands open and away from his sides. He looked older than he had on the Dragon. He was staring at the kelp.

"I was told to bring them in gently," he said. "That's the truth. I thought it was a kindness. There's lamps where they were going. My sister's there." He swallowed. "And I was told that if the surveyor came, I was to keep clear and let him do what he does. I asked what that meant. He said I'd know it when I saw it."

"He wanted this," Ilan said.

"There was a boat standing off to the south. Small, fast, with a glass eye on a mast. It was there all morning. It's gone now." Corin looked at him. There was no hatred in the broad face. There was something nearer to pity. "He's never lost a ship he didn't mean to lose. I've learned that much, working for him. I'm sorry about the girl. I saw it take her."

He turned his needle, and gathered the boy Tam in behind him, and went up into the silent gold. Okonta watched him go. She did not lift a hand.

"I said the rule to him, not to you," she said, when he had gone. "I told you that on the black sand. I'll keep it." She wiped her mouth with the back of her wrist. "Don't ask me to say more than that tonight."

The old caller of Fallow had come to the middle of the valley. He knelt, and put his palm flat on the sand, and stayed so, with his eyes shut. When he opened them they were wet.

"She's gone," he said. "From here to the ridge, and as far behind us as I can feel. Ten kilometres. More." He looked up at Ilan. He did not seem angry. He seemed puzzled, like a man who has come home and found the house moved. "That's the road home. We couldn't go back now if every lamp in Fallow came on."

Ilan had nothing to say to him.

"You got my people out of a net," the old man said. "I'll remember that. I'll remember the other thing as well. I don't know how to hold both." He got up, with a hand on his knee, and went back to the sleds.

---

Hesper came at dusk.

He had seen the lights an hour before, yellow-green, moving along the edge of the Tangle to the west. He had known that she would be carrying one now.

She came down alone into the valley. She was thinner, and burned dark, and the cord was on her wrist. She did not look at the dead. She looked at the wrecks of twelve ships lying among the kelp stems, and then she looked at him.

"So that's how it's done," she said.

"Hesper. Don't."

"I taught you a rule on the floor of a hold. *Ask. Do not order.* I made you say it till I was satisfied." She spoke quite softly. "I've been asking for six months. I've a hundred callers in the wild kelp, asking with all they've got, and we can sink one ship in three. You stood here and gave an order, and there's twelve of them on the bottom. I watched you. You weren't even breathing hard."

"Listen to the kelp."

"I am listening. It's quiet." Her eyes went over the silent forest. For a moment something in her face flinched. Then it set. "I can live with quiet. I've lived with it since they put a name on a stem for me. Do you know what's at the end of your eleven routes, Wrack? Port Halcyon. I've been wondering for months how a hundred of us could ask loud enough."

"It took Sefa's gift. It came for me, and she held me, and it took hers."

That stopped her. She had known Sefa from the day she was born.

She looked across the valley, to where a thin girl with a grey band on her arm was binding a sailor's ribs with strips of sea-silk. She looked for a long time.

"Then it took the wrong one," Hesper said.

She went up into the dark. A little later the lights along the Tangle moved off toward the south, and there were more of them than there had been.

---

He did not sleep that night.

He went along the sleds. Yeva had told him to look at it, all of it, every day. He looked at each of the hundred and sixty-one. He made himself see the faces. Most of them were young. One had a letter in his breast pocket that had come apart in the water. One was a woman of fifty with a cook's burns on her forearms. A boy near the end of the last row could not have been more than seventeen, and somebody had closed his eyes.

*It isn't the islanders' children I'm angry with,* Davin had said, pulling a knot tight with his teeth. *I've seen them at the rail.*

He had slept very well for fifteen years. He had drawn eleven lines and never gone to see.

He sat down at the end of the row. After a while Sefa came and sat beside him. Her hands were in her lap. She kept turning them over.

"The rays keep what the undertow takes," she said. "You told me. So they've got it. It's somewhere." Her voice wavered and held. "That's something. It's more than those have."

"I'd give you mine if I knew how."

"I know you would. It's a stupid thing to say." She leaned her head against his shoulder. "You're not to do that again. Not ever. Not if they've got me in a net. Do you hear?"

"Yes."

"Say it."

"I'll not do it again."

"Good." She was silent. "It was so *quick*," she whispered. "That's what I can't stop seeing. Asking takes you all day and you're shaking after. That took you one breath, and you looked like you were doing sums."

Round them the forest stood in the dark without a sound. Far off to the north, at the very edge of hearing, where the dead water ended, the chord began again.

In the morning the people of Fallow took up their sled ropes. They went on toward the canyon, with a hundred and thirty islanders walking among them in hard-suit helmets brought up out of the wrecks, because there was nowhere else to put them. Past the ridge, where the water could hear, Fallow's callers would keep the air in the helmets sweet. They did not sing. Ilan and Okonta saw them over the ridge and into water that could hear.

Then they turned the needles south, for Tidehall.

# Part Five: One Sea

## Chapter 12. The Great Moot

He caught up with the tug on the edge of the Bright Shelves.

It was six days out of Lashing, with Dace's boat ahead of it to find the Eyes. Raske had chosen forty of her people to come. The rest stayed at Lashing with their lamps. She had chosen the forty by asking who was willing to be looked at by two hundred holds of people who had every reason to hate them, and taking the first forty hands. The two apprentices were among them. The woman from engines was another. Her name was Hanne, and she had not put her lamp down since Keelhaven.

The storm had thinned behind them and fallen away, and they lay under a clean sky. Ilan left his needle with Okonta and went up the side. He thought it right to come to the Moot as the islanders came, on a deck, in plain view. Nobody on the tug asked him about Fallow. Raske looked at his face, and at Sefa's hands, and sent the apprentices below.

After the Belt, the Bright Shelves were like stepping out of a cellar into noon. Ilan had seen them from below, through the wall of the Dragon. From a deck they were stranger still. The sea was a pale green so clear that the tug seemed to hang in air. Its shadow ran along the sand ten metres down, sharp as a cut-out. White sand went to the horizon in every direction. There were dark meadows of seagrass, and herds of silverbacks drifting over them, and the herders looked up at the keel and waved. Between the meadows stood the reefs of glass coral. They chimed whenever the swell moved through them. The engineers lined the rail and said nothing at all.

"There's nowhere to hide," Hanne said at last.

"No," Ilan said. "They've lived their whole lives in plain view. It makes them open-handed, and a little vain."

Nine Spires stood up out of the sea like the fingers of two hands.

There were nine Maker towers. They were white and slender and fluted, and each was as thick at the base as the dome of Low Anthem. They rose from the sand through the surface, and thirty metres on into the air. They were crowned with galleries where people walked in the sun. Fifty thousand mariners lived in them, above the water and below. At the tide line, where the towers met the sea, broad stone aprons ran out. Driftfolk rafts were tied up along them by the dozen, and people were trading across the waterline as they did at the Wet Market. But here it was done in daylight, and nobody was looking over a shoulder.

The northern holds called the place half agravar. Ilan could see why. The mariners of Nine Spires wore sea-silk cut in island fashions. Some had steel knives at their belts. Their children ran along the aprons shouting to the raft children in a mixture of both tongues. He saw an old mariner sitting on a step with his feet in the sea, reading an agravar newspaper.

It had fed and housed the speakers of two hundred holds for a week, and it was quietly pleased with itself.

Its speaker met them at the apron. She was a brisk, handsome woman of fifty named Odile. She wore a pair of agravar spectacles on a cord and had the air of a person with a great many lists. She looked at the tug, and at the forty islanders on its deck in their borrowed hard suits.

"Well," she said. "Nobody's ever done *that* before." She did not seem displeased. She turned her eyes on Ilan, and they cooled. "You're him. I've read the paper. I can read their script; most of us can. And I've had the long-call from Fallow, which came quicker." She let it stand a moment. "You'll be lodged apart. Nobody will harm you. I've given my word on that to the north, and I've given my word to the Breakers that you won't leave. Your engineers are guests of this hold. They may go where they like. They'll find my people ask a great many questions."

"Thank you."

"Don't. I'm a hostess. It isn't the same as a friend."

He was given a room high in the eastern tower, with a window on the sea. A guard stood at the door. He was a polite young man with a fishing spear, who was plainly dying to ask about the Dragon. Sefa came up at dusk to say that her mother was not among the northern speakers who had arrived, and that she did not know whether she was glad. Then she went down again, to keep an eye on the apprentices. She had kept the band on her arm. She had told nobody at Nine Spires why she no longer used it.

He sat at the window through most of the night. Far below, on the lit apron, Teodora Raske was sitting cross-legged on the stone with a lamp in front of her. A ring of mariners sat around her, forty or fifty of them, craning to see.

---

Tidehall lay a day beyond.

They went out to it in the morning, the whole of them, in a great slow crowd. The speakers swam, and the people of Nine Spires, and Driftfolk in boats overhead. The engineers walked along the sea floor in their hard suits, each with a mariner at the elbow. It was like a pilgrimage, or a fair.

The Makers had built it. Nobody knew what for. It was a bowl sunk into the white sand, a kilometre across, and terraced all the way round in tiers of the pale stone. Sun came down into it through ten metres of water and lay on the tiers in moving nets of light. And it had a voice. A word spoken at the centre could be heard on the highest tier, as clearly as if the speaker stood at your shoulder.

No hold had ever claimed it. The holds of the south met here in Moot once a year, a few dozen speakers, and rattled about in it like peas in a drum.

Sefa had told him what a Moot was, in the days at Low Anthem, when it had only been another strange word. The mariners had no king and no parliament. Each hold was ruled by its elders, and no hold could command another. They had learned that at Sounding. When a matter touched many holds, each sent a speaker, and the speakers met and talked until they agreed. They did not vote. They talked, for days if need be, until nobody was left who would not go along. If they could not agree, they did nothing. It was slow, and it had kept the peace for nine hundred years. There had been Moots of the north and of the south. There had never been a Moot of all the waters. The world was too wide, and until now there had been nothing all of it needed to say.

Tidehall could have seated every speaker of every hold in the world. It never had.

It was full now.

They had come from everywhere. He saw the people of Deep Chorus, grave and formal in dark sea-silk, with Elder Vey at their head and the grey caller Aurel beside her. He saw Rime, forty of them in sealskin, sweating and scornful in the warm water. Brask was among them, a head taller than the rest. Pearl Stair had sent twelve small holds with one speaker between them. She was a tiny fierce woman with pearls in her hair, and she looked as if she had not agreed with anything since childhood. There were holds he had never heard of. There were holds with their lamps out, and their speakers sat very upright and did not look at anyone. On the upper tiers, behind the speakers, thousands more had come to watch.

In a block on the eastern side sat a hundred or so who wore no hold's colours at all. Each had a knotted cord at the wrist. Hesper was in the front row.

He had asked to stand alone. Okonta, Sefa and Dace were somewhere behind him. So was Teodora Raske, with her forty, the first agravars ever to attend a Moot. Nobody had quite known what to do about that. In the end they had been put on the lowest tier, in front, where everyone could see them.

Odile called for quiet, and got it. She held up a purple flimsy.

"This has gone to every hold," she said. Her voice came back from the far rim a second later. "Some of us can read agravar script. For those who can't: it says that the man called Wrack, who lit the Lantern, restarted the Dragon and mended Deep Chorus, is an agravar officer named Ilan Voss. It says he chose the places where Halcyon dumps. The man himself is here. The Moot will ask him if it's true."

She laid the flimsy down.

"There is a second matter, and it isn't on any paper. Seven days ago, in the valley south of Fallow, twelve agravar ships were sunk in a breath. Ten kilometres of the Anthem Waters have been deaf ever since. Fallow's caller is here, and will tell you what he felt. The Moot will ask about that too."

Ilan swam to the centre of the bowl.

He had thought for ten days about what he would say. He had composed explanations. His mother; the promise; a boy who had been taught to bury half of himself and had done it too well. It was all true. Standing there, with two hundred holds looking down at him, he found that he did not want to say any of it.

"It's true," he said. "My name is Ilan Voss. My father was an agravar. My mother was a mariner, of what hold I never knew. I was Chief Surveyor of Halcyon. I drew the routes. Eleven of them. One passes over Low Anthem. I chose deep water over your holds because you couldn't complain. I never came to see what it did. The charts on that paper are mine, and so is the signature.

"The other is true as well. A hold was being taken up in nets. I asked the sea to stop it, and I couldn't make it hear. So I gave it an order. It obeyed me. A hundred and sixty-one people died, and the water there will never answer anyone again. I'd not do it a second time for any reason. I've been made to promise."

The bowl gave it back to him, word for word.

The noise that followed was not shouting. Mariners do not shout much. It was a low sound from several thousand throats, and it went on a long time.

A man from one of the dark holds rose. He said that the thing was simple. This man should be put out of the sea, onto the land he came from, and no hold should receive him again. The speaker of Pearl Stair said the same, more sharply. She added that a hold which let its lamps be mended by its poisoner deserved to sit in the dark. Half the bowl agreed. It was an old punishment and a mild one. He could feel that many of them would be relieved to have it over.

Then Hesper stood.

She had changed. She was thinner, and burned dark by salt. When she spoke, nobody else did.

"I knew him as Wrack," she said. "I taught him the first rule, on the floor of a hold he'd got bombed. I buried my husband four days after. His name was Davin. He mended nets. I've more right than anyone here to want this man put out." She looked down at Ilan without any expression. "I don't want him put out. That's waste. I was at Fallow. I saw twelve ships go under while he drew one breath. There's no caller in this bowl who could have sunk the smallest of them. He owes us. So give him to us. Give him to the Breakers, and we'll point him at Port Halcyon. He can pay what he owes in the only coin they've ever understood."

That got a different sound. There were fewer voices in it, and they were harder.

"Or," Hesper said, "if he won't, he's no use to anyone, and he knows where every hold in the north lies. Then I'd not put him out. I'd put him down."

"May I answer?" Ilan said.

"You may," said Odile.

"I won't drown a city, and I won't give the sea another order. Not for you, Hesper, and I owe you more than I owe anyone living. There are two million people in Port Halcyon. I was one of them. Most of them have never heard of you. They think you're a story." He turned, slowly, so that he was speaking to the whole bowl. "And I'm not asking to be let off. I'm asking to be put to work. I've no defence. All I've got is what I've done since. The Lantern is lit, eight halls of the nine. The Dragon runs. Deep Chorus breathes, and Elder Vey is here to tell you how near it came. There's a Wellwalker walking again who spent eight months in a tank."

He pointed to the lowest tier.

"And those are forty agravar engineers who walked out of Keelhaven, and there are two hundred and seventy more at Lashing. They can open a lamp. A month ago they couldn't. Some of you watched them do it last night. In a year they'll be able to mend an air-heart. So will anyone of yours who cares to sit beside them. I can't make. Jef died before he taught me. But there needn't ever again be one old man holding all of it up. He told me that himself. It was the last thing he asked me for."

He stopped. Then, because it was true:

"Your holds are failing. Most of you know it. Some of you have been hiding it from your own people for years, as Deep Chorus did. I'm not the only one in this bowl who's been keeping a secret."

Elder Vey rose into the silence.

"He is correct," she said. "We hid it for six years. A child of sixteen shamed us into saying so, and after that we lost nobody. I will speak for him."

"So'll Rime," said a voice like a slab being dropped. Brask had not waited for his elders. He was on his feet with his arms folded. "I rode north with him. He lay on the floor of the Dragon for a day with his arms in its guts. He's not much to look at. Jef wasn't much either." He sat down again.

"So will I," said somebody on the northern side.

It was a broad woman with scarred forearms. She spoke as the speaker of Low Anthem. She must have come in that morning, with the last of the north. Ilan heard Sefa make a small sound behind him. He could not look at the woman, and then he made himself.

"I'm Ione," she said. "I've twenty-one dead and a third of my hold under water, and these." She held up her arms, and turned them, so that the upper tiers could see. "And since Fallow I've a daughter whose hands are empty. She held him up when the sea came for him, and it took hers. She tells me she'd do it again. I've not decided whether to believe her. I'm not forgiving him. I want that understood. I'm a healer. I looked inside his head before he knew what was in it. What I found was a man who'd thrown away everything he was, because he couldn't bear it. Then I watched him get up and work a kelp terrace, badly, for nineteen days." Her voice was quite even. "I say he mends my dome. He mends every dome on those eleven routes, with his own hands, before he's let do anything else. After that I'll think about it."

"Nobody's asked me," said a voice behind him.

Sefa had come down onto the sand. She was very small in that great bowl. She had the band on her arm, and she held her hands a little away from her sides, as if they belonged to somebody else.

"I'm the one it took from," she said. The bowl carried it to the highest tier. "So I'll say. He gave that order because he was frightened, and there was a child's arm sticking out of a net. I'd have done it too, if I'd known how. Then he sat up all night with their dead, and looked at every face." She turned her head toward the eastern block. "Hesper watched the same thing. She went away pleased. That's all the difference there is. I can't heal any more. I can still tell."

Nobody spoke for some time.

There was an old law, Odile said at length, for when a Moot could not agree about a caller. The caller might ask the sea to speak for them.

"It means a shared calling," Hesper said flatly. "You all know the song. I'll say it for the islanders." She turned her head toward Raske's forty. "When callers work together, we work in chorus. Each of us holds a piece. I hold this end of the net, and you hold that end. It's strong, and it's brittle. If one drops their piece, it all comes down. There's another way, in the songs. Many callers pour their strength into one, and that one does the asking. One will, and a hundred people's strength behind it. It's never once been done. Every caller who's tried to carry other people's strength has gone into the undertow. Some of them took the others along."

She looked at Ilan.

"You went under at Deep Chorus. I heard. You'll go under here, and I'll have had my way without lifting a hand."

"Probably," said Ilan. "Will you lend me yours?"

For a moment he thought she would refuse. Then she showed her teeth, in something that was not quite a smile.

"Oh, yes," she said. "I want to see this."

---

They came down out of the tiers, every caller in the Moot. There were some three hundred of them. They made a ring around him on the white sand. Aurel of Deep Chorus was there. A boy from Rime came who was no older than Sefa, and had to be pushed forward by Brask. Hesper came, and eleven others in knotted cords.

He knew what he had done wrong at Deep Chorus. He had reached out and taken, and held, and the holding had hollowed him. He knew what he had done at Fallow, which was worse. He had emptied himself of everything but his own will. This had to be the other emptiness. This time he did not reach at all.

He stood still. He asked the sea for nothing whatever. He thought of the Dragon's ribs, and the fine silted channels in them. He thought of how the bound water ran through when they were clear. He made himself, as nearly as he could, into a thing like that. He was an empty place, and a way through. He said to three hundred people, *Whatever you wish to give. No more than that. I'll only carry it.*

It came slowly, one and then another.

A thread of strength came from the boy from Rime, clean and cold as ice-melt. The callers of Deep Chorus gave something deep and formal, and all together, like a held chord. A woman from Pearl Stair gave grudgingly, and then all at once. More came, and more. It did not fill him; it went through. He kept nothing. He only gave it a direction, and the direction was the smallest and gentlest thing he could think of.

A swell rose at the centre of Tidehall. It was no higher than a man's knee.

It rolled outward, over the rim of the bowl and across the Bright Shelves in a widening ring. Where it passed, the glass coral chimed. Every reef for a hundred kilometres was ringing at once. They heard it at Nine Spires. They heard it on the Driftfolk rafts at the tide line, and men came out on deck to listen. It was a single chord, and it was the voices of two hundred holds. It went on ringing for a long time after the swell had gone.

Nobody was lost. He looked for the cold in his feet, and it was not there.

He opened his eyes. Three hundred callers were looking at him. None of them looked like someone who had just seen a power. They looked as if they had been listened to.

Hesper was looking at her own hands.

The Moot sat until dark. In the end Odile gave its judgement, and nobody was left who would not go along. Ilan Voss was bound to the mending of the holds for the rest of his life, beginning with the eleven routes. He was never again to command the sea. If he did, any caller who saw it might put him down, and no hold would ask why. The engineers were to be received as guests of all the waters, and any hold might send its people to learn beside them. And the holds were to act together, for the first time ever, in whatever came next.

---

Ione found him afterwards on the rim of the bowl, in the last of the light.

Sefa was with her, holding her arm with both hands, as a much smaller child would. Bother had gone quite mad. Ione detached her daughter gently, and sent her off with the hound. She stood looking at Ilan.

He had promised to tell her to her face. He had told two hundred holds first. It was not the same, and he knew it.

"It was my hand," he said. "The line over Low Anthem. I drew it eleven years ago, in an office. It took me about a minute. I'd like to tell you I didn't know there were people there. I knew. I'd decided not to know."

"I gathered that."

"I'm sorry isn't—"

"No. It isn't." She folded her scarred arms. "Don't say it to me again. I've heard it, and it's no use to me. Mend the dome." She was silent. "She's grown. Sefa. She went away a child with a band she hadn't earned. She's earned it now, and she's nothing to wear it with. A Wellwalker taught her, she says. In a canal." She shook her head slowly. "She says you nearly died at Deep Chorus, and she hit you."

"She did. She was right."

"And at Fallow she held you."

"Yes."

"I'd like to hate you for that more than for the dome. She won't let me." Ione breathed out. "She's usually right. She gets it from her father." For a moment something moved in her face that was not anger. "I meant what I said in there. Every word. But I'll tell you one thing more. I didn't say it in the bowl, because it's nobody's business. When I laid that water on your head, I felt what was under the blank. I didn't know what it was. I knew it was eating you. I thought, whatever this man's done, he's the only one who'll never let himself off." She turned to go. "Keep on not letting yourself off. And come and eat with us. Sefa's found some islanders she wants me to meet."

He looked for Hesper after that. He wanted to thank her. She had given more than anyone.

The eastern block was empty. So were eleven other places in the tiers.

"They left at dusk," Okonta said. Her face was grey. "Twelve of them, all Mighty, and every one a Breaker. I asked a girl from Pearl Stair where they were bound, and she laughed at me." She drew a breath. "I've listened for them. They're running north by east, fast. It's the straight line for the Crown." She paused. "There's a needle with them. I know the hull."

"Corin."

"He's told them he's done with the grey man. Perhaps he is. I think he's told them something else as well. How a thing can be built quickly."

## Chapter 13. The Signal

"A chorus of twelve," Ilan said. "How long?"

They had run north for six days. The needles went under the storm, and Dace's boat over it, and they had met again at a rock.

It was one of the Stubborns. There were forty or fifty of those, scattered off the western end of the shelf. They were small islands, too steep or too poor for any corporation to want. They had never signed a charter. The people on them fished, and grew brack rice on terraces no wider than a table, and owed nothing to anybody. Ilan had flown over them often. He had thought of them, when he thought of them at all, as a gap in the map.

This one was called Old Tolla. It was a single village of forty houses, built of grey stone and roofed with turf. It clung to the side of a crag above a cove just big enough for six boats. Goats stood on the roofs. Rain blew through it sideways.

At the top of the village, where the path ended, was a shrine. Sefa found it on the first morning and came down white.

It was a hollow in the rock, older than the houses. There were offerings in it. He saw fish-hooks and shells, and a child's shoe. At the back, worn nearly smooth, a figure was carved. It was a woman, long in the limb, rising from a wave. Her eyes were very large.

"They leave her the first fish of every catch," Sefa said. "I asked an old woman who she was. She said, *the neighbours.*" She was twisting her hands. "They *know*, Wrack. They've always known. Everybody up here says we're a story, and there's a rock where they've been leaving us fish for a thousand years."

The village asked no questions of Driftfolk, or of anyone Driftfolk brought. They had been given a fish-loft over the cove. Rain hammered on its roof.

"To build something that could take a city?" Okonta considered. "It's not one great heave. Each of them holds a piece, and they pile it up, day on day. Ten days, perhaps twelve. They'll do it well offshore, in clean water, where the sea can hear them."

"And Halcyon's engine sails in five days. For the Anthem Waters." He rubbed his eyes. "If I go for the wave, the fleet reaches Deep Chorus. If I go for the fleet, a city drowns. And then every agravar on Ovara has a reason to boil the sea, and they'll be right."

"So which?" Dace asked.

"Neither. Not yet." He spread the purple flimsy on the table. "Rook did me a favour. He told the mariners the truth about me, and the mariners are still standing. Now it's the other side's turn. There are forty million agravars who think mariners are a fairy tale, and that sea terrorists are sinking their ships. They'll cheer that fleet out of harbour. I want them to know what it's carrying. I want them to know whose signature is on it."

"And how," said Teodora Raske, "do you propose to tell forty million people anything?" She had come north with them, in Dace's deckhouse. She said she was too old to be left behind. "Orrin owns every screen."

Ilan had no answer. He knew Orrin Signal as well as he knew anything. It owned the broadcast towers and the cables. It owned the newspapers, and the little radios sealed in wax that were sold at the Wet Market. Every agravar child grew up with an Orrin screen in the corner of the room. It showed the weather and the shift times, and stories about brave company sailors. Nothing was said on Ovara that Orrin did not carry. It carried nothing that the five boards had not approved.

"I have someone for that," Dace said. She seemed almost shy about it.

---

Marit Coyle was thirty-four, and had presented the evening bulletin on Orrin One for six years.

She had silver on her eyelids. Ilan realised that he had seen her forty metres high on a wall in Saltgate, selling something. Hers was the best-known face on Ovara. She arrived at Old Tolla in a fishing smack, in borrowed oilskins. She was sick over the side before she said anything.

"I've been feeding the Clean Hands tonnage reports since I was a researcher," she said. She sat down at the table in the fish-loft. She looked at Ilan for some time, and it was not a friendly look. "Do you know how I started? I was twenty-two. They gave me a pile of Halcyon shipping returns to make into a nice story about exports. The figures didn't add up. There were eighty thousand tonnes a year going out of the Crown and not arriving anywhere." She took off her gloves, finger by finger. "It took me a year to find out where it went. Then I found a name on the routing orders. I used to imagine meeting you. I had a speech."

"I'd like to hear it one day."

"You wouldn't." She unrolled a plan of a building. "The Spire. Two hundred and twelve floors. Studio One is on the hundred and fortieth. There's a seven-second delay on everything, and a censor with his hand on a switch. Above him is a duty director who can cut the whole floor from the Exchange. I can get you into the chair. Guests are my business. I can't keep you on air once you open your mouth. Thirty seconds, if we're lucky."

"I need twenty minutes."

"I know what you need." She tapped the plan. "There's a backup transmitter on the hundred and ninetieth. It's there for typhoons. It isn't on the delay, and it can't be cut from below. It takes three people to bring it up. They'd all have to be willing to lose their jobs and very likely go to prison."

"Do you have three people?"

"I have a sound engineer whose brother was on a ship the Breakers sank. He wants to know why they're sinking ships. I have a floor manager who's Shallowborn, and nobody knows it but me." She glanced at Ilan. "That's the word for you, too, isn't it? One parent from each side. There are more of them than you'd think. They wear high collars." She went on. "And I have a transmitter technician who is simply very tired of lying." She rolled up the plan. "It isn't much of a conspiracy. It's four people who work in a building."

"That's what the sea was short of," Ilan said.

"There's something else, and I don't like it." She took a card from inside her oilskin. It was thick cream paper, with a blue crest. "Caspar Venn owns the Spire. He owns me, come to that. He's sent word by three different people that he wants out. He says he has read the tonnage too. He'll give you the main channel, with no delay and no censor, for as long as you like. He says he'll introduce you himself."

"Do you believe him?"

"I've worked for him eleven years. He has never given away a thing he could charge for." She turned the card over. "But it takes you in by the front door, under your own name, and it puts every screen on Ovara on you from the first word. So we say yes. And my three people bring the backup up anyway, and I watch what actually goes out."

"He'll have asked the Exchange," Ilan said.

"He asks the Exchange what to have for lunch."

"One more thing. You'll be a hundred and forty floors up, in a glass box. I'm told you can do remarkable things with water." She glanced at Sefa, who had come up the loft ladder dripping and was sitting on the top rung, staring at the silver eyelids. "There's a jug on the desk. That's all the water there'll be."

"I know," he said. "I won't need it."

Marit stayed that night. She would not sleep in the loft. She sat on the quay in the rain with a village blanket round her, looking at the water, and after a while Sefa went and sat down beside her. They were there a long time. Ilan did not hear what was said. In the morning Marit's eyes were red. She looked at Sefa, and at Sefa's hands, as though they were something on which a great deal had been spent.

"Six years," she said to Ilan, on the smack. "Six years I've read out that they don't exist. She told me about her friend. The net-mender." She pulled her hood up. "Don't waste my three people, Mr Voss."

---

Concord was three small islands joined by bridges, and nobody was poor there.

They were called the Sovereigns. Two hundred years ago the trading houses had fought their wars to a standstill and found they needed somewhere to sign the peace. They had made the three islands neutral ground. No corporation owned them. All five kept their boards there. The towers were glass from the pavement up. The streets were washed twice a day. There were trees in the squares, real ones, each with a brass plate and a guard.

Ilan had kept a flat here. He had walked these pavements in good shoes, thinking about tonnage.

Screens the size of buildings hung between the towers. That week every one of them showed the same footage. A freighter was burning on a dark sea. A line of text ran under it, about sea terrorists, and the protective fleet that would sail to deal with them. People stood in the squares with their faces turned up. Some of them cheered when the fleet was shown.

The Orrin Spire was the tallest thing on Ovara. It stood at the centre of the middle island. It was a needle of blue glass, so high that its top was lost in cloud. At its foot, taking up an entire block, stood a building with no windows at all. It was grey and smooth, and it had one door.

"The Exchange," Marit said, not looking at it. She was in her own clothes now, and her own face, and people turned to watch her pass. "Meridian Assurance. Orrin tells people what to think. Meridian's what happens if they don't. Every debt in the world is in there. Every indenture, every mortgage on every island. Every loan the other four have ever taken to build a ship. When two companies go to war, Meridian lends to both and insures both. It hires out the soldiers as well." She walked a little faster. "Don't stare. It notices."

She took him in by the front, on Caspar Venn's card. Teodora Raske came with him, carrying a document case. She had put on lipstick for it, and looked furious.

Venn met them himself, on the blue marble of the lobby. He was a slender, silver-haired man of sixty with a beautiful voice, and he held Ilan's hand in both of his. He said that it was a brave thing, and long overdue. He said that Orrin Signal was proud to be the house that told the truth at last. He said it very well. He had been saying things well for forty years.

The lift went up for a long time. His ears hurt. He felt the sea go from him, floor by floor, as it had in the Brightwater tower, but further. By the hundredth floor it was a whisper. By the hundred and fortieth there was nothing. He had not been so alone since the Slick.

They put him in a small white room with a mirror ringed in bulbs. A girl put powder on his face. She chattered about the weather. Her hands were quick and kind. He thought, *she is one of forty million, and nobody has ever told her anything true.*

Marit came in and shut the door. She stood behind his chair and looked at him in the mirror.

"I'll give you the speech," she said. "The short one. I wanted to ask you whether you ever thought about them. At all. Whether it kept you up." She met his eyes in the glass. "I had a whole second part, for when you said no."

"I didn't think about them," Ilan said. "I slept very well. That's the thing I can't get over."

She was silent. Then she nodded, once.

"That's worse than what I'd imagined," she said. "Good. Say it like that. Don't dress it." She straightened his collar, impersonally, like a nurse. "Two minutes. The man with the clipboard is called Bertil. When he looks at the clock, keep talking."

He was walked down a corridor, into the cold bright box of Studio One. There was a desk and two chairs, and a wall of glass with the clouds beyond it. He sat down, and looked into a lens.

A hundred and forty floors below him was the sea, and he could not feel it at all.

Venn took the second chair himself. He had insisted on it. He looked into the lens with his grave, handsome face, and said that tonight Orrin One would set aside its schedule. A man believed dead had come forward with a confession, he said, and the islands should hear it without interruption. He turned to Ilan with an open hand.

"My name is Ilan Voss," Ilan said. "I was Chief Surveyor of Halcyon Dredge & Mineral. The company told you I drowned. I'd like to tell you why I didn't."

Nobody cut him off. The red light stayed on. He talked for six minutes, and Venn nodded gravely beside him. It was going so smoothly that the back of his neck had begun to crawl.

Marit was not looking at him. She stood behind the camera with a monitor in her hand, the small kind that the floor staff carried. She had gone white under the silver.

She turned it toward him.

It showed Studio One, and his own face, seven seconds behind. His lips were moving. The words under them were not the ones he had said. *I lifted the barge. I gave the order. Twelve ships.* It was his voice. It was being cut out of this very speech, a phrase here and a phrase there, as fast as he gave them. Then the picture changed. It was a valley in a kelp forest, seen from the surface through a long lens. Twelve grey hulls went under together. There were men in the water. A line of text ran beneath: VOSS, FORMERLY OF HALCYON, CLAIMS COMMAND OF SEA TERRORIST FORCES. ADMITS MASSACRE OF MERIDIAN PATROL. 161 DEAD.

*A boat with a glass eye on a mast,* Corin had said. *It was there all morning.*

"I am so sorry," Caspar Venn said, quite gently, with his hand over his microphone. He had seen the monitor. "It was put to me as a matter of the public safety. And he does hold all my notes, you know."

Marit looked past the camera at the floor manager. He was a heavy, quiet man with a clipboard, in a high collar. He was looking at the clock.

The big screen on the studio wall broke up, and came back. The picture was grainier, and it had no caption. It showed the studio from a different angle. Ilan was in his chair. Caspar Venn of Orrin Signal was half out of the other, with his mouth open and one arm stretched toward a man in a glass booth.

"Backup's up," the floor manager said. "No delay. That's what's going out now, everywhere, and that's the chairman's face in the middle of it. Go on, sir."

Venn sat down again, slowly. He seemed to be trying to decide what expression to wear. He had not found one when the camera left him.

Ilan went on.

He did it as he had done it at Tidehall, with no explanations. He gave his name and his grade. Raske opened the case and held the sheets up to the lens one after another. Her hands were perfectly steady. They were the eleven charts, with their red lines. They gave the tonnage, by the year, and what was in it.

"This is what your islands put into the sea every night," he said, "where you were told nobody lives. People live there. There are two million of them. They breathe water, and they farm kelp. They've been netting your filth with their bare hands for two hundred years. These are the arms of a healer called Ione." It was a photograph Dace had taken at Tidehall. "I did that. I never met her. I did it with a pen. I slept very well."

He showed them the order for the charges on Low Anthem, and said the number twenty-one. He said some of the names. He told them what had lifted the barge, and that it was him.

"You were shown twelve ships a minute ago," he said. "That was real too. Nobody made that picture up. They were taking a mariner town in trawl nets. Nine hundred people, to be put in tanks on a Meridian shore and worked for twenty years. I tried to stop it, and I couldn't, and I was frightened. So I did what I was trained to do. I gave an order. A hundred and sixty-one of your sons and daughters died of it, in about the time it takes to shut a door. I looked at every one of them afterwards. I have their names, from the ones who lived. I'll read them to anybody who asks me." He kept his eyes on the lens. "I'm not the leader of anything. I'm a man who has now killed people both of the ways there are. With a pen, and with a word. I slept very well after the first. I haven't slept since the second."

"Now the ships," he said. "You've been shown ships burning. They're real. There are mariners called Breakers who have stopped being patient, and I can't tell you they've no cause. But ask yourselves why they started this year. I'll tell you. The one man who kept their cities alive was killed four months ago, and their lamps are going out. And then there's this."

It was the last sheet. Raske held it up for a long time.

"This is a Maker engine. Halcyon took it from a drowned city under the Girdle. I led that dig. It turns seawater to steam, as much as you point it at. Kessler-Vane built the hull to carry it. This lady designed that hull. She walked out when she learned what it was for. The fleet you're cheering sails in two days to boil the northern holds. There are twelve thousand people in one of them. I mended its air myself.

"And the man who holds every debt on these islands wants that engine for something larger. His name is Anselm Rook. He's the chairman of Meridian Assurance. You have never once been shown his face. Ask why."

There was a noise at the studio door.

"I'm not asking you to forgive me," Ilan said to the lens. "The mariners haven't, and they're right. I'm asking you to look. I signed these. I'm showing you my own name. Go down to the waterline in Saltgate, or Clearwell, or the Crown. There's a mariner healer there with two basins, mending your children for nothing. Ask her what she is."

The door came in.

There were eight of them, in Meridian grey, with a captain. They had weapons. They spread out along the wall as men do who have done it before. In the booth, the censor had his hands on his head.

"Off," the captain said. "Cut it. Now."

"I can't," said Marit pleasantly. "It isn't in this room. It isn't on this floor." She had picked up the small camera from the desk and was pointing it at him. The red light on it was on. "You're live, Captain. So is your face. To every screen on Ovara, including the one in your mother's kitchen. What would you like to do?"

Nobody did anything.

Ilan saw the floor manager step, without any fuss, into the line between the guns and the chair. He was unbuttoning his collar as he did it. The sound engineer came out of his corner and stood beside him. Then the girl who had powdered Ilan's face came, and a man with a cable over his shoulder.

That was how Orrin Signal fell. It took perhaps ten seconds, and nobody raised a hand.

The big screen on the wall broke up. A new picture came through from an Orrin news boat. Whoever was running the transmitter fifty floors up had patched it in without being asked.

It was a harbour. Black cliffs ringed it, and a city was stacked up inside them. Beyond the breakwater, the horizon was in the wrong place. It was too high. There was a long green line standing where the sea should have been flat. It was miles off, and the evening sun was shining through it.

Words crawled under it. PORT HALCYON. SEA LEVEL ANOMALY. And beneath that, a second line: HALCYON FLEET SAILED 0400 TODAY, TWO DAYS AHEAD OF SCHEDULE.

Somebody in the studio made a small sound.

*Ten days, perhaps twelve*, Okonta had said. It had been six. He saw now why. Hesper had stood in a ring of three hundred callers at Tidehall, and felt how strength could be passed from hand to hand. Before that she had stood at the edge of a silent valley, and watched how quickly a thing can be done by somebody who does not ask. She had taken both away with her.

He stood up.

"That wave was raised by mariners," he said to Marit's camera. "Twelve of them. I know the woman who leads them. Halcyon killed her husband. If it falls, two million of you die. Then your fleet boils two million of them. After that nobody will be left who remembers how any of it began." He was already unclipping the microphone. "I can stop it. I think I can stop both. I have to go now."

The captain had not moved from the door. He was a man of about fifty. He was looking at the screen, at the tiered city under the green line. His face had gone the colour of putty.

"My daughter's at the refinery school," he said. "On the waterline."

"Then let me by," said Ilan.

The captain stood aside.

## Chapter 14. The Wave

The lift took four minutes to reach the ground. He stood in it with Teodora Raske and felt the sea come back to him, floor by floor, like blood into a numb hand.

In the lobby nobody stopped them. A hundred people stood on the blue marble with their faces turned up to the screens. A woman had her hand over her mouth. Outside, the washed streets of Concord were full, and nobody was moving. The screens between the towers showed the green line on the horizon, and the black city under it. They showed nothing else.

A man in a good coat turned from one of them and saw Ilan. He opened his mouth. Then he stepped back, off the pavement, to let him by. Others turned. They made a lane for him the whole length of the avenue, in silence. He walked down it with an old woman in lipstick at his elbow, and he did not know whether they would cheer him or tear him apart. Neither did they.

"I'm for Lashing," Raske said, at the seawall. Dace's boat was there, with the engine running. "I've three hundred people there who can open a lamp, and I think you're going to want them." She gripped his arm, hard. "Don't die. You've not finished teaching me."

He had called Okonta from the service stair of the Spire, through the Thread, the moment he could feel anything at all. He had not known he could do that. She came up out of the harbour with both needles as he reached the wall. Sefa slid off Dace's deck and onto the needle behind her, with Bother under one arm, and nobody had the time to argue.

"It can't be done," she said.

They were running north already, flat out, through the tail of a squall.

"The Crown's three thousand kilometres east of here, at the far end of the islands. A needle would take two days, and you've got hours. The only thing fast enough is the Dragon. And it's a thousand kilometres off, on the southern Shoulder." She was shouting over the hull-song. "It doesn't come to the Girdle, Wrack. You saw. It goes under, a kilometre down, and it doesn't turn aside for anything. It runs pole to pole and nowhere else. It's run that line since before there were holds."

"It's coming," Ilan said. "I asked it."

He had felt it turn. He was still feeling it. Far to the south-west a long bright hum was bending off a line it had held for three thousand years. He had rebuilt that heart himself, on the floor of the gallery, with a strand of lampweed round his wrist. He knew every wheel in it. It knew him.

That was what the Thread was for. Jef had seen it. The old man had sat on a step with a mug, with his hands shaking and his heart failing. He had looked at a stranger who could push the Dragon's water about from outside, and he had thought: *he ought to be able to drive it.* He had spent the last of himself on that.

Ilan knew what he was doing. He had turned it over on a frigate's deck at Lashing, and on most nights since. There was one heart on Ovara large enough. For three thousand years it had run where no islander could lay a hand on it. He was bringing it up into the light. There were two million people under a wave, and he could not think of another way to reach them.

It came out of the rain at sunrise like a white road.

It was on the surface. Nobody living had seen that. The great ribs rose out of the swell in a line a kilometre long, each trailing a mane of spray, and the bound water in them blazed in the early light. It did not stop for him this time. He did not ask it to. He put the needle alongside at full speed and went aboard as the Threaded do. He laid himself along its brow with both palms on the warm metal. He stopped being a man riding something.

He was a kilometre long. He was faster than weather. The Dragon had never been driven before, only left to run. Driven, it went at twice the pace it had ever kept. The sea parted round his head and closed behind his tail, and he could feel all of it. He felt every wave and every keel for a hundred kilometres. He felt the storms as a pressure on his back.

Okonta was beside him, flying escort. Other needles were dropping in behind her, out of the squalls, one by one. There were nine of them, then fifteen. They were Threaded who had gone home when Jef died. They had sat in dark holds and listened to a broadcast, passed down from the tide line by long-call. They had made up their minds. He knew one of them by the hum of his needle. It was the young man who had gone to Fallow, to his mother.

The Dragon ran east along the Girdle.

It was the only time he ever saw the islands as a whole. They went by on either hand, one after another, out of the rain and into it again. He saw the Green Terraces, high and wet, with Clearwell's white steps climbing through the cloud. He saw the orange sky over Keelhaven, and its sirens were all sounding at once. He passed Saltgate on its black pillars. The neon was dark, for the first time in its history. Every roof and gantry and stilt of it was crowded with people, and so were the open locks, and at the waterline he saw the lamps of the Wet Market held up in hands.

He passed Lashing in its Eye. Six thousand hulls rode there under a ring of blue sky. All along the barge rails there were lights. They were white and steady, hundreds of them, and they were not lanterns.

Fishing fleets hauled their nets and stood to watch. Children were held up on shoulders. All along its road, the people of the islands came out to see the mariners' god go by, off its course, with a man lying on its head.

He reached Port Halcyon at sunset.

---

The Crown was a ring of volcanoes. They were black and bare and steep, and ore showed in them in rust-red veins. Smoke stood over them. The largest had blown its side out long ago, and the sea had come in. That was the harbour. The city was inside.

He had lived in it for fifteen years, and had never once seen it from the water.

It climbed the inner wall of the caldera in tiers. At the bottom was the Waterline. That was refineries and quays and tank farms, and the barracks of the people who worked them. It lay under a brown haze, lit by gas flares. Above it were the Stacks. They were a thousand grey tenement blocks, built so close that the streets between them were stairs. Two million people lived there. And at the top, along the crater's lip, was the Rim. It had gardens, and white houses, and clean air. He could see his own office window. Ash was falling over all of it, soft and grey, as it fell most days. It lay on the roofs of the Stacks like dirty snow. On the Rim, men were paid to sweep it.

He could hear the sirens. Under them was a sound he knew. It was two million people with nowhere to go. On Ovara there has never been anywhere to go.

The wave was made.

It stood a kilometre off the harbour mouth. It was two hundred metres high and nine kilometres long. It was a wall of green water with a white lip, quite still, and the last light of the day was shining through it. He could see fish in it. Before it hung twelve small figures with their arms raised.

He left the Dragon lying across the harbour mouth and went forward alone.

A needle came out of the shadow of the wave to meet him. He knew the hull.

He had his arm's length of sea about him, and this time he was ready. He did not need it. Okonta went past his shoulder like a thrown knife. The two needles met prow to prow, and hung there, turning.

"Get out of his road, Corin," she said.

"She asked me to keep him off until it's done." Corin's broad face was grey with weariness. "It's the only thing any of them has ever understood. You said so yourself once, in the kelp. Twenty years ago."

"I did. Where did he put your sister?"

"What?"

"The grey man. Which shore? You never knew. I flew in over it just now." She pointed past him, with her whole arm, at the foot of the black city. "Look at the refinery quay. Under the flares. Those sheds with the tin roofs."

He looked. Ilan looked too. Along the Waterline, behind the sea-wall, stood row upon row of long low tanks. There must have been two hundred. Pipes ran down from them into the harbour.

"That's where Kelder is," Okonta said. "And three holds more. Eleven thousand of ours, in orphan water, at the bottom of that hill. He has put them under your wave. He'll have known for months where it would fall."

Corin did not say anything. His needle drifted a little, as a thing does when nobody is steering it.

Then he turned it and went in through the harbour mouth, flat out, toward the tin roofs. She let him go.

Up close he saw what Okonta had meant. Each of the twelve held a part. There was a rib of the wave, and a root, and a shoulder. Each was balanced against the others like the stones of an arch. Their arms were trembling. Their faces were grey. They had been holding it since yesterday.

"Wrack," Hesper said. Her voice was raw. She did not turn her head; she could not. "Go away."

"Hesper—"

"I mean it. You've seen what they do. You saw the Slick. You were *in* my hold." Her breath came in gasps. "They sailed, did you know? Yesterday morning. With the engine. They were always going to. So don't tell me about children."

"The fleet's been recalled. It's coming back here to shoot at you. I've perhaps half an hour." He swam round until he was in front of her, between her and the city. "Look at me. I drew the line over Low Anthem. Me. Not that city. It was my chart and my pen. If somebody has to be under this when it comes down, I'm here. I won't move."

Her eyes came to his face. They were bloodshot, and wet.

"I can't forgive you."

"I know. I'm not asking you to."

"And I can't let go." It burst out of her. "Don't you see? None of us can! We built it too big. We learned something from you at Tidehall. How to hand strength on, one to the next. And we learned something at Fallow. We didn't ask for this, Wrack. Each of us told our piece of it to stand. It went up like a wall going up. Six days, and we were so proud. Now Calla's failing on the left root, and Brin's been bleeding from the ears since noon. If one of us drops, the lot goes, on them and on us. And it won't hear us. We told it to stand, and it's standing, and it has stopped listening. I've been trying for three hours to think how to put it down. There's no way to put it down!"

So it was not vengeance holding the wave up, in the end. It was twelve exhausted people who could find no way to stop.

He thought, quite clearly, *I know what that's like.*

"Then give it to me," Ilan said.

"You can't carry—"

"I'm not going to carry it. I'm going to be a place for it to pass through. You've felt me do it." He opened his hands. "One at a time. Calla first. Whatever you can't hold. I'll only take what I'm given."

Nobody moved. Then, on the left, a grey-faced woman gave a sob, and let go.

The water of the wave was deaf. He could not have asked it for anything. But it was not the water he took. It was twelve wills that were holding it up, and those a person could carry.

He felt her part of it come into him and go through. He kept none of it. He gave it down into the sea, and it spread away. The wave shivered along its whole length, and held. Then Brin gave his, and the next, and the next. Each time a little more of the green wall slumped at the foot, quite quietly. Hesper was the last. She looked at him for a long moment over what was left of it.

"Ask," she whispered. "Do not order."

"Please," Ilan said to the sea.

The wave lay down. It went back into the ocean like a sheet smoothed over a bed. A swell no higher than a child passed under the breakwater. It lifted the fishing boats, once, at their moorings.

He heard the city. It was a sound like wind.

Hesper had folded up in the water with her arms round her knees. Davin had said that it was boys on those boats. Ilan thought that she was hearing him.

Then the horizon flashed.

---

They had come round the eastern head while he was not looking. There were forty ships. In the middle of them, riding low, was a vast grey unfinished hull with scaffolding still on its upperworks. Light was building in a slot along its bow, too white to look at.

They had seen a man standing in their harbour mouth beside the Dragon. A wave that could kill a city had lain down at his word. They did what frightened people with a weapon do.

The first shot was not aimed. It went wide of him by half a kilometre. A line of white ran across the sea, and the water under it turned to a wall of steam with a crack like the sky breaking. A fishing boat at the edge of it was gone.

He reached for the sea to shield the city, and there was nothing.

He knew the feeling at once. It was the feeling from the Slick. There was no door, and no house. He looked down. The water of Port Halcyon harbour was the colour of tea, and an oily sheen slid on it. The whole of it was deaf, from the breakwater to the refinery quays. It was two hundred years of what ran off those tiers. Some of it was his.

The light in the slot was building again. He could feel the engine through the Thread. It was a hot hard knot of Maker work, and it was badly wrong. They were turning it toward the harbour mouth, and toward everything behind it.

*You can't mend anything you won't look at,* Yeva had said. *Purging is your one.*

He stopped trying to call. He put both hands into the filthy water, as he had once held a cupful in a canal in Saltgate. He looked at it. He thought of the harbour as a sick body. It was a wound that somebody had left to rot. He wanted nothing for himself from it at all. He did not want safety, or even to be forgiven. He asked the sea to *care*.

It began at his hands. Clear water spread from them like a stain running backward, faster and faster. The filth came out of it. It came up in threads and ropes, and then in great black skeins, from the mud and the pilings and the hulls. It was two centuries of it. It gathered above the harbour in a turning sphere, as dark as a hole in the evening. He set it down on the concrete of the Halcyon refinery quay, behind its own sea-wall, where it had come from.

Port Halcyon harbour was clean to the bottom. People on the Waterline were looking down at their own sea floor for the first time in their lives. There were things lying on it that the company had said were not there.

And it could hear him.

The second shot came straight. He raised the harbour to meet it. It was a wall of clean sea a kilometre thick, between the beam and the city. The beam struck it, and boiled into it, and guttered. In the same moment he reached out along the Thread with the craft, across eleven kilometres, into a machine he had once stood inside. It was seated wrong. It had been dragged out of its vault and bolted into a hull by people who thought it was a machine like theirs.

He moved three small things.

The light in the slot went out.

After that he lifted the fleet. He was so tired that he could barely frame the asking, and he asked very simply. Forty warships rose out of the sea on forty pillars of water. He carried them in over the breakwater, with their crews clinging to the rails. He set them down in the berths they had sailed from, one by one, as gently as he had ever done anything. Nobody was hurt. Among the faces at the rail of a destroyer he saw a lieutenant with her helmet off. Her lips were moving, and he knew what the word was.

On the bridge of the unfinished hull a man in a grey coat stood quite still, with his hands at his sides. Adler Crane had taught him to play cards. He had said he was sorry, and had seemed to mean it. He was looking at Ilan across the water as a man looks at a bill that has come due.

Ilan let the water go. He was on his knees on nothing, on the sea, and the cold had hold of him to the waist. It was the plain cold of being ebbed, and not the other. He thought that this time it would last a week.

It was very quiet. Every camera Orrin owned was on him. Small arms came round his chest from behind, and something whiskered shoved its nose into his ear.

"That'll do," Sefa said fiercely. "That's enough. You don't ask for one more thing. Do you hear me?"

He meant to obey her. He was sliding down into the dark already, and it was almost pleasant.

Then something rang in his head, faintly, as a glass rings when another is set down beside it.

The sea was shut to him. But the Thread was not the gift, and it had never been shut. He could still feel the lamps of the Rim, far up, and the dead knot of the engine. He could feel the Dragon lying across the harbour mouth behind him, a kilometre of slow bright hum.

A needle had come into one of its cradles. It was an old pattern.

## Chapter 15. The Heart

He knew which cradle. It was the last in the row, the one with the square name cut beside it.

He turned in the water. Sefa's arms were still round his chest, holding him up, and she cried out at him to be still. The Dragon lay across the mouth of the harbour where he had left it. Its ribs stood out of the swell in a long line against the sunset, each with its mane of bright water. He had ridden on its brow that morning and been a kilometre long. He reached for it now as he would have reached for his own hand.

Someone else had hold of it.

It was not like the fight in the gallery. There was no blow. There was only a pressure, steady and patient, like a man leaning his weight on the other side of a door. Ilan knew every wheel in that heart. He had laid them out on the floor in order and put them back with his own hands. But the other had ridden the Dragon before Okonta was born. He had lain in its gallery as a young man, and listened to it, year after year, with the best head of the sixty. He could not have mended a hinge on it. He knew it as a blind man knows a house.

The Dragon's note wavered. The great blunt head swung in toward the harbour, toward Ilan, and stopped. The bound water flared white along its whole length. He heard the ribs groan.

*Stay,* he asked it. *Please. Stay.*

It was all he had. He was ebbed to the bone, and shaking, and the Thread wanted attention as a needle wants it. He had none left to give. He held the door with his fingertips.

Above the city, the big screens changed.

They showed a crest he had seen on a building with no windows. A voice spoke out of every wall in Port Halcyon. It was speaking, in that moment, out of every wall on every island. It was a calm voice, and unhurried, and Ilan had heard it across a table once a year since he was sixteen.

It said that in view of the extraordinary situation, Meridian Assurance was exercising its rights under all outstanding instruments. Every debt was called. Citizens, houses, cities and corporations had thirty days to pay, in scrip or in service. It said that service would be accepted at any Meridian door. And it said that in consideration of that service, the house would issue to every household that gave it a deed of ground. The ground was new. It would be made available within the season. There would be enough.

"What does that mean?" Sefa said.

He could not answer her. The door was going.

Okonta had seen the Dragon's head swing. She did not ask what it meant. She went for the cradles with fifteen needles behind her, in a silver line, low over the water.

They never reached them. Grey needles came out of the smoke round the eastern head, where forty warships lay canted in their berths. There were twenty of them and more. They had been waiting there all afternoon, behind the fleet. They met the silver line over the harbour mouth, in the last of the light, and every camera Orrin owned was pointing at it.

Ilan saw her break through. She went under two of them and over a third. She reached the Dragon's brow and struck the place where a panel had once slid open for a stranger, like an eyelid. It did not open. *He tells it who to let in,* a boy had said in a dark hall, a long time ago. She beat on it with her fist.

He did not let go. He was sure of that afterwards. There was simply nothing left to hold with.

The Dragon turned its head to the open sea.

It went out past the breakwater along the white road it had come by, with twenty grey needles flying round it like gulls round a trawler. He felt it run west. He felt it for a hundred kilometres, and two hundred, a long bright hum going away. Then it tilted, and went down.

"He's drafting them," he said. His lips were numb. "All forty million. And he's paying them in ground."

"What ground? There isn't any."

"Yours," Ilan said. "The Shelves. The south of the forest. I brought it to his door, Sefa. I brought it up for him."

He did not hear what she said to that.

He slept for three days.

He knew very little about them. Somebody carried him. There was a stair, and a smell of chalk and wet wool. He was colder than he had been at Low Anthem, and dry to the bone. The sea was shut to him. Where the Dragon had been, at the back of his mind, there was a place like the socket of a tooth. Once he woke enough to see Sefa asleep on the boards beside him, with Bother across her feet. Once there was a tall grey woman bending over him, with cool dry fingers on his neck. She said to somebody, *He's wrung out like a cloth. Leave him.*

When he woke properly it was morning, and he was lying under a blackboard.

It was a schoolroom. Small desks had been pushed back against the wall. Children's drawings were pinned along it in a row. They were of ships, mostly, with a great deal of smoke. Through the window he could see the harbour, and the tiers of the Stacks climbing away above it into the ash. The water was clear to the bottom. People stood all along the quays, looking down into it, as they must have stood for three days.

"It's the refinery school," Sefa said. "On the Waterline. A man in Meridian grey carried you up here. He came from Concord on the night packet. He said his daughter's in the top form. He's standing outside the door with a gun, and he won't say who he's guarding you from." She put a blanket round him. "Don't sit up. Yes, you're going to. Slowly, then. I'm to tell you in order, Yeva says, or you'll get excited."

Yeva was at the window. She had come off her road, and fifteen other Wellwalkers with her. She did not turn round.

"You're ebbed worse than I've seen in a living man," she said. "It'll be a month. If you call anything larger than a bathtub before then, you'll go under, and it won't be your memory this time. It'll be all of you. I'd tell you to rest, if I thought you could hear the word."

Sefa told him.

The Dragon had run west along the Girdle and gone down in the open sea, at a place where a drilling platform stood on six legs. Okonta had followed it as far as she could. She had come back with eleven needles of her fifteen.

"She says it's lying in a ring on the sea floor, round something. She says it isn't singing." Sefa hesitated. "She says the hum went out of it on the second day. As if something had been taken out."

"Two wheels," Ilan said. "As wide as a table."

"And the sea's going out."

He looked at her.

"There. Round the platform. It's standing back. Okonta saw it. A wall of water, in a ring, grey, quite still, and bare mud inside it. On the first day it was a kilometre across. It's twelve now. It jumps. It stands a while, and then it goes out another kilometre, all at once." She was twisting the hem of the blanket. "Dace sent word from Saltgate. The mariners have stopped coming in through the locks. The Lockkeepers sent a boat out to look at the wall, and now they read the distance to it from the gate boxes every morning. They've never done that. And Odile sent a ray. She wants to know how far the dead water will come."

"And round the wall? Outside it?"

Sefa did not answer.

"Deaf," said Yeva, from the window. "Sixty kilometres out, when I came through, and growing. I felt it from the smack. It was like walking into a room where somebody has just died." She turned at last. Her long worn face was hard. "Aurel of Deep Chorus has sent a long-call. The holds on the southern edge of the forest have stopped answering their evening song."

He shut his eyes. He saw a soup bowl on a table, and the water standing up round the rim.

"What are the islands doing?"

"Queueing," Sefa said. "At the grey doors. Marit's still got the transmitter, and she shows the harbour all day, and you, and the Makers' engine. But there are speakers on every wall that aren't hers. They read out the deeds. So many paces by so many paces, on the new ground, with your family's name on it." She looked at her hands. "People are *glad*, Wrack. Not all of them. A woman came to this door yesterday and kissed your blanket. Another one spat at the guard, and said you'd come to take her children's ground away before they'd ever stood on it."

There was a knock, a heavy one. The door opened a hand's width.

"There's a big docker here from Saltgate," the man in grey said. "With a little girl. He says he knows you."

---

Garran had come along the Girdle on a coal lighter. He stood just inside the schoolroom door with Pim on his arm, turning his cap in his free hand. He looked at the small desks as though they might break.

"I'll not stop," he said. "I can see how you are. I only came to ask you a thing. I've asked everyone else, and they all know the answer, and they all know a different one."

"Ask."

"They read my name out, on the Sluice. Garran, porter, and one dependent. A deed. Forty paces by twenty, they said, with the right to build." He said the figures as a man says a prayer he has only just learned. "I've never stood on a thing that was mine. Nor my father. We've paid rent on a plank since there was a Saltgate. And she'd have it after me." He looked down at the child, who was staring at Bother. "I saw what you did here. I saw the ships put to bed. I'd follow you into the water, and I can't swim. But I've got to tell her something. So you tell me. Is it true? Is there ground?"

"Yes," Ilan said. "It's true. He can do it."

"And you're against it."

"The ground's the floor of somebody else's house. He takes the sea off it, and everything that lived there dies, and so does the water for a hundred kilometres round. The people who were under it go into tanks, and work twenty years for the passage." He made himself go on. "And the deed's his, Garran. You'd stand on it for as long as you paid. You'd have swapped a plank for a field, and it'd be the same landlord."

Garran thought about it. He was not a quick man, and he did not pretend to be.

"That's so," he said at last. "I know that. I think I knew it on the Sluice." He settled Pim higher on his arm. "It's still a field. That's the trouble. You've told me what's wrong with his. You've not told me where there's another."

"No," Ilan said. "I haven't."

"When you can, I'll listen. So will a good many." He put his cap on. "She wanted to see the hound."

Pim was lifted down. She and Bother regarded each other, with great seriousness, for a minute or so. Then Garran took her away down the stair.

---

Adler Crane came that afternoon, between two fishwives.

He had walked off the unfinished hull on the first evening, they said, and given himself up to the first person he met on the quay. It had been the larger fishwife. She had not known what to do with him. She had kept him in her net-store for three days and fed him, and now she had brought him along.

He was a spare, grey, tidy man in a grey coat. He had taught Ilan to play cards. He stood in front of the blackboard with his hands at his sides.

"I asked him twice," he said.

"I know. You told me at the rail."

"I wanted to be sure you'd heard. There was a good deal of wind." He cleared his throat. "I'd like to tell you what I know. After that you may do what you like with me. I shan't make difficulties."

He told it as he had always given his reports, in order, with the figures.

The engine was in two parts. Halcyon had been given the lance, which was the part that could be pointed. The other part had never left the city. It was a ring, forty metres across, set into the floor of the lowest vault, and it was that which told the sea where it might not be. The chairman had kept it for himself. He had let the fleet sail with the lance for one reason. He had wished to see what Ilan would do to it.

"You shut it off from eleven kilometres," Crane said. "He had three men on that hull with glasses, watching the housing, to see what you moved. They couldn't tell him. I was there when he heard. He wasn't angry. He said, *then I shall have to run it as it is.*"

"It's seated wrong."

"He knows. It runs rough. That's why the wall goes out in jumps. His own engineers have told him it won't hold steady." Crane looked at the floor. "They've told him something else. If it's stopped all at once, or if it fails, the wall comes down. All of it, in one piece. There's a great deal of water in a ring sixty metres high and forty kilometres round, and it grows every day. They say it would go over every island on the Girdle."

Nobody in the schoolroom spoke.

"So it can't be broken," Yeva said.

"No, madam. He has thought of that. He generally has."

He gave the rest. There were two thousand Meridian soldiers on the platform and in ships at the rim. There were twenty-two Threaded. The heart of the Dragon had been lifted out through the gallery floor, on the second day, and carried down into the vault.

"One more thing," Crane said. "He's kept your office. On the Rim. He's had the bluewood table polished every week since you went into the water. I thought you ought to know he wasn't pretending. He never does pretend. I used to think that was a virtue."

"Why did you give yourself up?"

"I stood on that bridge and watched you put forty ships to bed without cracking a cup." For the first time the tidy voice was not quite steady. "I'd thrown that into the sea. With a cable tie on it. I should like to be of some use to somebody before they hang me."

Ilan looked at him for some time. He thought of an old man with his feet in the water. *Teach people you don't like.*

"I've no use for a hanging," he said. "Can you open a seam?"

"I can't open a tin."

"Then you'll learn. You'll sit in the third row and you'll do as you're told." He turned his face to the wall. "Somebody find him something to eat."

He tried to get up at dusk, and the floor came to meet him.

Sefa got him back onto the blanket. She did it with a practised arm, and a look that Ione might have worn.

"I can't call," he said. "A month, she says. He'll have dried another hundred kilometres by then. I can't reach the Dragon. There's nothing there to reach. I can't break the thing, or it drowns everyone I broke it for. I can't order." He laughed, and it hurt his ribs. "I promised you. And the Moot. And if I did, I'd only deafen whatever he's left."

"No," Sefa said. "You can't." She sat down beside him and held her hands out, palms up, in the last of the light from the window. "Nor can I. We're a fine pair."

He looked at her hands. They were thin and brown, and there was a new scar across one thumb. They had closed a porter's child's lungs in the time it takes to boil an egg.

"Garran asked me where there's another," he said.

"I heard."

"Rook asked me the same. At the table. *And the forty million?* I said I'd find them somewhere. He said that Jef never knew either." He was looking at the drawings on the wall. One of them was not a ship. It was a house, with a door, and a figure at each side of it. One figure was standing on a green line, and the other on a blue. "I do know. I've known for a year. I walked into it in a hard suit, with a lamp."

"The city."

"Dry halls for a million. Wet halls for a million more. Streets that run down into canals, and canals that run out to sea. It was built for people who did both. He's sitting in the middle of it, Sefa, and all he can see is the vault. He's using it for a pump-house."

"It's under forty metres of mud. And he's taken the sea away from it."

"It was built to come up." He had not known it until he said it. Then he knew it as he had known that a coupling would run cooler backwards. "Jef told me. Nothing they made was meant to stand still. It all wants to go from somewhere to somewhere. That ring isn't a wall. I've stood in it. They built it when the sea began to rise. They didn't mean to keep the sea out for ever. Nobody who thought like water would have meant that. They meant to lift the city *with* it. A little at a time, as the sea came up. Something went wrong, or they quarrelled, or they were afraid, and they shut it behind three doors." He was breathing hard. "It's seated wrong. That's all. It's been seated wrong for three thousand years."

"Then seat it right."

"I can't lift a bathtub."

"Who can?"

"Nobody." He stopped. "Everybody. It would want every caller there is, passing it hand to hand, as at Tidehall. It would want healers, thousands of them, if that dead water can be brought back at all. Nobody's ever tried. It would want Teo's three hundred, to wake the place street by street. And boats to carry them, and the islands to send the boats." He looked at her. "Both peoples. All at once. And I can't order one of them to come."

"No," said Sefa. "You'll have to ask."

"I'm bad at it."

"You're terrible at it. You were bad at the gate latch, too." She pulled the blanket up to his chin, and tucked it in, not gently. "You'll get practice. Go to sleep. We'll start in the morning."

# Part Six: The Turning

## Chapter 16. The Long Way Round

He began at the bottom of the hill.

It was two hundred paces from the refinery school to the tin roofs, and it took him half the morning. Sefa walked on one side of him and the man in Meridian grey on the other. The dockers of the Waterline watched him pass. Some of them took their caps off. Some did not.

The tanks stood behind the sea-wall in rows. Each was fifty metres long, a trough of raw concrete filled to the waist with water piped up from the harbour. Electric lamps hung over them on wires, hard and white, and they did not flicker. That had been in the paper. *Lamps that do not fail.* The people of four holds sat in the water in lines, as they had been put. Each wore a brass tag on a cord at the wrist, stamped with a number.

Nobody had locked the sheds. There had been no need. There was nowhere to go.

Somebody had been at the sluices, all the same. The iron gates at the seaward end of every row hung open, sawn through at the hinge. A big grey-bearded man was sitting on the wall beside the last of them with a hacksaw across his knees. A woman sat against his shoulder, and two girls.

"Three days," Corin said. He did not get up. "The dockers lent me the saws. They'd heard the sirens, same as these did. Nobody came to open a door for any of them." He looked at the blade. "He'll have reckoned the cost of that. He'll have had a figure for it."

"I need them," Ilan said. "I've come to ask."

"Then ask them. Not me. I'm nobody's pilot."

The speaker of Kelder was an old man with a mild, defeated face. He listened, standing in the trough with the white light on his head. Ilan told him what was being done to the sea. He told him about the ring, and the city under it, and what he thought could be done. He said that he could do none of it himself. He was asking for every caller and every healer the four holds had, to go to the edge of dead water and risk the undertow, for a place that none of them had seen.

"We marked a paper," the old man said. "Twenty years. I marked it for all of us. Our lamps were out."

"I can't unmark it. I can tell you that the man who holds it is drying the sea you came from."

"And if we come out of these tanks, where do we go after? Kelder's dark."

"I don't know. I think I know. I can't promise it."

The old man considered him. "You're the first of them who's said so," he observed. He turned, and spoke to the nearest of his people, and they spoke to the next. Ilan watched it go down the rows. It was a Moot, he saw. It was a small one, held in a trough. It took two hours, and nobody raised a voice.

Then the callers of Kelder got up out of the orphan water. They went down through the open sluice into the harbour, which was clean, and could hear. He saw their faces as it touched them.

By evening the tanks were empty. Eleven thousand mariners hung in Port Halcyon harbour under the eyes of the Stacks. On the quay a docker's wife was handing bread rolls down over the edge, one at a time, to children who had never seen bread.

Okonta had watched it all from the wall. Corin came and stood in front of her. He had the hacksaw still in his hand, and seemed not to know it.

"I know where his twenty-two fly," he said. "I know their watches, and who's tired of it. I'd fly for you. If you'd have me."

"I'm not him," she said. "I can't make you anything."

"No."

She was silent. Then she said, "Say it, then."

He said it. He was fifty-eight years old, and his voice shook like a boy's. *Keep the water. Own nothing you can't carry. Come when you're called.*

"To whom?" Okonta said.

"To you. You're alive."

She nodded once. She had still not smiled. But she put her hand on his forearm, as an old man had gripped hers in the gallery of the Dragon, and held it there.

---

He could not call. He could ride.

The Thread was not the gift, and being ebbed did not touch it. He lay along the needle with Sefa behind him and thought, with some bitterness, that it was the one thing Jef had given him that he could not spoil. Okonta flew on one side, and Corin on the other. They ran west.

Saltgate stood on its black pillars with its neon lit, and it was quiet.

He had never seen the canals empty. The water in them stood as high as ever, and nothing moved under it. In the Wet Market the lowest tier of planking lay bare under a metre of sea, and so did the tier above it. The stallholders of the dry tier sat on the boards with their goods around them and nobody to sell to. The mariners had gone. The dead water was coming east, and none of them would wait to feel it arrive.

On the Sluice the noodle stall was open. The old man with the bucket looked at him twice, as he had before.

"You're Tomas Voss's boy," he said. "I said so to my wife, the last time. She said he was drowned." He ladled a bowl, and would not be paid. "Your mother used to come down for broth. She never took the noodles. I always wondered."

Ilan ate it standing on the towpath, under the fourth-floor window. The canal below him was black and still, and nothing swam in it.

The Lockkeepers kept their senior box at the north gate. He climbed to it by an iron ladder, slowly, stopping at every landing. The woman inside was seventy. She had a brass badge worn smooth, and a kettle, and a logbook the size of a paving stone.

"We don't take sides," she said, before he had his breath. "I'll tell you that at once, and save you the stairs next time. Four hundred years. Halcyon's asked, and Kessler's asked, and your mother's people have asked. We keep the gates."

"I'm not asking you to take a side. I'm asking you to read me a measurement."

She looked at him over her cup. Then she opened the book.

"Eight hundred and ninety kilometres from this gate to the wall, on the ninth of the month," she said. "Eight hundred and seventy yesterday. It comes on faster every week. There's four centuries of soundings in this book, and not one of them is a distance to the edge of the sea." She shut it. "My grandfather kept this box. He used to say that the only thing a Lockkeeper has to know is that the sea comes in and the sea goes out. He never said what we did if it went out and stayed."

"When it reaches you, there are no gates."

"I can count, young man." She drank her tea. "No sea, no gates. No gates, no Lockkeepers, and no Saltgate. That isn't a side. That's arithmetic." She reached up and took hold of a brass handle over her head, polished by generations of hands. "What is it you want carried, and where to?"

"People. To the edge of it."

She pulled. A horn sounded over the north gate, deep and long. Another answered from the east, and a third. He had lived in that city for sixteen years and had never heard them. Nobody had. They were for the day the gates were shut.

"Every gate barge we have," the old woman said. "And I'll have the figures read from every box on the hour, for anyone who'll listen. I don't say they'll believe a Lockkeeper. But we've never yet told them anything that wasn't so."

Garran's dockers loaded the barges that night, in the rain, by lantern.

---

He went north, because there was one hold he could not ask by messenger.

They received him in the high hall, under the vault of clear glass. He had been told what the hall did. He stood on the inlaid wave in the middle of the floor and spoke to seven elders, and knew that twelve thousand people were hearing him twice.

"I've come to tell you three things, and then to ask for one," he said. "The first is that two of your hearts can't be mended. Elder Vey has told you that. They want making, and nobody alive can make. The second is that there's dead water coming up from the south. Your choirs have heard the southern holds go quiet. It will be here before the year is out. The third is that I can do nothing about either. I'm ebbed. I can't call a cupful. And when I wasn't ebbed, I gave the sea an order, and killed ten kilometres of it. You know that too."

The canyon gave it back to him, from below, and from above.

"There's a city under the Girdle. I think it can be raised. I think that if it is, the thing that's killing the water stops, and there's room in it for every dark hold there is. I can't raise it. It would take every caller you have, and every healer, at the edge of deaf water, passing strength hand to hand through a man who has gone under once already. Some of them may not come back whole. I've nothing to offer for it. I'm only asking."

Elder Vey sat very straight. It was some time before she spoke.

"You ask," she said. "You have not told us what you will do for us."

"No."

"He never asked us for anything. The old man. Not in nine hundred years. He came, and mended, and complained of his knees, and went away." Her hands were folded in her lap. "I have wondered, since, whether that was why we let him carry it. It is very easy to be grateful. It asks nothing of one."

She did not put it to the elders. She lifted her head, and spoke to the vault.

"The hold has heard him," she said. "Let the choirs answer."

For a moment there was nothing. Then, far down in the canyon, a single choirmaster sang a phrase. It was four notes, rising. Another took it up across the gulf, and another above. It climbed the walls tier by tier, forty choirs one after the other, until the whole canyon was ringing with it, and the glass over his head hummed.

"That is yes," said Elder Vey. "You'll want Aurel. He has already packed."

At the sea-door on the rim a huge young man in sealskin was sitting on a bale.

"Rime's sent forty," Brask said. "And a sledge of cod. Southerners never bring enough to eat." He looked Ilan over, from the feet up, as he had at Greywatch Stone. "You're less than you were. You look better for it."

The Eyes had begun to drift.

Dace said so, when the needles found her. The calm places of the Belt had moved on a pattern since before there were charts. Now they were sliding, all of them, toward one point in the open Girdle, like crumbs toward a hole in a cloth. Lashing had gone with its Eye. It lay now on a flat grey sea, under a ring of sky, at the edge of the dead water.

He came to it on the twentieth day, and did not know the place.

There were boats to the horizon. Lashing's six thousand hulls were the middle of it. Round them lay the gate barges of Saltgate, and tugs out of Keelhaven, and the fishing smacks of the Stubborns. He saw the boats of Old Tolla, with the first fish of the catch nailed to their bows. There was a white Brightwater hospital ship. Her captain had turned her round at sea, he heard, on the day he learned how Dr Marsh had died. There were ferries, and pleasure yachts off the Rim, and a coal barge with four hundred people standing in it. Agravars stood at every rail.

And in the water were the mariners. Two hundred holds had sent everyone who could travel. Odile had the Moot there, with her lists. They hung in the warm cloudy sea in their thousands, long-limbed and dark-eyed, looking up. He saw the dark silk of Deep Chorus, and the sealskins of Rime. He saw the brass tags of Kelder, still on their cords. Somebody had told them they might take the tags off, and they had said that they would do it afterwards.

It was the first time in three thousand years that the two peoples had been within arm's reach, in such numbers, with nothing in anyone's hands. Nobody was speaking. Forty thousand people were looking at each other across a metre of air and water. They were finding out that the other was real.

To the west, where the sea should have gone on, there was a wall.

It was forty kilometres off, and it filled a quarter of the horizon. It was grey, and it did not move. It stood a little higher than the sea in front of it, like the edge of a table. Mist hung along its top. As he watched, the whole length of it shivered, and stepped toward him, and was still again.

He could not feel the water under the boat. Nor could anyone. The deaf sea began two kilometres to the west, at a line as sharp as a shoreline, and the callers of two hundred holds had drawn up along it like people at the lip of a cliff.

---

The rays came on the twenty-second day.

They had never before come into the Girdle. They rose round the rafts in a slow wheel, hundreds of them, pale wings turning under the grey water, and on every deck the agravars went silent. Some knelt. The old one with the notched wing came alongside Dace's boat. A tall woman in a patched coat stepped off its back and over the gunwale, as one steps off a ferry.

Her hair had begun to grow. She looked at Ilan, wrapped in his blanket in the stern. She looked for longer at the girl beside him.

"Show me your hands," Maelis said.

Sefa held them out. Maelis took them in her own and turned them over. She pressed the palms with her thumbs, as Jef had pressed Ilan's fingers under a lamp.

"They told me," she said. "They've had it since Fallow. They wouldn't let me come until I'd sat nine days with the water." She took a small clay bottle from her pocket, stopped with wax, and set it on the thwart between them. "I ask everyone the same question. I asked him. What will you do with the life you get back?"

Sefa looked at the bottle. Her mouth worked.

"I thought I knew," she said. "I had it all ready, in case you ever came. I was going to say I'd go and look at that tank with the lid off, and then find every other one like it." She drew a breath. "It's not that, though. It's that I thought it was mine. I thought I was good at it. I was *proud*. And then it went, and I was still there. I could still set a bone. I could still tell when somebody meant a thing." She looked up. "If I had it back, I'd know I was only where it went through. I think I'd be better at it. I don't know what I'd do. I'd just stop thinking it was me."

Maelis was silent for some time.

"He took thirty-eight years to find that out," she said, with a glance at Ilan. "And had to be drowned twice." She put the bottle into Sefa's hand, and closed the fingers on it. "Drink it in the sea. Not in a boat."

Sefa went over the side. Bother went after her. Ilan could not see what happened under the water. He saw the hound come up alone, and bark, once, at nothing.

Then she surfaced beside the boat. She was holding a double handful of sea up in front of her face, and she was laughing and crying together. It held its shape a little, as water should not. It was warm. He could see the warmth, like the air over a stove.

"That is the first thing I have enjoyed in half a year," Maelis said. She sat down on the thwart. "Now. They've told me something else. In fifty-five years they have never once told me what they want. I've asked. They thought it was funny." She looked out at the turning wings. "They're tired, Ilan. They have remembered everything, for everybody, since before the Makers. Every drowned child. Every war. All that the undertow ever took. Nobody has ever taken any of it off them. They've carried it by themselves." She folded her hands. "There are walls in that city which remember. They want them lit. They want, for once, to put something down."

"And you?"

"I'm coming in with you. I knew him when he was fourteen and couldn't speak. He taught me a game with stones." Her face was quite calm. "I should like him to see mine."

Hesper came last, on the evening before.

He was in the stern, watching Odile and forty choirmasters sort eleven thousand callers into rings. He had nothing to do with it. Odo of Lashing was doing the same for the boats, through his brass trumpet. Teodora Raske stood on the deck of a tug with her three hundred and eleven, checking the seals on one another's hard suits. Adler Crane was among them, in a borrowed helmet, holding a lamp for Hanne. They looked like people about to sit an examination.

He had been somebody that people could not do without. He was watching it stop. He found, to his surprise, that it was like setting down a sled rope.

"You've got thin," somebody said, in the water.

Hesper had her arms folded on the gunwale. She looked ten years older than at Low Anthem. The cord was gone from her wrist.

"I went back," she said. "To where we built it, off the Crown. And then I went to Fallow. I wanted to see it properly. I'd only seen that it worked." She was not looking at him. "There's nothing. No weed. No fish. I put my hand in, and it was like putting it into a dead mouth. I did one of those. I stood in front of two million people and told the sea to stand, and it never spoke again." Her jaw was tight. "Davin would have known. He'd not have needed to go and look."

"I needed to go and look. I never did. For fifteen years."

"I know. It's why I can stand to talk to you." She glanced along the boat. Sefa was asleep in the bows with her hand trailing in the water. Even from the stern he could see that the sea round her fingers was faintly gold. Hesper looked at that for a long moment. Something went out of her shoulders. "Then it wasn't the wrong one," she said. "It was only lent."

"You said you'd stop me yourself, if I did it again without learning."

"I did. I'll hold to it." She almost smiled. "You're not to try lifting that city, Wrack. I can see you thinking about it. You'd die of it, and it'd come up crooked. You showed three hundred of us a thing at Tidehall that had never been done, and then you went off to do everything by yourself, as usual. You can't. *We* can. You've only to be the place it goes through." She pushed off from the gunwale. "I've twelve who know how a great weight is built. And how it isn't. Odile's put us in the first ring."

Later, when the lanterns were lit, a big man came picking his way across the rafts with a child asleep on his shoulder.

Garran stood on the planking and looked west. The wall was a darker line against the dark. Now and then it shivered.

"Is that it?" he said. "The other field?"

"It's under that."

The porter studied it for a while, as he might have studied a crate too heavy for one.

"Then we'd best go and fetch it," he said.

## Chapter 17. Site Nine

The healers went first.

There were six thousand of them. They came out of two hundred holds, with grey bands on their arms, and they drew up along the edge of the dead water in a line two kilometres long. Sefa was among them. So was Ione. The sixteen Wellwalkers stood at intervals, like posts in a fence.

Nobody knew whether it could be done. Yeva said so, plainly, to those near enough to hear.

"Purging takes out what has been put in," she said. "There's nothing put into this. It's clean. It has been told not to listen, and it doesn't. I can't purge a thing of an order." She looked along the line. "There's a fourth form of intending. Most of you have never used it. Restoring. You've seen it grow a hand back at the Lantern. I've used it twice, on people who had forgotten who they were. I have never heard of it used on water."

"We have," said a voice.

It was one of the healers from the Hush. There were forty of them, in dark grey, and they had not spoken since they came. The Hush was where people were brought when nothing was broken and they still could not go on.

"It's what we do," the healer said. She was a small woman, and she kept her voice low from habit. "You don't push. You don't ask them for anything. You sit down in the dark beside them, with a hand on them, and you want nothing at all. Not even for them to get better. You stay. Sometimes it takes a year." She knelt at the edge, and put her hands into the dead sea to the wrist. "Sit down. All of you. We'll show you."

Six thousand healers sat down in the water.

For an hour nothing happened. The boats rocked. On the decks, the islanders watched a line of people doing nothing, and did not understand what they were looking at. Ilan lay in the stern of Dace's boat and understood it very well. He had stood waist deep in a canal for an hour once, with a cupful of water in his hands, wanting too much.

"There," Sefa said.

He could not feel it. He was shut out. But he saw the callers behind her lift their heads, all together, like hounds.

A hand's width of the dead water had begun to hear.

It was slow. By noon the line had gone forward the length of a boat. Then it went a little faster, and then faster again. The sea behind the healers leaned in over their shoulders, as the old water of the Well had once leaned in over three hundred pools, and helped. It went like a thaw. First there was a wet patch on the ice, and then a channel, and then the sound of running.

They did not need the whole ring. They needed a road.

The islanders gave it to them. It was Garran who saw how. A healer who had sat four hours with her hands in dead water could not then swim a kilometre and do it again. So the barges came up. The dockers of Saltgate lifted the healers of Kelder aboard, with their brass tags still on their wrists, and sat them along the gunwales with their arms over the side. Then they rowed. They rowed the line forward into the grey, a boat's length at a time, with a mariner trailing her hands from every thwart. When a healer flagged, a docker's wife put bread into her mouth.

It took two days and a night. On the second evening a road of living water a kilometre wide ran west from the rafts, straight as a rule, to the foot of the wall.

---

Rook's Threaded came on the first afternoon.

Ilan saw the grey needles lift out of the mist along the top of the wall, twenty-two of them, and drop toward the line of barges. He saw the silver ones go up to meet them. He could do nothing. He lay and watched Okonta fly.

It was not much of a fight. Corin saw to that. He went out ahead of the rest with his hands open, and he called to the grey riders by name, one after another, in a voice that carried across the water.

"Joss! Where did he put your mother? Did he ever tell you which shore?" And then: "Tam! It's the Waterline at Port Halcyon. Under the wave. I sawed the gates off myself. Go and ask anyone!"

Nine of them turned their needles before a blow was struck. Four more came over in the night. The rest went back behind the wall.

The soldiers were another matter. Meridian had forty ships at the rim, and two thousand men, and those men had been promised ground. A gunboat came down the road of living water on the second morning with her crew at stations. She stopped across the bows of the leading barge.

Odo of Lashing handed his brass trumpet to Garran.

"I've been promised it too, lads," the big man said. His voice went over the water like a crate being set down. "Forty paces by twenty. I can tell you whose it'll be. Come and look at what he's done to get it."

Marit Coyle had a camera on the gunboat from a smack alongside. The Lockkeepers' horns were sounding from every gate barge in the line. The gunboat's captain stood on his bridge for some time. Then the crest came down from her mast, and she swung in behind the barges, and rowed.

---

The wall was sixty metres high.

From the foot of it there was nothing to see but grey. It went up sheer out of the sea, as smooth as poured glass, and it made no sound. The callers of the first ring put their hands on it and took them away again. Hesper said that it was like touching the face of someone who had died angry.

"Over it," Okonta said. "It's the only way. Two to a needle."

He had seen her fly a needle through the air once, the length of a dock road at Keelhaven. He had thought then that it was a thing no needle should be able to do. Now thirty of them went up the face of the wall together, with an engineer in a hard suit clinging behind each rider. Teodora Raske rode behind Corin with her eyes shut. Hanne had her lamp tied to her wrist. Adler Crane held on to the girl with the harpoon. Maelis sat behind the old woman with the clay tube, as upright as in a chair.

Ilan rode alone. He could still do that.

They came over the top, and he saw what Anselm Rook had made.

It was a pit. It was eighty kilometres across, and the sea stood round it in a ring, like the rim of a bowl. The floor was mud. It lay steaming under the sun, grey and cracked, to the edge of sight. There were wrecks on it, keeled over. There were the bones of whales. There were fish beyond counting, lying where the water had left them, and the smell came up to him even at that height. Far off, absurdly, a drilling platform stood on six rusty legs in the middle of a plain.

Under the platform was the city.

It had come part of the way out of the silt, as the sea drew off it. He saw roofs, and the tops of towers, and the lines of streets, all plastered grey. Round the central hall lay a ring of great hoops, one behind another, with nothing between them. He did not know them at first. They were ribs. The bright water had gone out of them. The Dragon lay coiled on the mud like something a tide had left, and it made no sound at all.

"This is his field," Raske said, behind Corin.

They set down in a square. Ilan stepped off the needle onto the mud of a street he had last walked in a hard suit, with a lamp. He was barefoot, in a tunic of sea-silk, with a tool in his pocket. He had not a cupful of the sea to his name.

A lamp came on.

It was at the corner of the square, a pale column under a crust of silt. It came on as he passed it, white and steady. The next one lit before he reached it. They went on ahead of him down the street, one after another, as lamps are lit before a guest.

"It knows you," Hanne whispered.

"It knows a pair of hands," Ilan said. "It would know yours. Go and find it some seams."

The engineers went. They went by twos into the grey streets, with their lamps and their tools, to do what they had been taught on a barge deck at Lashing.

There were soldiers on the stair to the vault. There were a dozen, in grey, with a sergeant. They had been watching lamps come on down an empty street behind a barefoot man, and they did not look like men who wished to be asked for anything.

Crane stepped in front of Ilan and took off his helmet.

"Stand your people down, sergeant," he said, in his tidy voice. "I'll sign for it."

The sergeant looked at him. Crane had been their chief of security for eleven years, and had never once raised his voice to any of them. The sergeant stood aside.

---

The three doors were open.

Beyond them the vault went down into the rock like the inside of a bell. In its floor was the ring. It was forty metres across, a single hoop of the pale metal, set flush with the stone. Water ran round it in a sheet. It was not the bright water of the Dragon's ribs. It was grey, and it ran in jerks.

At the centre, bolted into a frame of raw agravar steel, two wheels were turning, one inside the other. Each was as wide across as a table.

Anselm Rook was sitting at a bench beside them, in his shirt. His coat lay over the back of the chair. His collar was open, and the scars showed on his neck. There were drawings all round him, hundreds of sheets, pinned and weighted. He looked ninety. His hands were wrapped in rags, and the rags were stained.

"You came the long way," he said. He did not seem surprised. "I heard horns."

"I asked."

"Yes. I thought that might be it." His eyes went past Ilan, and stopped. "Maelis."

"Hello, Anselm."

"You've cut your hair."

"He died."

"I know. I—" He did not finish it. She had come down the last step and was standing with her hands in her pockets, looking at him. She did not look angry. She looked as she had looked across a bowl of soup, taking stock.

"You always did move first," she said. "You never could wait for the other person's stone."

He turned his face away from her. After a moment he held out his bandaged hands to Ilan, with a small object in them.

"Look at this," he said. "I should like you to see it."

It was a panel, no longer than a finger, from the housing of the outer wheel. It had been opened along a seam.

"Tuesday," Rook said. "Forty-nine years, and it came on a Tuesday. I had the pictures in front of me, as he did. I'd tried it nine thousand times." Something that was nearly a smile crossed the square, exhausted face. "He said I had hands like a pair of feet. He was quite right. It was the wrong seam, of course. There's nothing behind it."

Ilan took the panel. He turned it over. It had been done clumsily, and by force, and it had been done.

"It's seated wrong," he said. "All of it."

"It runs."

"It runs like a man shouting. Every time the wall jumps, a little more of it goes soft. Your own engineers told you so. Crane said."

"Crane." Rook glanced at him without interest. "Yes. They told me. I had a figure for it. I judged that I should have it steady before it mattered." He nodded toward the roof, and the eighty kilometres of mud above it. "Listen to that, Ilan. Nothing. That's ground. It's the first new ground on Ovara in three thousand years. I've read forty thousand names onto it already."

"I've come to seat it."

That reached him. The dark eyes sharpened.

"For me?"

"No. Right." Ilan went to the edge of the ring and knelt, and put his palm flat on the metal. He could feel it, as he had felt the Dragon's brow under his hand at Greywatch Stone. It was labouring. "It isn't a wall. It never was. They built it when the sea began to come up. They didn't mean to keep the sea out. They meant to lift the city with it. A little at a time, so that the top was always dry and the bottom always wet, however high the water went. Wet halls and dry halls. It was built for people who did both."

"That gives me one city."

"It gives everybody one city. Two million rooms, and no landlord."

"And the other thirty-eight million?"

"The Dragon ran between cities," Ilan said. "Hundreds of them. Jef only ever looked for this one. The rays remember where the others lie. They've been waiting three thousand years for somebody to ask."

Rook was silent. Then he shook his head, slowly.

"It isn't ground," he said.

"No. It's better. It's somewhere to go."

"You can't promise it."

"No."

"He never could either." Rook reached into his shirt pocket and brought out something no larger than a plum stone. He held it between finger and thumb, and looked at it with his head a little on one side. Round the ring the grey water leapt. Ilan felt the vault shudder. Far overhead, and forty kilometres off, sixty metres of sea stepped forward. "I can run it faster. I'd rather not. One more step, and it's over your barges."

Nobody moved.

"I can't stop you," Ilan said. "I've nothing. I can't call a cupful, and I'll not order. I've come with a tool and thirty people in borrowed suits." He stayed on his knees. "You opened a seam on Tuesday. Don't you want to know what's behind the right one? I can show you. He'd have shown you. He didn't know how to teach it. I do. I've taught three hundred. I've the man who drowned me in my third row." He took Jef's tool from his pocket and held it out across the ring, handle first.

Rook looked at it for a long time.

"He offered me that," he said. "On the step, when I was twenty-three. I told him I would rather have the thing it opened."

He did not put out his hand.

It was the wall behind him that answered. Ilan had passed a lamp at the foot of the stair, and the stair had lit. Now the stone of the vault paled, and brightened, and turned to light from floor to roof. Rook saw it on the faces in front of him before he turned.

The pictures moved. They showed the ring, new, and the city standing round it under a clean sea. They showed the hoop turning, bright. A street rose out of the water as gently as a breath, with children sitting on the steps. They showed it done a hundred times, over a hundred years, as the sea came up.

Then they showed a man. He had long limbs and broad hands, and a square, clever, frightened face. He stood where Rook was sitting. He was shouting at the others in the vault. He did something to the ring, quickly, by force. The sea stood back from the city. It went grey. The pictures showed the wall, and the pit, and the fish.

They showed it fall.

They showed what was left afterwards, and how long nothing lived there. They showed the three doors being shut. And they showed the people who had shut them, parting on a shore. Some went down into the water. Some climbed away. They did not look at one another.

"They lost their nerve," Rook said. His voice had no breath in it.

"They saw what it left."

The old man sat looking at the wall. He had been listened to all his life. Nobody in the vault said anything to him now.

"The figure," he said at last. "If it comes down. What did Crane tell you?"

"Every island on the Girdle."

"Yes. That was mine as well." He looked at the plum stone in his fingers, as a man looks at a column that will not add. Then he set it down on the bench, among the drawings, quite gently. "Seat it."

"Take the tool. I'll show you where."

"No." He pushed himself up, with a hand on the chair, and went and sat down on the stone at the rim of the ring. He moved like a very old man. "I am too old to be anybody's third row. I should like to watch. I've only ever seen it done wrong."

It took eleven hours. It was the largest thing Ilan ever mended, and he did it with his hands.

Hanne held the lamp. Raske called the sections, in the voice she used across a design loft. Crane fetched and carried. Ilan cut the wheels out of their steel frame and seated them where the Makers had meant a heart to sit. He turned one of them end for end, and it ran cooler. Under the ring there were channels as fine as hairs, and they were silted, and he cleared them one at a time.

When he closed the last seam, the grey water dropped into its channel. For a moment the ring was dry. Then the water came again, and it was bright.

Above them, forty kilometres away on every side, the wall let go.

It did not fall. He had been sure, and not sure. It sank. It came down a hand's width at a time, as the ring bade it, and the sea it had held back walked in across the mud at the pace of a tide. The healers met it as it came. They were ready now, and there were six thousand of them. They sat in the shallows with their hands in it, and it came in hearing.

"It'll be here by morning," Ilan said. "This vault fills first. Anselm, put your helmet on."

Rook did not move. He was watching the bright water run.

"I can't breathe your sea, Ilan," he said. "I never could. I tried, when I was a boy. She held my head under."

"You swallowed half the Well," Maelis said.

"I did." The square face eased. "I think I shall stay. Somebody ought to sit with it. And I find that I don't care to go up and be forgiven by a porter."

"Anselm—"

"Go on. They'll want you in the water. It's the only thing I taught you that I'm not ashamed of. Knowing where you're wanted."

Maelis had taken off her coat. She folded it, and laid it on the stone, and sat down beside him at the edge of the ring. She put out her hand. After a moment he gave her his, rags and all.

"I'll stop," she said to Ilan, without turning her head. "It's what the Hush is for. Go up."

He went up the stair, and the doors were open behind him. The last he saw of them was two old people sitting side by side in the light of a wall, like children on a dock.

---

The sea reached the city at dawn.

He was standing in the central square when it came. It ran in along the streets ankle deep, and then to the knee. It was warm, and it was clean. He could not hear it. He was ebbed to the bone, and Yeva's hand was on the back of his neck, and Sefa's arm was round him. He was not to ask for anything. He was the empty place and the way through, and that was all.

He never afterwards managed to tell anyone properly what it was like.

The callers of the Moot came first, three hundred of them, as before. He knew them now. He felt Aurel's held chord, and the boy from Rime. Then every Touched child and net-mender from two hundred holds came after them, with their one small room's worth of sea apiece. There were thousands of those. Each gave a thimbleful, and it went through him like rain. The callers of Kelder gave, with their brass tags on. Hesper's twelve came in last, hard and bright and exact. They knew how a great weight is built.

Something colder and older came up under all of it.

The rays did not lend strength. They lent memory. They showed him the sea floor as it had lain before the Sinking. He saw every street and every foundation. He saw where the rock was sound and where it was not. He had been holding a guess. Now he had a drawing.

He gave it all to the ring, and the ring knew what to do with it.

In three hundred places, in the dark streets, the engineers had found their seams. A lamp came on under the silt, and then a street of them. Air-hearts that had been still for three thousand years began to open and close. The city drew a breath. It shook off its mud, as a sleeper shrugs off a blanket, and it began to help.

It rose.

They saw it from the boats. The sea had come back over the pit, flat and pale. Now it paled further, from below. Then it boiled with light. Then the towers came through the surface, streaming, into the morning.

They were white. They were taller than the Spires. They were fluted and slender, and bridges sprang between them, and every window in them was lit. Every person on every deck cried out together. The city went on rising. Tier came after tier, and street after street. There were squares, and stairs, and long arcades. There were domes of the pale glass, and gardens that had been silt for three thousand years. Water poured out of every door and down every stair in white cataracts, and ran away.

It stopped when half of it stood dry in the air. The other half lay in clear deep water. The streets ran down into canals, and the canals ran out to the open sea. A person could walk from the high towers down a broad white stair and, without a break, go on walking under water.

The old platform stood up on its six legs in the middle of a square, like a toy somebody had left behind.

Nobody went under. The Wellwalkers had gone among the callers the whole time. So had Ione, and so had Sefa, with a hand here and a word there: *enough, that's enough from you, sit down.* Not one of them gave more than they had.

As the city rose, every wall in it turned to light. Marit Coyle put what the walls were showing onto every screen on Ovara.

It was faces. The Makers had been ordinary people, with children. They had long limbs and large dark eyes. They had broad hands and weathered skin. Often both were in the same face. They were one people. The walls showed the city as it had been, with markets in it, and schools. They showed the sea rising, slowly, over lifetimes. They showed a quarrel in a vault, and what came of it. And they showed both peoples forgetting, a little more in every generation, until one of them was a fairy tale and the other was a plague.

Round the rafts the rays had stopped turning. They hung in the clear water with their wings spread, quite still, as they had hung once before a window at the top of the world.

On the deck of a Keelhaven tug a welder looked at the screen in the wheelhouse. Then he looked at his own hands. Then he looked over the side at a net-mender from Pearl Stair, who was looking at hers.

He reached down. She reached up.

It happened all along the rails after that. Ilan saw Brask hauled onto a coal barge by six stokers, and heard him laugh. He saw the old woman who kept the shrine at Old Tolla lean out of a smack and touch a mariner child's face. And he saw Garran lift Ione of Low Anthem bodily up onto a gate barge. The big man stood there holding her scarred arms in his two hands, and saying something. Ione was nodding.

Maelis came up the vault stair at noon, when the water in it was still. She was alone. She had her coat over her arm.

She did not say anything about it, then or later. But she stood for a while in the square, looking up at the lit walls. And she said that the third tower was out of true.

## Chapter 18. Meet

Meridian Assurance did not so much fall as cease to matter.

It held every debt in the world. Every one of them was priced, in the end, on a single fact. Land was scarce, and a person who left the island they owed on had nowhere else to stand. It had called them all in at once, and promised to pay for the service in ground. The ground was under sixty metres of sea again. Within the thirty days, something like three million people simply left. They walked onto boats with what they could carry. They came to a city that had no owner and no charter. The streets were empty, and the lamps were on.

Its chairman was not seen again. The house put out no statement. Nobody on the islands had ever been shown his face, and so nobody could quite say what was missing.

Meridian's mercenaries were owed back pay. They looked at the screens, and at the sea, and they recalled a certain evening at Port Halcyon. A good many of them went too. The captain from Studio One was among the first. He brought his daughter.

The building with no windows was still standing a year later. Some people said the clerks were still inside, adding it all up. In the flooded dock beneath it they found a cradle of an old pattern, with nothing in it.

Halcyon's board went before a court of both peoples, the first there had ever been. It sat in Tidehall, and Odile kept the lists. Adler Crane walked into it between two fishwives, and told it everything he knew. Caspar Venn sent a statement, which was beautifully worded. Dr Marsh's successor opened the wards of forty hospitals, and said that it had always been the late chief's intention. Ilan did not attend. He had eleven domes to mend, and he had not been let off them.

---

The ring ran down when the city stood, and was still. It had been made to rest between one lifting and the next. In the first week Ilan took the two wheels out of it again, and carried them up to the square where the Dragon lay. Twenty-nine Threaded held lamps for him, and a thirtieth, who had said the rule late. The old woman with the clay tube told him that he need not think of thanking anybody.

It sang at the second asking. The bright water came back into its ribs between one breath and the next.

---

He went to Low Anthem first.

He went on the Dragon, on its old course, to Greywatch Stone. He read the names on the benches again. He had twenty people with him. Ten were engineers of Keelhaven, with Hanne at their head. Ten were mariners from Deep Chorus, who had sat beside them at Lashing for a month. Sefa came, and Ione, and Bother.

The forest was humming. He stood in it for a while before he went on.

It took them forty days. The crack in the dome ran from the crown to the sea floor. Hanne looked at it for a morning and said that she would not know how to begin. A boy from Deep Chorus said that it looked to him like a slipped seam, only very large, and he was right. They closed it between them, a hand's width a day. Ilan held the sea back from the flooded third while they worked. He asked for no more than he needed. When the air-heart of Low Anthem opened and closed again, and the water went out of the meeting hall, the lamp at the centre of it was still burning. There was a carved stick lying beside it. Nobody had moved it.

He lit the ten dead lamps on the dark side one by one, with Jef's tool. It took him a week. The people of the hold took down the lampweed, strand by strand. They did not throw it away. They planted it along the terrace wall, where it would be seen.

On the last evening the whole hold went out onto the terraces. The hundred and ten who had come home from Deep Chorus were among them. They sang the tending song, the slow one, for work that is never finished.

He did not know it. The undertow had taken it at Deep Chorus, and had not given it back. He had never asked the rays for it. It had seemed a small thing to trouble them with, beside what they carried. He stood at the edge of the terrace with his mouth shut.

A lean woman came and stood beside him. She had been at the foot of a kelp stem all that afternoon, where a name was cut that had risen a hand's width since he saw it last.

"You don't know the words," Hesper said.

"I did once. I've lost them."

"I haven't sung them since he died." She was looking at the dome. It was lit all the way round, for the first time since before her grandmother was born. "He'd be unbearable about this. He'd say he told me so. He never told me any such thing. He just went on mending nets." She breathed in. "It starts low. Listen."

She sang it to him, a line at a time. Her voice was rusty. He sang it back and got the words wrong, and somebody on the terrace laughed. It was not unkind.

---

They called the city Meet. Nobody decided it. One day it was simply what people said.

Maelis came in the spring, on the Dragon. Her hair had grown to her collar. She walked the wet streets for a morning with her hands behind her back, looking at everything. He walked beside her. He found that he was more nervous than he had been at Tidehall.

There was a great deal to look at. Three million people had come to a city built for two, and had fitted themselves in. Islanders had taken the high towers, and at once begun to hang washing from them. Mariners from the dark holds had taken the water quarters. They had planted kelp in the canals. The streets between, which were ankle deep at high tide and dry at low, had turned into a market without anybody's leave. It was like the Wet Market at Saltgate, and a hundred times the size. There was a noodle stall from the Sluice. Next to it, a woman from Pearl Stair was selling sea-silk to a line of welders' wives. The air smelled of frying oil and of salt.

In a square by the water, agravar children were being taught to dive by mariner children, who found the whole business hilarious. Under an arcade, Teodora Raske was shouting at an apprentice from Deep Chorus about the seating of a coupling. Sefa was at a table by the canal with two basins and a queue. She wore a Wellwalker's grey now. Bother was asleep under her chair.

Maelis stopped, and looked up.

"The third tower is still out of true," she said.

"I know. I've been busy."

"He'd not have left it a season. He'd have said so before he said good morning." She was silent a while. "He'd have liked the lamps. He'd have said the workmanship was shocking, and he'd have gone round touching them all." She walked on a few paces. "Anselm asked me, down there, whether he would have liked it. The ring, running as it ought. I told him that he'd have found fault with the bench." She looked at him sidelong. "They'll make a god of you. You know that. They did it to him. He said it was dreadful. People bring you fish."

"They've started. I gave it to the noodle stall."

"What else are you doing about it?"

"There are four hundred people in this city who can open a seam. There'll be four thousand in a year. I teach a class every morning, and I've made it a rule that anybody who passes has to teach one." He hesitated. "He told me to teach people I didn't like. I've got Adler Crane in the third row. The court gave him to me. He's quite good."

Maelis looked at him for a long moment. It was the look she had given him over a bowl of soup, the first night.

"Yes," she said. "That's him all over. That's exactly the kind of thing he'd have found funny." She glanced at the table by the canal. She looked at the girl who had hit him across the face in a heart-chamber. "And you've somebody to tell you when you're being an idiot. In a sisterly way. Good. Keep her."

"I found something," Ilan said. "I wanted you to see."

It was in a hall under the eastern quarter that the silt had sealed. He took her down by a stair that nobody else used.

It was a workshop. It was ten times the size of the one at Kettle House, and it was just as untidy. The benches were of a pale stone he had never seen. There were racks of tools. He had one of those tools in his pocket; he had carried it round the world. In the middle of the floor was an air-heart, half built. Its maker had laid down the work one evening three thousand years ago, with every part set out in order beside it, and had never come back.

And the walls were alight.

He laid his palm on the nearest. Pictures moved under it. They showed a pair of hands, long-fingered and broad, doing a thing with the pale metal that he had tried and failed to imagine at a bench in the Pale. They did it slowly. They did it again when he asked.

"Archive walls," he said. "They still read. They show how. Step by step. I've been coming down at night." He touched the half-built heart. "He said tend, mend, make, and that making was the long one. He was right. It'll take me years. But I finished the housing last week, and it's true. I'm bringing the morning class down here as soon as they're ready."

Maelis had gone to the wall. She stood in front of it with the light on her face. She was watching the hands.

"He sat in front of these for eleven years," she said. "When he was a boy. He used to tell me about it when he couldn't sleep. He said he'd give the rest of his life to see one lit again." She laid her own hand on the glass, beside the picture. She stood so for a time.

When she turned round her face was composed.

"I asked you a question once," she said. "I ask everyone. Most of them answer it and go away, and I never learn if they meant it." She took her hand from the wall. "What will you do with the life you got back?"

He looked up through the roof of the hall, in his mind. He looked through the streets, and out across the whole breathing sea of Ovara. A hundred holds still had their lamps dark. The Dragon needed new bearings. There were ten domes still cracked, on ten routes, with his name on every one. The Slick lay in the north, and there were eleven others like it, full of poison he had put there himself. The eels in them were still spreading. South of Fallow there were ten kilometres of silence that he had made in one breath. Forty healers of the Hush sat there now, in turns, with their hands in it. They had brought back the width of a terrace. They said that it would take a hundred years, and that they had the time. And the rays had shown him where four hundred other cities lay, and Garran had asked him, only that morning, which one they were to fetch next.

He took the tool out of his pocket, and rolled up his sleeves.

"This," he said.
